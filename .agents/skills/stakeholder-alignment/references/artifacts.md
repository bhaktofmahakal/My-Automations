# Stakeholder Alignment - Frameworks, Templates & Checklists

*90 artifacts extracted from Lenny's Podcast and Newsletter*

## Frameworks

### Communication Is the Job (Leadership Accountability Framework) (Essential reading for product builders—part 2)
Bosworth's principle that communication failures are always the communicator's fault, and leaders must take full responsibility for how their message lands.

How it works: When communication fails:
1. Symptom: It feels like you're 'pushing with a rope'—you have a clear vision but people aren't doing what you expect.
2. Wrong response: Blame the audience for not understanding.
3. Right response: Take full responsibility. Sit down and ask questions from a place of humility to hear what they actually took away.
4. Then: Take full responsibility for any discrepancy from what you intended and make corrections with your entire audience.
5. Principle: When this happens, it is YOUR fault. Communication is the job itself, not a side task.

### Consulting vs. Collaboration vs. Consensus Decision-Making Model (Autonomy vs. direction - Issue 35)
A framework distinguishing three modes of group decision-making, originally based on Crucial Conversations, to help managers set the right expectations for how decisions get made

How it works: Three modes:
- Consulting: Everyone shares their perspectives (input-gathering, not binding)
- Collaboration: Everyone works together toward a solution
- Consensus: Everyone must agree before moving forward

The diagram from Crucial Conversations shows four decision-making methods on a spectrum:
1. Command — Leader decides, no discussion
2. Consult — Leader gathers input, then decides
3. Vote — Majority rules
4. Consensus — Everyone must agree

Lenny's recommendation: Collaboration and consulting are vital. Consensus is an anti-pattern. Great products (Apple, Tesla, Amazon) come from the least consensus-driven cultures. Ask: Which framework does your team THINK they are using? Is that the BEST framework for your team?

### Cross-Group Alignment Process (How Gong builds product)
A quarterly process for resolving cross-group overlap, platform-wide needs, and capability sharing across product groups

How it works: Trigger: Groups observe overlap with other groups, or identify knowledge/skill gaps.
Process: Quarterly meeting of all product group leaders.
Each group leader highlights:
1. Areas requiring platform-wide support
2. Areas applicable to additional teams
Output: Concrete plan determining which group leads each capability and when other groups get involved.
Escalation: Decisions that can't be resolved at group level bubble up to CPO or head of UX.
Examples of conflicts: One group building general-purpose analytics that another group needs on a different timeline. One group building AI capabilities that need to appear across the system while other groups have tight plans.
Approach: No fixed recipe — reviewed as a team ahead of each quarterly plan to ensure alignment.

### Evidence Hierarchy for Influencing Decisions (How to get better at influence)
A prioritized list of the most powerful types of evidence to bring when trying to convince stakeholders

How it works: Most powerful evidence to bring, in priority order:
1. Hard data
2. Synthesized user research findings
3. One-off anecdotes
4. Competitive analysis
5. Similar examples of this working elsewhere

Key tactic: Ask the other person 'What evidence would convince you of this being the right decision?'

Key insight: Often we think someone disagrees with us because of a personal issue, when in reality you just don't have a convincing case.

### Failure as Most Likely Outcome framing (Tanguy Crusson)
A deliberate positioning strategy for internal bets where you constantly remind the organization that the product will probably fail, which creates scarcity and autonomy

How it works: Key principles: 1) Remind everyone there's ~70% chance this won't exist in 6 months. 2) This prevents over-investment from other teams wanting to help (help comes with conditions that slow you down). 3) Creates permission to hack, not follow design guidelines, not fit target architecture. 4) Allows team to move at startup speed. 5) Respects company's investment by not dragging 50 people into an unvalidated bet. Use it when: other teams want to change services for you, platform teams want architectural alignment, process teams want OKRs/roadmaps.

### Fight Club Meeting (Megan Cook)
A dedicated weekly meeting for cross-functional leaders to surface and resolve conflicts.

How it works: A 30-minute weekly block. Attendees: Product, Engineering, and Design leaders. Rule: Everyone knows they are going in specifically to have a conflict and solve hard problems, removing the anxiety of bringing up disagreements.

### Five Paths for Saying No (Response Spectrum) (Saying no)
Five scripted response templates sorted by how good you think the idea is, from 'yes with tradeoffs' to 'hard no'

How it works: Path 1 (best idea): 'Yes, but here's what'll need to change. Should we move forward?' — Use when you believe the idea should happen immediately OR when you want to communicate how painful the change will be. Lay out: existing priorities that get pushed, launch dates changing, resources moving, risks introduced, impact on dependencies.

Path 2 (good, not now): 'Yes, but not right now because we should stay focused on X because of XYZ. Do you agree?' — Use when you like the idea but don't want to disrupt in-flight work. Bring up the roadmap, planning memos, broader strategy, pain of change, urgency assessment.

Path 3 (redirect): 'No, but how about we do XYZ instead, which achieves a similar outcome?' — Use when the manager found a real opportunity but not the optimal solution. Look for a simpler or more impactful alternative.

Path 4 (explore): 'No, but there's something there. Let's explore it further. Here's what I suggest as a next step…' — Use sparingly. Communicate a clear next step and timeline. Explore via: discussing with smart colleagues, wireframes with users, prototyping, writing a 1-Pager, running a quick A/B test.

Path 5 (hard no): 'No, because it's a bad idea for reasons XYZ. Do you agree?' — Use when the idea is just plain bad. Reasons: low ROI, strategically misaligned, too high-risk, bad product experience, high cost.

### Five Tactics of PM Influence (A PM’s guide to influence)
A comprehensive framework of five tactics PMs can use to drive alignment on complex initiatives

How it works: Tactic 1: Seek intel on how each stakeholder makes decisions — Understand what they value (goals, incentives), who they consult, and what they're afraid of through 1:1s and observation.

Tactic 2: Frame your message from their POV (not yours) — Reframe proposals to demonstrate how they help stakeholders reach their goals. Speak their language.

Tactic 3: Prime detractors and champions alike in the 'meeting before the meeting' — Meet with detractors to understand concerns and steelman their arguments. Meet with champions to confirm support and ask them to chime in during the meeting or pre-brief decision makers.

Tactic 4: Make people feel heard and validated — Play back statements in your own words, use progressive alignment checkpoints during meetings, and acknowledge feedback explicitly.

Tactic 5: Manage the clock — State desired outcomes upfront, redirect tangents, adapt format to audience, and ensure decisions happen within the allotted time.

Practice approach: Start with low-stakes situations before applying to high-stakes meetings.

### Four-Touchpoint Editorial Feedback System (Upasna Gautam)
A structured system of four recurring events to manage stakeholder intake and feedback from hard-to-schedule users: (1) Weekly Demo Days, (2) Working Sessions, (3) Breaking News Dress Rehearsals, and (4) Office Hours

How it works: 1. Weekly Demo Days: Open forum facilitated by PM, design lead, and tech lead to deep dive into existing features, preview upcoming features, recreate workflows, and evangelize the product across the business. 2. Working Sessions (renamed from 'user testing sessions' to be more collaborative): Breakout groups of journalists/editors recreate their specific workflows in the new platform. 3-6 sessions per team, 2 hours each, customized per team (politics vs entertainment vs health vs homepage). Critical part of onboarding process. 3. Breaking News Dress Rehearsals: Scripted simulations of breaking news scenarios to stress test the platform at breaking news speed. Engineers and support team observe while PM and editorial stakeholder facilitate. 4. Office Hours: Open blocks 1-2x per week where PM, design partner, and tech lead are available for questions, troubleshooting, and feedback.

### Hell Yes / Whole Body Yes (Kenneth Berger)
A standard for consent and agreement to avoid lukewarm commitments that fail later.

How it works: Treat anything short of a 'hell yes' as a 'no'. If you get a lukewarm response, ask: 'What would it take to get to a hell yes?'

### Identity Threat (Boz)
A psychological framework explaining why people react aggressively during disagreements.

How it works: When a core part of how you see yourself (e.g., 'being right') is questioned, you react defensively because reconceptualizing yourself is expensive. Counteract this by using the phrase 'Fascinating. You have to tell me more about why you think that.'

### Influence = Evidence + Trust (What jury duty taught me about product management)
A two-factor model for understanding and improving your ability to influence stakeholders, derived from jury decision-making instructions

How it works: Your ability to influence others boils down to two things:
1. How convincing your evidence is
2. How much people trust you, your evidence, and others on your side

What counts as evidence (from the judge's instructions, adapted for PM):
- Hard evidence (data, metrics, user research findings)
- Expert testimony (subject matter experts, industry benchmarks)
- Firsthand accounts from trusted witnesses (customer interviews, team observations)

What does NOT count / what to avoid relying on:
- What you told people happened (your narrative alone)
- Speculation or hearsay
- Sympathy or emotion
- Perceived authority bias (what you think the boss thinks)

If you're having a hard time influencing someone, instead of assuming they have something against you, double down on evidence and trust.

### Internal Virality Alignment (Gaurav Misra)
A bottom-up method for securing organizational alignment and executive buy-in for new product directions using internal prototype distribution.

How it works: Instead of writing docs and holding stakeholder meetings: 1. Have a small 'design engineering' team build a highly functional prototype. 2. Share the build informally with a few internal employees. 3. Let the prototype spread via internal word-of-mouth. 4. Wait for executives to hear about it organically and demand it be put on the official roadmap.

### MEOWs (Mod Experience Oriented Wins) (What 5 years at Reddit taught us about building for a highly opinionated user base)
A strategy of sequencing small, trust-building wins for a key user group before launching controversial features that affect that group.

How it works: MEOW = Mod Experience Oriented Win (actual Reddit internal acronym)

Strategy: If a product threatened to increase the day-to-day effort of moderators (e.g., introducing livestreaming as a new content type) AND the trust score for moderators had been trending downward, Reddit would:
1. Adjust the launch schedule
2. Potentially delay the controversial launch
3. Ship a few MEOWs first — small improvements that directly benefit moderators
4. These MEOWs would boost moderator trust
5. Then launch the more controversial feature when the Trust Vault was sufficiently filled

Principle: Sequence trust deposits before trust withdrawals.

### Magic Questions (Hilary Gridley)
A communication tactic to extract mental models from stakeholders.

How it works: Instead of asking open-ended questions (e.g., 'What are the rules?'), make a statement or propose a specific scenario and append 'Do you agree?' or 'Is that right?' to force a calibration response.

### Mindful Communication for PMs (Upasna Gautam)
A communication approach centered on deep listening, speaking in the listener's language, and pausing before reacting

How it works: Principles: 1. Be deeply aware of the conversation you're having—be fully present with only that person. 2. Listen before speaking—shut your mouth and let stakeholders vent, then extract the root cause. 3. Conversate in the language of the listener—use their vocabulary, their nicknames for features, their terminology. 4. Pause before reacting—this is the key to mindful communication. 5. You don't need all the answers—knowing where to go to get the answers is sufficient. 6. Remember you are a proxy, not the actual user, even if you use the product yourself.

### People Don't Care What You Know Until They Know That You Care (Shaun Clowes)
A life motto about influence: trust and relationships must come before technical correctness or data. Influence starts with caring about others' outcomes and incentives.

How it works: Principles: 1) Being right doesn't matter if people don't trust you, 2) First build trust and relationships, 3) Understand others' outcomes and incentives, 4) All good things (influence, partnerships, progress) sit on top of that foundation. Especially important for former engineers transitioning to product who over-index on technical correctness.

### Platform Principle Stack Ranking (Brandon Chu)
A decision-making tool for resolving conflicts between different stakeholders in a platform ecosystem.

How it works: Establish a clear hierarchy of constituents before building. Example: Amazon prioritizes the Consumer over the Seller. Shopify prioritizes the Merchant over the Developer. Use this stack rank to resolve data policy and UI design disputes.

### Prioritize and Communicate (Wes Kao)
A two-by-two framework for handling new requests: both prioritize the request among your existing priorities AND communicate where it sits to the requester

How it works: Four quadrants: 1) Prioritize + Communicate (ideal — slot it in your priority list and tell the requester where it sits), 2) Prioritize only (you adjust but don't tell them — risky), 3) Communicate only (you say you can't but don't reprioritize — feels like just saying no), 4) Neither (worst). Example: 'This is going to be third on my priority list. Does this seem reasonable? Should I do this sooner?'

### Product Decision-Making Roles (How Shopify builds product)
Clear delineation of who makes what kind of decision in product reviews

How it works: Three decision rights in product development: 1. Product (PM) makes the call on: 'Should we do this at all?' - The go/no-go on whether a project is worth pursuing. 2. Engineering and UX have veto power on: 'How we do it' - The implementation approach, technical architecture, and design quality. 3. PM puts their body on the line for: 'Is this ready to ship?' - If PM says yes and the thing's garbage, the PM has to wear it. Personal accountability for ship quality.

### Seven Strategies for Influence (The Frodo Framework) (How to get better at influence)
A comprehensive framework of seven strategies PMs can use to lead through influence rather than authority, each illustrated with Lord of the Rings examples and real-world Airbnb cases

How it works: 1. Make their goals your goals — Frame asks around the other person's goals and incentives, not yours
2. Charge your trust battery — Build trust over time through reliability, listening, helping, and reciprocity
3. Help them see what you see — Bring people on your thought journey; slow is fast with people
4. Show success — Build a track record of hitting goals and shipping successful products
5. Bring evidence — Use hard data, user research, and analogous examples to make your case
6. Leverage authority — Gain support from influential people to boost credibility (use sparingly)
7. Be likable — Practice specific likability behaviors that make influence easier

### Silver Burrito metaphor for alignment (Setting goals - Issue 23)
A metaphor to diagnose and communicate the alignment trap — when team members each imagine different goals inside the same project.

How it works: The metaphor: A silver-wrapped burrito (like on a Chipotle billboard) looks delicious to everyone because each person pictures their own favorite burrito inside. Similarly, projects often look exciting to all team members because each person is imagining a different goal they're personally most excited about — e.g., one person thinks the goal is 'increase conversion,' another thinks it's 'make users happier,' another thinks it's 'refactoring code.'

**The trap**: Assuming everyone is aligned on what success looks like.
**The leader's job**: Unwrap the burrito — make the goal explicit so everyone sees the same thing.

**How to use this**: Before kicking off a project, ask each team member what they think the goal is. If you get different answers, you have a silver burrito problem. Align on a single, explicit goal before proceeding.

### Spark to Wildfire Hype Strategy (How to ship like a startup)
A four-stage progression for building internal and external momentum for a new product idea

How it works: Stage 1 — Light the first spark with hackathons: Build a working prototype at company hackathon. Make the demo big, funny, and relatable.
Stage 2 — Share raw progress async: Post prototype videos regularly in Slack (one engineer posted 70+ before launch). Don't wait for polish. A steady drip of updates builds trust and keeps excitement high.
Stage 3 — Turn your spark into a wildfire at the all-hands: Advocate to use your MVP in a real-world scenario like a company all-hands meeting. Pause feature development to refine for this moment. Address bugs in real-time. A flawless presentation in front of the whole company seals the deal.
Stage 4 (Bonus) — Share beyond your company: Launch in open beta to gather feedback. Share WIP vision on platforms like X (with GTM team's blessing) to drive external hype.

### Stakeholder Approval Target (80/10/5 Rule) (How to know if you're doing a good job as a product manager)
A guideline for how much stakeholder approval a PM should aim for to know they're striking the right balance between being liked and pushing hard enough

How it works: Target stakeholder perception breakdown:
- ~80% of people loving you
- 5-10% feeling uneasy
- Occasionally a few people being like 'WTF'

Key insight: If 100% of people love you, you may not be pushing hard enough. People don't need to be happy or agree with you all the time, but in general leaders and teammates need to feel good about how you're operating and leading.

### Storytelling Reflection Questions for Meeting Prep (Getting buy-in)
A set of reflection questions to craft a streamlined meeting agenda that drives alignment and avoids unnecessary backstory.

How it works: Before presenting, reflect on these questions (referenced as a visual/slide in the newsletter):
- What context does the stakeholder NEED to know to make a decision?
- Eliminate everything else (or put it in the appendix).
- Focus on WHY stakeholders should care about the idea.

Examples of framing 'why':
- Prioritizing a recommendation feature will improve conversion.
- Changing the sprint process will free up time to reduce tech debt.

Key principle: Cut the backstory to prevent meeting attendees from zoning out. You can always share more information if asked.

### Tailored Communications Framework (How to build your PM second brain with ChatGPT)
A method for using the second brain to translate one initiative into multiple audience-specific communications

How it works: Common audience translations needed for any initiative:
- Marketing: Short blurb for announcements
- Leadership: Three crisp sentences for a QBR
- Enablement: Deck that makes value obvious to sales
- Design: User-testing script that speaks to the problem, not the roadmap

Workflow:
1. Word-dump everything into the Project: the overview, goals, nuances, what you believe is most important for each audience
2. It can be as unstructured and messy as you want
3. The second brain returns tailored versions for each audience in seconds
4. You review and make final calls

Key insight: The hard part isn't the writing—it's the mental 'reload cost' of switching contexts between audiences. The second brain eliminates that step.

### The 'Nevertheless' Framework (Annie Duke)
A communication tool for delivering firm decisions after hearing dissenting opinions without being coercive.

How it works: Structure: 'I hear you and your input was incorporated. Nevertheless, this is the path we are going to take.' This balances making people feel heard with maintaining leadership authority.

### The Silver Burrito Alignment Test (Setting goals)
A metaphor and mental model for diagnosing whether your team is truly aligned on what success looks like for a project or initiative

How it works: The Silver Burrito metaphor: A silver-wrapped Chipotle burrito looks delicious to everyone because each person is imagining their own favorite filling inside. Similarly, projects often look exciting to all team members because each person is imagining different goals they're personally most excited about (e.g., one person pictures 'increase conversion,' another 'make users happier,' another 'refactoring code'). A product leader's job is to 'unwrap the burrito' — explicitly define and communicate the specific goal so everyone sees the same thing. Use this as a diagnostic: if you haven't explicitly stated the goal, assume people are imagining different ones.

### The Silver Burrito Metaphor (A Three-Step Framework For Solving Problems 👌)
A mental model for understanding why teams are often misaligned on the problem they're solving, inspired by Chipotle billboard ads.

How it works: Chipotle billboards show a wrapped silver burrito — each viewer pictures their own ideal, most delicious burrito inside. Problem statements work the same way: everyone on your team has a unique version of the problem in their heads. Sometimes nearly identical, sometimes very different. The larger and more complex the project, the more likely they diverge. Your job is to 'open up the wrapper' and make sure everyone agrees on the burrito inside. This is why Step 2 (alignment) is critical.

### The Trust Equation (Paige Costello)
A formula for evaluating and building trust with stakeholders.

How it works: Trust = (Credibility + Reliability + Authenticity) / Perception of Self-Interest. Credibility is bringing insight. Reliability is your say-do ratio. Authenticity is vulnerability. Self-interest is your hidden agenda.

### Three Levels of Product Work (Impact, Execution, Optics) (Shreyas Doshi)
A framework for understanding that product work operates at three levels, and misalignment between people defaulting to different levels is a hidden source of team conflict

How it works: Impact: What outcomes are we creating? What is the effect on customers and the brand? (CEOs/founders often default here). Execution: What does it take to get things done? How do we hit milestones? (PMs often default here). Optics: Creating awareness of impact and execution internally. (Important but dangerous when it becomes the goal). Diagnostic questions: Who does the org promote—impact, execution, or optics people? What does the CEO praise at all-hands? Context-dependent guidance: Early-stage teams should optimize for execution; platform teams may need more optics for communication.

### Three Pillars of Getting Buy-In (Getting buy-in)
A framework of three tactics that have the largest impact on getting buy-in for your ideas: Co-creation, Presence, and Storytelling.

How it works: Three common reasons leaders fail to get buy-in:
1. Assuming everyone is thinking what you're thinking — the 'right answer' must be obvious, but it almost never is.
2. Feeling time-crunched — believing there isn't enough time to get everyone fully bought in.
3. Fear of disagreement — afraid someone will dissent, so you try to sneak the plan through (sometimes subconsciously).

Three tactics to counteract these:
1. Co-creation — Collaborate with others to identify the best solution. Creates better ideas and makes people feel invested.
2. Presence — Project mature self-confidence; own the room through verbal cues, body language, and mindset.
3. Storytelling — Present a narrative that resonates with your audience; focus on why they should care.

Core insight: Buy-in is the result of showing your team why your idea achieves THEIR goals.

### Three Pillars of Relationships (Evan LaPointe)
A biological breakdown of how professional relationships are evaluated by the brain.

How it works: 1. Ability: Utility and skill level. 2. Trust: Risk assessment (Level 1: simple delegation, Level 2: doing it as well as you, Level 3: doing it better than you). 3. Appeal/Experience: How it feels to interact with the person (the ultimate biological filter).

### Three Speeds of Influence (Evan LaPointe)
A model for choosing how to change someone's mind based on the situation and habitat.

How it works: 1. Slow: Let them fail and learn naturally. 2. Moderate: Teach them a new concept so they see the world differently over time (Challenger Sale approach). 3. Fast: Use cognitive dissonance to directly challenge the underlying belief driving their behavior.

### Three Tenets of PM Stakeholder Management (Vikrama Dhiman)
Three principles for working effectively with stakeholders as a PM, functioning as a daily mantra

How it works: (1) Raise difficult issues without being difficult to work with. (2) Bring out important topics without drawing importance to yourself. (3) Be in charge of getting decisions made without making all decisions yourself. Key technique: bring conversations from emotional space to logical space when pushing back.

### Tradeoff-Based No Framework (How tech’s most resilient workers handle burnout)
A method for saying no to requests by framing responses as tradeoffs rather than open-ended questions, maintaining control of your workload.

How it works: Instead of saying: 'I have options A or B. Which do you want?' (open-ended, blocks your ability to define a 'ready' state)

Say: 'I have option A and B. Here's how we got to these options. Here are the tradeoffs. Please let me know your thoughts. I'll move forward with A if I don't hear any new feedback.'

This reframes no's as tradeoffs — both to yourself and to others. Saying yes to something always means saying no to something else.

### Trust Battery Concept (How to get better at influence)
A mental model from Tobi Lütke (Shopify CEO) for thinking about trust as a gradient/battery rather than binary on/off

How it works: Trust is not binary (trust vs. don't trust) — it's a gradient with many points on the spectrum, like a battery. You can charge your trust battery through trust-building behaviors and draw on those reserves when you need people to trust you and follow your lead. The implication is that you should consciously invest in charging your trust battery with key people BEFORE you need to make withdrawals.

### Two Stack Levels Up/Down, Left/Right (Fareed Mosavat)
A mental model for organizational curiosity to build trust and earn sponsorship.

How it works: Down: Understand technical/billing details (e.g., Stripe, database). Up: Understand your boss's and their boss's priorities (e.g., board level strategy). Left/Right: Understand what adjacent teams (core product, sales, enterprise) are doing.

## Templates

### Active Listening Playback Statements (A PM’s guide to influence)
Ready-to-use statements for making stakeholders feel heard during meetings, including progressive alignment checkpoints

How it works: Playback statements to make people feel heard:
- 'What I'm hearing is [restate their concern in your own words]. Is that right?'
- 'It sounds like your main concern is [summarize]. Did I capture that correctly?'

Progressive alignment checkpoints (used during the meeting rather than waiting until the end):
- 'So it seems like we're aligned on [point A]. Is that fair to say?'
- 'Before we move on, let me make sure we're on the same page about [topic].'

Acknowledging feedback (example from Slack CEO interaction):
- 'We hear your concern about [specific concern]. We've incorporated that by [specific change].'
- 'Your feedback on [topic] was really helpful. Here's how we've addressed it...'

Purpose: People who feel dismissed go into a stress response (fight, flight, freeze, or fawn) and become defensive. People want reciprocity — they won't let you influence them if they feel you're not willing to let them influence you.

### Canonical Project Document (Naomi Gleit)
A single source of truth document for any project that outlines the basic building blocks and links to all other relevant documents.

How it works: Must include: 1. Discrete work streams. 2. Single-threaded owner (STO) for each work stream. 3. Canonical meetings list. 4. Canonical email list. 5. Canonical chat group. 6. Canonical nomenclature/vocabulary.

### Decision Deadlock-Breaking Script (Five habits of highly annoying product managers)
A verbal script PMs can use when a team discussion is stuck and consensus seems unlikely, to move forward decisively while maintaining psychological safety.

How it works: Script: 'It seems like we're not going to get to a consensus here. It happens! I vote we go with [X], move forward, see how it goes and change course if necessary. Everyone OK enough with that?'

Alternative tactic: Ask your team directly: 'Would you rather keep hashing this out, or would you prefer I just make a call so we can move on?'

Context: Use at ~70% information (per Bezos's recommendation). Waiting for 90% information means you're probably being too slow.

### Monthly Design WIP Deck (Katie Dill)
A low-friction way to share work-in-progress designs across the company.

How it works: A shared Google Slides deck where designers paste screenshots or prototypes of medium/large projects every month. Shared with PMs, Eng leaders, and execs to spot collisions and align on the user journey. Must be kept low-maintenance (no polished presentations required).

### Options and Recommendation Framework (Matt LeMay)
A structure for responding to stakeholder feature requests without saying no.

How it works: When asked to build something, present 3 options. Outline the trade-offs for each (especially regarding how it affects the team's impact goals), and provide 1 clear recommendation. This invites constructive feedback rather than defensive hole-punching.

### Product Digest (Christine Itwaru)
An internal communication tool to prepare revenue teams for upcoming product changes.

How it works: Focuses on readiness rather than just status updates. Includes: how to get ready for the feature, how to get excited about it, and how it impacts specific internal roles (like Customer Success Managers).

### Shareable Email: How to Get the Most Value Out of Your Data Scientist (This Week #5: Overcoming impostor syndrome, introducing growth to an org, and how to partner with your data scientist)
A ready-to-forward email that a data scientist (or PM) can send to product and engineering partners to establish effective working norms. Designed to be copied and sent as-is.

How it works: Subject: How to get the most value out of your data scientist

Hello,

I came across some advice on how to get the most value out of your data scientist, and here's what it said:

1. TREAT THEM AS A PARTNER: Share what you are trying to achieve and why, not just the queries you need run. If they are senior (and good), you'll be astounded by how many new ideas and novel insights they'll bring to the table.

2. KNOW WHAT MOTIVATES THEM: Find out what kind of work your DS partner enjoys doing most, and try to give them as much of that type of work as possible (when there is flexibility). Some DSs love knocking out answers to questions. Some love tackling long-term deep problems. Some love low-level infrastructure work.

3. PRIORITIZE ASKS: DSs are often barraged with questions, seemingly always urgent—a lot of 'this'll be quick!' Work with your DS on a (public) prioritized list of outstanding to-dos, and when new asks come in, work together on the updated priorities. Review together weekly.

4. KNOW WHAT YOU'LL DO WITH THE ANSWER: For the questions you ask your DS, have a clear and precise answer for 'What will you do with the answer when you have it?' Your DS should ask you this each time. You will find that many of the things you want to know are not actually going to change anything, and thus not worth looking into.

5. SHARE FINDINGS WIDELY: Each time a data question is answered or an insight discovered, encourage the DS to share it with the wider team and other DSs. Try to save them somewhere that will last. This information is gold for when you are looking for new ideas.

6. PUT THEM IN THE SPOTLIGHT: Encourage your DS to be the one sharing their work in meetings, presentations, brainstorms, etc. Help them get the credit they deserve for the work they do.

7. FIND A BALANCE: Don't be data-driven—be data-informed. Use the insights from the data as one input to a decision (so critical), but leave a bit of room for other inputs beyond data.

### Show Don't Tell Wall (Anuj Rathi)
A visual artifact mapping the exact user journey or company strategy to align stakeholders.

How it works: Use a specific person (not a generic persona). Map their exact situation, time of day, emotional state, and every screen/pixel they interact with, including real-time delays (e.g., 30-minute food delivery wait) and edge cases.

### Source-of-Truth Alignment Document (14 habits of highly effective product managers)
A living document that crystallizes the team's goal, problem statement, key assumptions, and key decisions to maintain alignment

How it works: Structure includes: (1) Goal — the single goal the team is working toward; (2) Problem statement — succinct description of the problem being solved; (3) Key assumptions — what the team believes to be true; (4) Key decisions — decisions that have been made and their rationale. This document is created early and revisited frequently to keep the team aligned. Changes to priorities/strategy are reflected here, automatically surfacing trade-offs and constraints.

## Checklists

### Authority Sources to Leverage (How to get better at influence)
Five types of influential people whose support can boost your credibility

How it works: In the workplace, you gain influence by building relationships and support from:
1. Team leads
2. Senior managers
3. Executives
4. The CEO
5. Specific influential people around the company

### Five Steps to Influence Prioritization in a Large Company (This Week #9: Breaking into growth, leading with influence, and (not) stepping on toes 🦶)
A checklist for PMs to get their asks prioritized when they lack direct authority, especially in bureaucratic organizations

How it works: 1. Find the decision-maker: Identify who makes the final call on prioritization. If they defer to someone else, focus on that person. If unclear, ask the most senior person involved 'Who's the decision-maker on this?'

2. Ask direct questions: When the decision-maker isn't ready to commit or kicks the can down the road, ask: 'What would it take to convince you this is worth prioritizing?' Then deliver exactly that.

3. Make the ask crystal clear and trustworthy: Tasks stall because they aren't concrete enough or leaders don't trust the scope estimate. Spend time refining the problem and solution. Get buy-in from the people who will do the work (engineer, designer, etc.).

4. Help them see why it's a priority: Build a strong case beyond 'it's a quick fix.' Understand the prioritization landscape. What convinced YOU it's high priority? Quantify the impact.

5. Set a deadline: Share a date by which you need a decision, even if arbitrary (e.g., 'I'd like to make a call by X/X to keep things moving swiftly'). If you miss the date, use it to highlight how long the process is taking.

Bonus — Shine a mirror to the org: If this is a recurring problem, put together a short doc or deck collecting examples of small things that took too long. Add your recommendations for how to fix the problem. Share with leaders — they'll either agree and want to address it, or explain why things work as intended.

### Five-Step Playbook for Resolving Role Overlap with a Peer (This Week #2: Tackling the chicken-and-egg problem, building a growth team from scratch, and addressing overlap with PM peers 🤔)
A step-by-step approach for a new PM (or anyone) to resolve role ambiguity and tension with a peer who has overlapping responsibilities and more tenure

How it works: 1. **First, try to work it out with the person directly**: Talk to them one-on-one. Point out that you want what's best for the team (and company), you're feeling hampered and frustrated with a lack of clarity around your roles, and that you'd like to resolve this to make both of your lives better. If you reach agreement, codify it and share with your manager(s) to ensure alignment.
2. **If that doesn't work, go to your manager(s)**: Share concrete examples of where you see overlap, the negative impact it has been having, and your perspective on how to address it. Lean on them to clarify roles with you and the other person (and their manager). Don't be afraid to bring this up early — these things rarely get worked out without a manager being involved.
3. **Differentiate between historical roles vs. ideal roles**: Encourage everyone to step back from how things are today and think about what ideal roles and responsibilities *should* be as the org scales. What should each role be responsible for? What skillsets should you hire for? What is the career progression for each?
4. **Avoid waiting for the perfect answer**: Don't wait for all details to be worked out before making changes (this can take months or years). Figure out the biggest issues, align on a few improvements, and act on them.
5. **Consider changing titles or teams**: Maybe the program manager should be a product manager? Maybe you two should be on different teams? Think about the best long-term structural change, not just short-term fixes.

### Airbnb Double-Blind Review System — Using Evidence to Get Buy-In (How to get better at influence)
Real example of how Lenny's team used evidence from other fields to convince execs to approve a risky review system overhaul

How it works: Situation: The team wanted to change Airbnb's review system so that you could only see the other person's review after you left YOUR review (double-blind approach), hypothesizing this would lead to more honest reviews.

Challenge: Revamping the review system was a huge risk — time-intensive, could destroy credibility in the review system, and have unintended second effects.

Approach: Pulled together evidence from other fields including double-blind studies, experiment design principles, and other online review systems.

Result: The evidence convinced both the team and execs. The change not only increased review honesty but also increased the percentage of people who left reviews.

Lesson: If you're having trouble influencing others, bring more evidence. Ask the other person what evidence would convince them.

### Airbnb Superhost Badge — Reframing the Pitch (How to get better at influence)
Real example of how Lenny convinced the Airbnb search team to add a Superhost badge by reframing his pitch from his team's goals to theirs

How it works: Situation: Lenny was leading the launch of the Superhost program at Airbnb and needed the search team to add a Superhost badge to listing cards.

First pitch (failed): Focused on Lenny's team's goals — increasing host retention and host engagement. The search team was skeptical that the badge would pull users toward suboptimal (lower-ranked) listings.

Result: The search team agreed, the A/B test showed positive results for guest conversion, and everyone was happy.

Lesson: Before asking someone to do something for you, take 10 seconds to reflect on what THEY want. Frame your ask in a way that highlights their goals.
