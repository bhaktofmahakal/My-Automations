# Mastering Customer Interviews - Frameworks, Templates & Checklists

*32 artifacts extracted from Lenny's Podcast and Newsletter*

## Frameworks

### Automated Customer Interview Recruiting System (Teresa Torres)
A system for automating weekly customer interview scheduling so product teams can maintain a continuous interviewing cadence without manual recruiting effort

How it works: Inspired by Nudge's choice architecture concept. Two strategies: 1) For consumers/end users: Embed an in-product intercept (like an NPS survey placement) that asks 'Do you have 20 minutes to talk to us?' If yes, send scheduling software link (Calendly, etc.), they pick a time, interview appears on calendar automatically. Can be refined by targeting where/who/how you show it. 2) For B2B buyers/decision-makers: Use internal teams (sales, account managers, support) who are already on calls with those people. Define a weekly trigger: 'This week we're looking to talk to someone experiencing [specific need/pain point]. If you're on a call with someone like that, send them our scheduling link.' Goal: product team does zero recruiting work. Tools mentioned: Scheduling (Calendly, Outlook, Google, Salesforce). Intercepts (Qualaroo, Ethnio, Intercom, Usabilla, Chameleon, Hotjar).

### Dollar-Driven Customer Discovery (Todd Jackson)
A structured approach to customer discovery that tests actual dollar potential rather than just gathering qualitative feedback, organized in three phases

How it works: Phase 1 - Identify Extreme Value: Ask non-leading questions about top 3 goals, what's hard about them, what's standing in their way. Present solution and look for 'wow statements' (not the word 'interesting' which means no) or demonstrated behavior (asking to meet again, wanting to share with team). Phase 2 - Ability to Pay: Ask if they're currently looking for a solution or building internally, where budget would come from (existing budget is best signal), and how their team makes decisions on third-party tools. Phase 3 - Willingness to Pay: Use the fair/expensive/prohibitive pricing framework (from Madhavan Ramanujam): What's a fair price? What's expensive? What's prohibitively expensive? The 'expensive' price is usually what they'll actually pay.

### Dropped Facts Interviewing Technique (My podcast tech stack, workflows, and lessons)
A technique for catching and revisiting interesting things guests mention offhand but quickly move past

How it works: When a guest mentions something interesting offhand but quickly moves on, note it and come back to it later. Guests move on for several reasons:
- They don't want to go off track
- They don't want to keep talking endlessly
- They don't want to sound egotistical

Often, the guest very much wants to talk about the 'dropped fact' and will give rich, detailed answers when you circle back to it. This technique surfaces some of the best content in interviews.

### Four Forces (Paul Adams)
A framework within Jobs to be Done to understand why people switch products.

How it works: Evaluates four elements: the attraction of the new solution, the push of the current situation, the anxiety of the new solution, and the habit of the present. Can be used for product adoption or even recruiting candidates.

### Funnel of Use — Talk to Failed Users (Uri Levine)
Instead of only speaking with successful users, systematically interview users who dropped off at each stage of the usage funnel.

How it works: Funnel stages: 1) Downloaded/visited but didn't register. 2) Registered but didn't try the product. 3) Tried but didn't get value. 4) Got value but didn't come back. At each stage, ask the people who dropped off WHY — they know something critical about your product's gaps. Focus on people far down the funnel who still bounced, not people who were never a fit.

### Gusto's Emotional Reaction Test for Customer Interviews (How to validate your B2B startup idea)
A framework for evaluating whether customer interview feedback indicates genuine demand, based on the intensity of emotional response

How it works: How to evaluate customer interview responses:

**Weak signal (means NO):**
You: 'Here's a new type of mint. It's different because it's organic and local.'
Customer: 'Oh yeah, that's cool.'
You: 'Would you buy it?'
Customer: 'Yeah, yeah, I may buy it.'
→ They're just being nice. They're not going to buy it.

**Strong signal (means YES):**
Ask: 'What do you feel about your current [solution]?'
Response: They start cursing. They get really upset unprompted. More than half of Gusto's interviewees started cursing about their payroll provider.
→ When you hear that strong emotion, you know you have something.

Key insight: What you're looking for is really, really deep emotion — not polite interest.

### Jobs to Be Done Framework (My favorite product management templates)
A framework for understanding customer needs by identifying the 'job' they are hiring a product to do

How it works: 8 things to use in the JTBD framework for product development. Focuses on understanding the underlying job customers hire products to accomplish rather than demographic or feature-based analysis. Linked at: https://uxdesign.cc/8-things-to-use-in-jobs-to-be-done-framework-for-product-development-4ae7c6f3c30b

### Jobs-to-be-Done Interrogation Technique (Robby Stein)
Clayton Christensen's interview/interrogation method for understanding why users hire a product, distinguishing utility jobs from emotional jobs

How it works: Interview users like an interrogation: 'Why do you use my product? Where were you? Were you in bed? At work? What were you doing? What brought it up?' Keep asking 'why' until you reach the causal moment (the 'big hire'). Key insight: distinguish utility jobs from emotional jobs — emotional jobs are often discounted but can be the primary driver (e.g., Close Friends was about feeling connected, not just sharing content).

### Layers of Language (Bob Moesta)
A mental model for navigating customer interviews to get past superficial answers and uncover true causality.

How it works: 1. Pablum layer (surface level, e.g., 'it was good'), 2. Fantasy/Nightmare layer (exaggerations of how good or bad it was), 3. Truth layer (the actual causal events and context).

### Nielsen Number for Research Right-Sizing (Shaun Clowes)
The principle that 7-14 user interviews is the sweet spot—fewer than 7 means not enough data, more than 14 means diminishing returns.

How it works: Before conducting research: 1) Right-size to 7-14 interviews, 2) Avoid leading questions designed to confirm what you already believe, 3) Set up to learn, not to validate. If you don't do these basics, no amount of LLM analysis will help—you'll just read back what you wanted to hear.

### Problem Priority Discovery Questions (Jen Abel)
A sequence of questions to determine if a prospect has a real, painful problem worth solving.

How it works: 1. Ask how they visualize the problem/solution in their head. 2. Check if it's a growing/widening problem. 3. Ask: 'Are you measuring or managing this problem today?' (If no, move on). 4. Ask: 'How have you tried to solve for it?' (Headcount, another tool, open gap). Avoid asking 'What keeps you up at night?'

### Six Phases of the Buying Timeline (Bob Moesta)
A timeline mapping the psychological stages a customer goes through when purchasing a product.

How it works: 1. First thought, 2. Passive looking (problem aware, solution unaware), 3. Active looking (problem and solution aware), 4. Deciding (making trade-offs), 5. First use, 6. Ongoing use.

### Story-Based Interview Technique (Teresa Torres)
An interviewing method where you collect specific behavioral stories from customers by asking about a recent concrete experience and then following the timeline with curiosity, rather than asking direct opinion questions

How it works: Steps: 1) Ask ONE opening question: 'Tell me about the last time you [did relevant activity].' 2) Situate them in the moment: 'Set the scene for me. Where were you?' 3) Follow the timeline: 'What happened next?' 'Tell me about the moment where you decided to...' 4) Go deep on friction points: When they mention something interesting, slow down and ask for more detail rather than moving on. 5) Summarize what you heard to show you're listening and bring them back to moments needing more detail. Anti-patterns to avoid: Don't write 50-question protocols. Don't ask direct opinion questions ('What do you like?' 'How do you decide?'). Don't stay shallow and move to the next story too quickly. Don't focus on what people would do—focus on what they actually did. Success indicator: The customer says 'When can we do this again?'

### Three Sources of Energy (Bob Moesta)
A framework for categorizing the types of energy required or expended when a customer tries to make progress.

How it works: 1. Functional energy (time, space, effort, knowledge), 2. Emotional energy (how the user feels internally), 3. Social energy (how the user wants others to perceive them).

### User Research by Observation (Summary: Lessons from working with 600+ YC startups | Gustaf Alströmer (Y Combinator, Airbnb))
Gustaf's approach to understanding user pain points through observation rather than questions

How it works: Core principle: Don't ask users if something is working—watch them do it. Observation reveals pain points that verbal feedback misses.

Key insight about user reactions:
- Founders fear users will try the product and hate it
- The most common reaction is actually indifference, not hatred
- This means: if you make improvements and come back, users will almost always give you a second chance
- Example: Airbnb started with air mattresses and iterated from there; Brian Chesky continues this approach today

Implication: Don't let fear of rejection stop you from putting your product in front of users. The downside is lower than you think.

## Templates

### Customer Call Prep Agent (Zapier Agents) (Make product management fun again with AI agents)
A complete step-by-step build guide for an AI agent that runs daily at 8am, scans your calendar for external participants, researches them on the web, and sends you a Slack DM summary before each meeting.

How it works: Ingredients: Zapier Agents + Google Calendar (or Outlook) + Slack (or MS Teams)

10 Steps:
1. Create Zapier account, navigate to 'Zapier Agents'
2. Select 'Create a custom agent,' name it, click 'Start from scratch'
3. Click 'Configure' at top, then 'Create behavior'
4. Set automation to run every day at 8 a.m.
5. Paste this prompt into instructions:

"Look at all my calendar events for today [CALENDAR FIND MULTIPLE EVENTS], find all the external participants (people who have a different email address domain than mine), and do a web search for each of those email addresses. Summarize who these people are (where they work, what title they have, how long they've been in that role, information on past roles, and anything else you deem relevant) and send me a message in Slack.

[SEND DIRECT MESSAGE] for each meeting with external participants (skip meetings with only internal participants) and tell me:
- Name of meeting
- Time of meeting
- Name and relevant information of each external participant (formatted on their own line)"

6. Delete placeholder [CALENDAR FIND MULTIPLE EVENTS], click 'Insert tools,' choose Google Calendar 'Find Multiple Events'
7. Link calendar software, select personal calendar
8. Delete placeholder [SEND DIRECT MESSAGE], use 'Insert tools' to connect Slack 'Send Direct Message' to yourself
9. Click 'Save instructions & test'
10. If happy, turn it on (only action is privately DMing you, so low risk)

### Customer Conversation Outreach Template (Zoelle Egner)
Simple email template for founders/PMs to consistently get customer conversations on their calendar

How it works: Template structure: 'Hey [name], thank you so much for using [product]. I would really love to hear about your experience so far and get your feedback. Do you have 10 minutes to talk on the phone?' Process: 1) Write the template once. 2) Have a hypothesis about the type of person you want to talk to. 3) Run a query against your database to find matching users. 4) Send three of these emails per week. 5) Do phone calls, not surveys (unstructured conversation yields richer insights). Key principle: None of this is complicated—you just need a system.

### Five Customer Discovery Interview Questions (This Week #3: Finding product/market fit, interviewing for a Director of PM role, and structuring discussions with senior leaders)
Concrete questions to ask potential customers after describing your product idea, designed to uncover pain points, current solutions, and willingness to pay

How it works: Ask these questions after you describe your idea:

1. How are you currently solving this problem?
2. What annoys you about your current approach?
3. If you had a magic wand, what would you wish for to solve this problem?
4. How much would you pay for this if it existed?
5. Why is this a problem for you? [Listen for answer] Why is that? [Listen for answer] Why is that? [Listen for answer] Why is that?

Note: Question 5 uses the 'Five Whys' technique — keep asking 'why' to drill down to the core pain point.

### Magic Wand Customer Interview Question (How to make an impact in your first 90 days)
A specific customer interview question designed to move beyond feature requests to underlying problems

How it works: Question: 'If you had a magic wand, what would you change about [product]?'

When to use: Talk to a few customers directly right away, or even before you join the company.

Why it works: Answers often break out from feature gripes into the underlying problems customers face that they wish you were solving better.

Benefit: Armed with this intel, you can share firsthand insights with the team during a period when you're otherwise operating with minimal data. Also role-models customer-centric behaviors and curiosity for your team.

## Checklists

### Community Member Research Process (A founder’s guide to community)
Step-by-step process for conducting user research interviews before launching a community

How it works: Steps:
1. **Start a spreadsheet** of people to interview. Include:
   - People you already have relationships with (more likely to say yes)
   - People you don't know well (will be more honest)
   - Criteria for diversity: roles (job titles, departments, company size) and demographics (race, sex, age, location)

2. **Reach out to 10 people to start.** Ask for a 30-minute call. Take lots of notes. Pull out 2-3 key insights per conversation.

3. **After interviews, follow up:**
   - Thank them for their time and feedback
   - Share key themes from research
   - Share how it's shaping the community plan
   - Ask if you're on the right track
   - Ask if they'd be interested in being a founding member

4. **Make it ongoing:** Commit to talking to at least one community member every week.

Key questions to answer in interviews:
- What are the biggest challenges they face in their role/industry?
- Where do they currently go for help and support?
- What communities are they already a part of?
- What would motivate them to participate in a community?
- What value would they expect to get?
- How much time could they commit?
- What format would work best for them (online, events, etc.)?

### Customer Discovery Signal Detection (Todd Jackson)
How to distinguish genuine interest from polite rejection during customer discovery conversations

How it works: Positive signals (wow statements): 'Does that really work?' 'I'd sign up for the wait list today.' Positive signals (demonstrated behavior): 'Can we meet again next week?' 'Can you send me the deck? I want to show my team.' Negative signals: 'That sounds kind of interesting' (interesting = polite no). 'We don't have the budget' = no. 'Not the right time, let's talk next year' = no. Rule of thumb for enough interviews: when you can predict 70-80% of what the next person will say.

### Four Signals of Pain and Pull (What to do if your product isn’t taking off)
Four concrete indicators that tell you whether your product is solving a real problem, used during customer discovery conversations

How it works: When talking to people, look for two things: pain and pull. Pain tells you there's an opportunity to solve a problem. Pull tells you that you're actually solving the problem.

1. People pay you money: Several people start to (or offer to) pay for your early product, ideally people you don't have a direct connection to.
2. Strong emotion: You're hearing hatred for the incumbents (i.e. pain) or a deep and strong emotional reaction to your idea (i.e. pull).
3. Cold inbound interest: You're seeing cold inbound interest in your product.
4. Continued usage: If you've got a prototype running, people continue to use your product even if it's bad.

### Seven Signals to Look for in User Conversations (Prioritizing at startups)
What to listen for when talking to early users and potential users to generate product ideas

How it works: When talking to early users and potential users, look for:
1. Significant pain points
2. Ideas that get them visibly excited, especially if that happens with multiple people
3. Features that would get them to switch from a competitor
4. Table stakes—features that would keep them from being able to use your product even if they wanted to
5. Potential solutions you could test quickly without a lot of code
6. Products that they'd pay you for right now if those existed
7. Early adopters to try out your early product—people who love trying new products

### Spending Time as a Founding Team (50/50 Rule) (A guide for finding product-market fit in B2B)
Spenser Skates' hard-won rule for how B2B founding teams should allocate their time.

How it works: Rule: 50% of founding team collective time should be spent talking to customers. 50% should be spent building.

Context: Amplitude's all-engineer founding team spent about a year building when they should have spent half that time talking to customers. They wasted time solving problems that didn't really exist or didn't have money or traction behind them.

For B2B: 'Talking to customers' means becoming a salesperson.
For Consumer: 'Talking to customers' means meeting them directly (like Airbnb famously did).

Anti-pattern: Engineer founders default to building because they know how to solve those problems, but they end up solving problems that don't have money or traction behind them.

### User Research Session Questions (How to develop product sense)
Three sample open-ended questions to ask during user research sessions to build empathy and uncover subtle needs.

How it works: 1. What do you think the purpose of this product is? Who do you think it's for?
   → Reveals how effective your landing page is; gives you words that resonate with users to describe your product.
2. Now that you're in the product, what actions do you want to take?
   → Helps you understand which features are easy to discover and how clear your navigation and CTAs are.
3. What are you thinking right now? How does that make you feel?
   → Ask at every new step the user lands on, because people tend not to notice their self-talk and feelings otherwise.

## Examples

### Facebook India Friend Graph Fix (Bangaly Kaba)
A case study demonstrating the importance of in-market understand work to overcome cultural product assumptions.

How it works: Indian users had far fewer connections. On-the-ground research revealed Western profile fields (job, school) were irrelevant, and names (e.g., Amit Kumar) were too common. Users relied on photos (cars, pets) to identify friends, forcing a redesign of the recommendation UI.

### Persona - Rick Song's 'Friend Zone' Avoidance Technique (Todd Jackson)
Rick Song sat down with early customers one-on-one and directly asked whether Persona was a necessity, what would happen if it disappeared, and whether they'd switch to a cheaper competitor

How it works: Questions asked to first 5-10 customers: 1. Is Persona a necessity for your company? 2. If we went away, how painful would that be? 3. If a competitor came along that charged half as much, would you switch? Framing: 'I need your help. It is very important to me that this company succeeds. I don't want you to be nice to me.' Purpose: avoid being in the 'friend zone' where customers like you but don't need you.

### Search Behavior Discovery and Fix (The inside story of Facebook Marketplace)
How Facebook discovered users weren't searching on Marketplace and the simple UX fix that permanently changed behavior

How it works: Problem: Search numbers on Marketplace were incredibly low after launch. Research method: Showed buyers two test phones — one with Marketplace, one with a competing local classifieds service. Asked them to 'find an iPhone to buy.' Finding: On Facebook, users immediately started scrolling; on other apps, they searched. Insight: People had learned that Facebook's search bar was only for finding people, so they never thought to use it for products. Solution: Added a large search button next to the search bar. Result: Search participation rate changed trajectory dramatically. Even after the button was removed, search rates remained at the new higher level — a permanent behavioral shift.

### The Facebook Super Hider (Judd Antin)
A case study showing how a single qualitative user interview (N=1) can falsify a flawed quantitative assumption.

How it works: Facebook engineers wanted to use 'hiding a post' as a signal for bad post quality. Research brought in a 'super hider' for an observation session behind a two-way mirror. The user hid every single post they read, revealing they were just trying to achieve 'inbox zero' on an infinite scroll. This immediately proved to the observing engineers that 'hides' was a flawed signal for quality.
