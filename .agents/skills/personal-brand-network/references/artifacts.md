# Personal Brand Network - Frameworks, Templates & Checklists

*43 artifacts extracted from Lenny's Podcast and Newsletter*

## Frameworks

### Boomer-Compatible Story (Paul Millerd)
A communication strategy for handling questions about unconventional work by having a simple, socially legible description ready.

How it works: When asked what you do, have a simple label ready that doesn't invite interrogation: 'I'm an entrepreneur,' 'I'm a business owner,' 'I'm tinkering' (Lenny's version). Purpose: Deflect social pressure and avoid triggering other people's insecurities about work. Especially useful for conversations with parents and non-tech people.

### Content Cadence Sustainability Framework (Taking the week off + a newsletter cadence update)
A simple decision framework for adjusting content output based on audience feedback and creator sustainability.

How it works: Key inputs for deciding to change cadence:
1. Audience feedback — Are readers/listeners saying there's too much content?
2. Creator sustainability — Can you maintain this pace long-term (forever)?
3. Depth vs. volume tradeoff — Will fewer pieces allow deeper, higher-value content?
4. Total value audit — What else do subscribers get beyond the core content? (e.g., community, archives, tools, partnerships)

Lenny's specific change:
- Newsletter: From 3 paid + 1 free post/month → 2-4 paid posts/month, 0 free posts
- Podcast: From 1-2 episodes/week → 1 episode/week (starting 2026)
- Rationale: Most common feedback was 'too much content'; wants to sustain this forever; compensates with full value bundle.

### Content Creation 'Diet and Exercise' Rule (Sriram and Aarthi)
The most important rule for building a content presence: create something every single day to build muscle, rather than spending weeks on one perfect piece

How it works: 1) Create content every single day — this builds muscle and familiarity with the medium. 2) Don't spend weeks building up one perfect tweetstorm/blog post then stop — the effort is too high and unsustainable. 3) You don't need accomplishments to share — talk about your journey, what you're learning. 4) Be authentic, not aspirational (don't LARP as someone more senior). 5) Write about what you genuinely know deeply, even if it seems niche. 6) What seems basic to your peers is valuable to a much larger audience.

### Content Hierarchy of BS (Wes Kao)
A pyramid showing that different content formats allow different levels of unsubstantiated claims, with Twitter/keynotes at the bottom (most room for BS) and cohort-based courses at the top (least room for BS)

How it works: Bottom of pyramid (most BS room): Twitter, podcasts, short articles, keynote speeches — one-directional, can't be challenged. Middle: Long-form in-depth articles, books — must defend ideas and convince readers. Top (least BS room): Courses, especially cohort-based courses — live interaction, students can ask questions and challenge in real-time via chat. Key insight: hold yourself to the highest standard regardless of format.

### Counter-Programming Narrative (Hilary Gridley)
A mental model for recovering from a professional blow by identifying the feared perception and taking a small action to prove the opposite.

How it works: 1. Identify the negative event. 2. Ask: 'What am I afraid this person thinks of me?' 3. Ask: 'What is one small thing I can do to demonstrate the opposite?' 4. Execute the action without litigating the past.

### DM Q&A as Micro-Blogging Strategy (Ryan Hoover)
A content creation approach where you open DMs, invite questions from your audience, answer them privately, then share anonymized screenshots publicly.

How it works: Steps: 1) Open DMs on Twitter, 2) Periodically tweet 'DM me if you have questions' to founders/audience, 3) Answer questions privately—the prompt comes from others, solving writer's block, 4) Screenshot your answer, summarize the question (keep it anonymous, remove sensitive details), 5) Share on Twitter every 3-4 days. Benefits: micro-blogging without needing to find topics, refines your own thinking, helps others with similar questions, generates discussion.

### Four Jobs of a Newsletter (Camille Ricketts)
The four primary reasons people read newsletters.

How it works: 1. Make money (e.g., investing/crypto). 2. Entertain (e.g., memes/cartoons). 3. Get better at work/life (e.g., self-improvement). 4. Inform (e.g., news).

### Infinite Games Content Strategy (1,000,000)
A mental model for sustaining a long-term content business by prioritizing stamina over virality

How it works: Core principle: Prioritize stamina over anything else. Individual viral posts come and go, but success is determined by how long you can stick with it.

Content selection heuristic evolution:
- Early days: 80% what you're energized/curious/excited about, 20% what audience wants
- At scale: 100% what you're curious about. Never publish something only because you know it'll do well.

Sustainability tactics:
1. Use guest posts to supplement your own ideas (most popular posts are now guest posts)
2. Invest 10+ hours editing each guest post through 5-6 iterations to maintain quality bar
3. Only take on projects that bring you joy (dropped Maven course because it didn't; created space for podcast which was better aligned)
4. Play 'infinite games' — don't rush milestones

### Jeff Atwood's Audience Building Formula (Gergely)
A simple three-step rule for building a successful blog or audience on the internet.

How it works: 1. Write a blog post. 2. Do this three times a week. 3. Do it for two years.

### LinkedIn 30 (Ada Chen Rekhi)
A challenge to overcome the fear of content creation by posting daily.

How it works: Post on LinkedIn every day for 30 days straight to get past the barrier of sharing. Reframing tip (from Lenny): Write for an audience of one, focusing on crystallizing a thought rather than chasing virality.

### Newsletter Growth Formula: Quality + Consistency (500,000)
A simple framework asserting that the only two variables that matter for newsletter growth are content quality and publishing consistency. Everything else (design, title, strategy, growth plan, vision) is secondary.

How it works: Things that don't matter: your design, your title, your strategy, your growth plan, vision... most things.

Things that matter: quality, consistency.

Growth comes from publishing something valuable, that people want to share with their friends and colleagues, over and over and over.

If your newsletter isn't growing, diagnose: Is it a quality problem or a consistency problem?

### Newsletter Growth Hierarchy (1,000,000)
A prioritized framework of what actually drives newsletter growth based on Lenny's experience to 1M subscribers

How it works: What works (in order of impact):
1. Word of mouth — the primary driver of all growth. Earned by publishing something valuable that people want to share, consistently, over years.
2. Platform recommendation features (e.g., Substack Recommendations) — responsible for roughly doubling growth from ~500K to 1M. But only works if other writers find your content worth recommending (i.e., still WOM).
3. Product bundles (e.g., Greatest Bundle Ever) — drove paid subscriber growth, but requires an existing large following. Doesn't work for early-stage.

What DOESN'T work:
- Paid ads
- SEO
- Referral programs
- Business development

Early-stage tactics that worked:
1. Guest posts in established newsletters (First Round Review, Andrew Chen's blog)
2. A popular content series (marketplace business series caused a large spike)
3. Publishing something valuable every single week for 9+ months

### Newsletter JTBD Identification Framework (500,000)
Two-question framework for defining your newsletter's job-to-be-done, with examples of JTBD categories from successful newsletters

How it works: Answer these two questions:
1. Who *exactly* is your audience? Think of a specific person—what would they find extremely interesting or useful?
2. What is the *concrete* job you're doing for them? Is it entertainment, helping them make money, inspiring them, helping them understand the world, something else?

Example JTBD categories:
- Help me parent better: ParentData by Emily Oster
- Help me cook better: Broken Palate, a newsletter by Alison Roman
- Help me understand what's happening in tech: Stratechery, Platformer
- Help me understand what's happening in the world: Noahpinion, The Free Press
- Inspire me: Adam Grant, Austin Kleon
- Entertain me: Seeing Things, Alphabet Soup

There are many categories and room for many winners within each.

### Personal Board of Directors (Gibson Biddle, Jeffrey Pfeffer)
A curated group of peers and mentors used to gut-check decisions and navigate career choices.

How it works: Consists of peers (former colleagues to compare notes/data with) and mentors (to see around corners). Rule: Don't ask someone to be a mentor; find ways to be helpful to them first.

### Staggered Peer Mentorship (Drew Houston)
A strategy for building an advisory network across different stages of company maturity.

How it works: Involves building relationships with founders at your current stage (for tactical micro advice), 2-5 years ahead (for scaling and macro advice), and 10-20 years ahead (for philosophical and meta advice).

### The Seven Rules of Power (Jeffrey Pfeffer)
A mental model for acquiring and wielding influence in organizations.

How it works: 1. Get out of your own way. 2. Break the rules. 3. Appear powerful. 4. Build a powerful brand. 5. Network relentlessly. 6. Use your power. 7. Success excuses almost everything.

### The Three Musketeers Content Strategy (Gergely)
Writing a larger project (like a book) by serializing it into smaller, engaging weekly newsletter posts.

How it works: Write content that satisfies immediate reader needs (like a weekly newsletter) while simultaneously building chapters for a larger, cohesive product (a book), using the immediate financial/audience pressure to get the larger project done.

### Whom to Ask: Six Selection Criteria (On asking for help (even when you really don’t want to))
Six factors for evaluating and selecting the right person to ask for help.

How it works: Evaluate potential help-givers on:
1. **Connection:** How strong is our relationship?
2. **Goals:** How overlapping are our goals?
3. **Expertise:** How relevant is their skill set?
4. **Seniority:** How much more positional power do they have?
5. **Ease:** How convenient is it for me, and for them?
6. **Reciprocity:** How much have I already supported them?

Key heuristic: Could my ask be at all compelling to this person? If not, rethink the ask or do more legwork.

Note: You don't need a profound relationship before asking. You can build the relationship WHILE asking by making the ask significantly more compelling. The level of intention you put behind your request is palpable.

## Templates

### Personal Interest Inventory (Matthew Dicks)
A strategic list of personal facts to weave into business conversations to build connections.

How it works: Create a list of personal traits/hobbies. Map each to its 'Total Addressable Market' (how many people share it) and 'Intensity of Connection' (how deeply they care). Example: Married = High TAM, Moderate Intensity. Marathoner = Low TAM, High Intensity. Weave these into answers to casual questions like 'How are you?'

### Three-Sentence Twitter DM Pitch to Web3 Founders (A product manager’s guide to web3)
A concise outreach format for reaching web3 founders or core contributors

How it works: Format: Send a concise 3-sentence pitch via Twitter DM to the founder or core contributor. Structure should convey: (1) Who you are and your relevant background, (2) What specific value you can add based on the project's status, and (3) A clear ask to connect. Focus on working backward from their problems, goals, and community ethos. Note: Many teams may not even know they need a PM, so frame your value in terms of their specific challenges rather than the PM title.

### Unique Success Interview Question (Camille Ricketts)
A prompt used to extract unique, non-obvious insights from interviewees for content creation.

How it works: Ask: 'What is one thing that you think led to your success that nobody else in your peer set has done? What was something you did on a lark or as a big bet that ended up being great?'

## Checklists

### 10-Person Networking Exercise (Jeffrey Pfeffer)
A tactical approach to building a strategic network without feeling cringy.

How it works: 1. Write a list of 10 people who would be important for your career goals. 2. For each person, figure out how to meet them (e.g., host a dinner, send a relevant article, introduce them to someone useful).

### Business Dining Etiquette Checklist (Sam Lessin)
Rules for navigating business meals as a founder or junior professional

How it works: 1) Don't order the most expensive thing on the menu, 2) Don't order first — let others set the tone for starters/length, 3) Go middle-of-pack to last when ordering, 4) Tip minimum 20%, more in high-profile situations, 5) Always offer to pay (even with billionaires) — expect to be declined, 6) Split bills evenly — don't itemize, 7) B for bread (left), D for drinks (right) — use hand shapes, 8) Knife blade faces inward, 9) Napkin in lap, 10) Offer sommelier a sip of nice wine, 11) Don't make your tip memorable in any direction

### Common Mistakes When Starting a Newsletter (Sachin Monga)
Pitfalls to avoid when launching a Substack or newsletter business

How it works: Common mistakes: (1) Overthinking audience perception — worrying too much about frequency, charging, or taking breaks, (2) Undervaluing yourself — being afraid to turn on payments when there's clearly demand, (3) Thinking it's 'too late' — the market is much earlier than it feels, (4) Setting stakes too high at launch — instead, frame it as an experiment, (5) Not opening up optionality — don't prematurely close doors on what the newsletter could become, (6) Forgetting that subscribers are forgiving — they granted you 'write access to their brain' and want you to succeed.

### Community Building as Party Hosting (Sriram and Aarthi)
Sriram's framework for building micro-communities based on dinner party hosting principles

How it works: 1) Set the vibe: tell people what kind of 'party' it is (formal dinner vs. sports bar). 2) Curate the original mix: include different energy types (the famous VP, the boisterous young exec, the quiet thoughtful person). 3) Welcome newcomers: give them an easy opening/introduction that makes them feel comfortable. 4) Monitor the emotional temperature: watch for people dominating or going silent. 5) Create rituals: do something at a regular cadence (e.g., weekly Zoom, monthly meetup). 6) Start small (5-10 people) for intimacy. 7) As you grow, create separate smaller spaces to maintain intimacy.

### Conversation Exit Strategies (Sam Lessin)
Techniques for gracefully ending conversations and leaving events

How it works: 1) Stand when someone leaves/arrives at the table, 2) Shake hands standing (never sitting), 3) 'I'm going to grab a drink' = conversation is ending (don't follow them), 4) Introduce them to someone else as a handoff, 5) Send a brief thank-you follow-up, 6) Don't take a call mid-exit — hit 'call back' button then walk away, 7) At large events, use the Irish goodbye (just leave quietly), 8) Maybe thank the host on the way out, 9) Don't make a production of leaving

### Digital Footprint Audit Checklist (How to pass any first-round interview (even in a terrible talent market))
A three-step checklist for reviewing and cleaning up your online presence before interviewing

How it works: 1. Know what's out there — search for content about you or authored by you (LinkedIn posts, Medium/Substack articles, GitHub repos, tweets, old blog posts, quotes in newspaper articles)
2. Assess whether it fits how you'd like to present yourself
3. Modify as needed to represent yourself as you'd prefer

Example: If looking for a data science role, spend a few hours organizing Python code in a public machine learning GitHub repo — refactor code written under timeline duress so you put your best foot forward if someone skims it.

Estimated time: 1-2 hours (one-time task for entire job search)

### Guild Night Format (Luc Levesque)
A logistical blueprint for hosting high-value networking dinners.

How it works: Rules: Exactly 6 to 8 people. Must be hosted at a home, not a restaurant. Food is catered/DoorDashed to avoid cooking stress. Centered around one specific mutual topic (e.g., AI, SEO). Runs from 6:00 PM to 9:00/10:00 PM.

### Introductions Checklist (Sam Lessin)
Step-by-step etiquette for entering a room and meeting people

How it works: 1) Arrive 10-15 minutes early (not more), 2) If late, apologize simply and move on — don't make a production, 3) Firm handshake (don't crush), 4) Repeat their name back ('Great to meet you, Lenny'), 5) Maintain eye contact, 6) Introduce your partner/companion first, 7) Use 'great to see you' instead of 'nice to meet you' (works whether you've met or not), 8) If someone else is late, don't make them feel bad about it

### Micro-Ask Mentor Outreach (Jules Walter)
A process for building a relationship with a high-profile mentor without overwhelming them.

How it works: 1. Reach out with a highly specific question that takes 2 minutes to answer via email. 2. Apply the advice. 3. Follow up weeks later showing the exact impact of their advice. 4. Only after proving value, ask for a 15-minute chat for a nuanced problem. 5. Always take notes and ask how you can help them.

### Network Freshening Checklist (How to be prepared for layoffs)
Five specific actions to build and refresh your professional network before you need it

How it works: 1. Identify your 5 most talented friends and former colleagues who work at other interesting companies. Casually ping them to see how things are going.
2. Start participating in a community (e.g. Friends of Lenny's Newsletter Slack, industry-specific communities).
3. Participate in a hackathon (browse Devpost or Eventbrite for options).
4. Go to a local meetup or talk on a subject you're excited about.
5. Reply to that one recruiter who reached out to you a while back, and ask them about the market these days.

### Networking Playbook (Sriram and Aarthi)
Sriram's step-by-step approach to building a professional network through consistent, authentic relationship building

How it works: 1) Meet every peer you don't directly work with — get coffee, no agenda. 2) Ask their life story, what they're working on, how you can help. 3) Ask 'Who else should I talk to?' 4) Meet your manager's peers. 5) Send cold emails to leaders ('I'm new here, let's grab coffee'). 6) Do two coffees per week (just 2 hours). 7) Always follow up after a great meeting. 8) Keep in touch at least once every 6 months to a year. 9) Expect nothing in return. 10) Let it compound over years.

### Newsletter Launch Checklist (500,000)
Steps to get started with a newsletter from scratch based on Lenny's experience

How it works: 1. Identify what you want to get out of your head and crystallize
2. Write it out and share it online (don't overthink it)
3. Post on Substack or similar platform to start collecting email addresses
4. Define who exactly your audience is (think of a specific person)
5. Define the concrete job you're doing for them
6. Find the Venn diagram of your energy + real-life experience + audience pull
7. Pick a sustainable cadence (weekly is a good starting point)
8. Share your cadence publicly to create accountability
9. Aim first for 1,000 people who think it's the best newsletter in the world
10. Spend almost zero time on non-writing activities (design, branding, strategy)
11. Focus all time on making the writing better
12. Cut 30-50% of words in every piece

### Path Expert Conversation (Paul Millerd)
A structured outreach method for learning from people who are further along a similar unconventional path.

How it works: Steps: (1) Identify someone 'a few years ahead of you on a similar path.' (2) Send a thoughtful message: 'You're on a path a few years ahead of me. I'm really curious about this. I don't have people in my life that have done things like this. Can I pick your brain?' (3) If they can't do a call, offer: 'Can I send you a list of questions?' Most people will agree, especially to written questions. Writers particularly love this format because it helps them generate new ideas.

### Touch the Back Wall (Networking Rule) (Ada Chen Rekhi)
A physical forcing function to overcome the awkwardness of networking events.

How it works: Rule: Go to an event, count out exactly 10 business cards, hand out all 10 to new people, physically touch the back wall of the venue, and only then are you allowed to leave.

### Script: Asking a Stranger via Email (On asking for help (even when you really don’t want to))
Example of reaching out to someone you've never met who has written about a relevant topic, building connection before making the ask.

How it works: "Hi, Tomas.

I read your post about saying 'no' more, and it sparked a whole chain of events for me: I realized how much I've been people-pleasing, and it's exhausting, but I'm so grateful for the tools you shared because I do feel some relief too [why them].

I'm wrestling with one piece that I'm hoping you can help with [signpost]: I feel a profound guilt after I say no, and I ruminate on it for days after.

Do you have 1-2 tips you can share via email about how you navigate the aftermath?

Or I'd be happy to pay for a session with you to talk more about it live if you're open to sharing your calendar link [opt-in]."

Note: Without relational connection, create one by sharing about yourself and the impact of their work. Make the ask hyper-specific and start small. Grow the ask once you're on a call.
