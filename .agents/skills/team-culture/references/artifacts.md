# Building High-Performing Team Culture - Frameworks, Templates & Checklists

*90 artifacts extracted from Lenny's Podcast and Newsletter*

## Frameworks

### 3 Traits of an Indistractable Workplace (Nir Eyal)
Criteria for building a company culture that supports deep work and minimizes unnecessary distraction.

How it works: 1. Psychological safety (employees can raise issues without fear). 2. A forum to talk about the problem (e.g., a dedicated Slack channel for feedback). 3. Management exemplifies the behavior (leaders disconnect after hours).

### 3x3 Focus Grid (Evan LaPointe)
A matrix for mapping and intentionally shifting a team's cognitive state.

How it works: Rows represent brain waves: Alpha (daydreaming), Beta (productivity/execution), Gamma (deep focus/learning). Columns represent brain systems: Safety, Reward, Purpose. Most teams are stuck in Safety-Beta or Reward-Beta and need to shift into Purpose-Alpha or Purpose-Gamma.

### Airbnb's Three Ingredients of Strong Culture (What Seven Years at Airbnb Taught Me About Building a Business)
A framework for building company culture with three key components.

How it works: Three key ingredients: 1) Founders obsessed with culture — influences first hires (who co-create culture) and values modeled (knowingly or unknowingly). 2) A strong sense of self — codified core values created by a small task force ~3 years in, used in: measuring success (are we achieving our mission?), hiring (core values interview group vets all candidates), evaluating performance (baked into peer-review process), and evaluating large deals. Everyone should be able to recount values verbatim. 3) Rituals — regular, even silly rituals (e.g., Cookie time Tuesdays, new hire tea time, hosted bar, human tunnels, fun facts) that create space for employees to strengthen bonds. Don't overthink; experiment and see what sticks.

### Always Day One (Ray Cao)
Maintaining startup mentality regardless of company size — staying hungry for growth, keeping flat communication, and avoiding the cultural drift that happens when companies mature

How it works: Key elements: (1) Maintain startup hunger regardless of market success. (2) Keep communication flat — anyone can message co-founders or senior leadership. (3) Actively resist cultural drift as new employees join. (4) Encourage sharing and engagement at all levels. Inspired by Amazon's Day 1 philosophy but implemented differently at TikTok.

### BB Time (Brené Brown Vulnerability Segment) (Ideal sprint length, designer vs. PM roles, running PM team meetings, running post-mortems, best product/executive coaches, and much more)
A meeting segment where team members vulnerably share what feels hard, fostering psychological safety and early blocker identification

How it works: What it is: A 5-10 minute segment in PM team meetings dedicated to discussing 'What feels hard (harder than it should be)?'

How it works:
- Budget 5-10 minutes in the weekly PM staff meeting
- Chatham House Rules: share hardships without worrying about negative reactions
- Spin off into separate threads if discussion balloons beyond time budget
- Must be modeled by the team lead first — otherwise it's 'just on this side of too weird'
- Start informal when team is small, make intentional as team grows

Topics include:
- Troubleshooting communications with other teams
- Tearing down a PRD that has gotten 'stuck'
- Trying on versions of a Vision/Strategy

Observed outcomes:
1. Blockers identified early and loudly — decreased wasted cycles, better approaches found earlier
2. Stronger team self-worth/self-esteem — fights the Spotlight Effect where you think you're the only one struggling
3. Increased esprit de corps — spontaneous brainstorming and mentorship (not just senior to junior, but people with differently 'spikey radar charts' mentoring each other)

Related concepts: Ravi Mehta's PM Roles radar chart (ravi-mehta.com/product-manager-roles/), Rands' 'Critically Fresh Information' signal network

### Compounding + Power Law + Entropy (Matt MacInnis)
A mental model for understanding why extreme effort is required for success.

How it works: Power laws dictate that top performers get 100x rewards, not 10x. Entropy dictates that systems naturally decay. Therefore, leaders must constantly inject energy to fight entropy and stay on the extreme right of the power law curve.

### Context, No Control (Ray Cao)
TikTok's core operating principle where employees are given full business context and encouraged to think like business owners across the whole business problem, while collaborating (not stepping on toes) when taking action. Paired with 'proactive thinking, reactive doing.'

How it works: Two components: (1) Context: Give everyone full visibility into the business, not just their piece. Like Legos — you need to see the full picture to build the right thing. (2) No Control: Don't cap people into boxes. Encourage them to think beyond their job description. Paired principle: Proactive thinking (think broadly about problems) + Reactive doing (collaborate with owners when acting). Anti-pattern: Too many IOs, people protecting their own work, delivering in silos without landing with cross-functional partners.

### Culture as a Product (Dharmesh Shah)
A mental model for building and scaling company culture.

How it works: Treat culture as the product you build for your employees. 1. Survey your 'customers' (employees) using an internal NPS. 2. Identify 'bugs' in the culture. 3. Hold an 'All-Minds' meeting to publicly review the bugs. 4. Commit to fixing certain bugs, and explicitly state which bugs are 'working as designed' and won't be changed. 5. Include aspirational features in your Culture Code deck, noting them as 'not true yet' so the team can build toward them.

### Cycle 1 vs. Cycle 2 Retention Framework (Three myths about sabbatical programs)
A framework for designing sabbatical policies based on two distinct retention goals: retaining early-career employees through their first inklings to leave (Cycle 1) vs. retaining employees for the long haul (Cycle 2)

How it works: Cycle 1 — Retaining employees through their first inklings to leave:
- Target: High-performing junior staff (mid-twenties) attracted to pay raises, titles, and novelty
- Design: Shorter sabbatical (~3+ months) within the first ~4 years of employment
- Examples: McKinsey/Bain/BCG offer 'externships' — partially paid multi-month stints at approved nonprofits; Endeavor offers sabbaticals where employees live and work from investee offices in different countries
- Goal: Give permission to grow, learn, and experiment with strings that lure them back

Cycle 2 — Retaining employees for the long haul:
- Target: Employees you want to keep for a decade or more
- Design: Longer sabbatical (e.g., 6 months fully paid) after longer tenure (~7+ years)
- Examples: Brighton Jones offers fully-paid 6-month sabbatical after 10 years; US Treasury offers extended leave without pay (LWOP) after 7 years with benefits and tenure clock maintained
- Goal: Reward long-standing relationships and track records, signal commitment to employee wellbeing

Bonus consideration: Sometimes turnover is the goal — Summit Partners created a sabbatical policy to give founding/zombie partners an opportunity to step away. All three left, and the policy now serves as a recruiting tool.

### Debate, Decide, Unite (Dharmesh Shah)
A cultural framework for decision making, adapted from Amazon's 'Disagree and Commit'.

How it works: 1. Debate: Openly discuss all options and variables. 2. Decide: A single designated Directly Responsible Individual (DRI) makes the call. 3. Unite: The entire team comes together to support the decision, framing it positively rather than just begrudgingly committing.

### Definitions / Magical Colander Framework (On being funny at work)
Technique for reducing any product, person, or concept to a single defining attribute that is memorable and potentially amusing

How it works: Mental model: Imagine a magical colander that can sift anything. Take an object, idea, or person and sift them through it to extract their single most amusing essential truth.

Examples:
- iPod = '1,000 songs in your pocket' (Steve Jobs — effective but not funny)
- Chair = 'an antigravitational device designed to keep your ass suspended in midair'
- Puppy (with small children) = 'a video game without a screen—a means of occupying your little monster's attention so you can poop in peace'
- Airplane = 'a time machine, transporting you long distances in short windows of time'

Competitor application:
- 'Elon Musk is the smartest, richest, neediest 14-year-old boy on the planet.'
- 'A Volvo is the perfect vehicle for someone who values safety over sexual partners.'
- 'Fiji water is a rectangular prismatic means of identifying yourself as a douchebag.'

### Disagree and Commit (Autonomy vs. direction - Issue 35)
Amazon's decision-making framework where leaders express disagreement but fully commit to the team's direction to maintain speed

How it works: Jeff Bezos quote: 'I disagree and commit all the time. We recently greenlit a particular Amazon Studios original. I told the team my view: debatable whether it would be interesting enough, complicated to produce, the business terms aren't that good, and we have lots of other opportunities. They had a completely different opinion and wanted to go ahead. I wrote back right away with "I disagree and commit and hope it becomes the most watched thing we've ever made." Consider how much slower this decision cycle would have been if the team had actually had to convince me rather than simply get my commitment.'

Key principle: The team doesn't need to convince the leader; they need the leader's commitment. This dramatically speeds up the decision cycle.

### Draw the Owl (Camille Hearst)
A mental model and company value (from Twilio) about taking action and figuring things out without a complete step-by-step guide.

How it works: Based on the meme: Step 1: Draw some circles. Step 2: Draw the rest of the f***ing owl. Used to encourage teams to just start building and figure it out along the way when a clear path isn't visible.

### Employee Needs Pyramid (Peak Model) (Chip Conley)
Maslow's hierarchy of needs adapted for employee motivation and retention.

How it works: Base level: Money/Compensation. Middle level: Recognition. Top level: Meaning. (Different industries weigh the base differently, but differentiation happens at the top).

### Gell-Mann Amnesia (Boz)
A cognitive bias where you trust media reports on unknown topics despite knowing they are completely wrong about topics you are an expert in.

How it works: Used to insulate teams from external criticism and stock market swings by reminding them that they know more about the reality of the company than the external critics do.

### Habit Formation Loop (Hilary Gridley)
A behavioral psychology approach to driving team behavior change.

How it works: Three pillars: 1. Consistency (daily actions). 2. Reduce Friction (start with easy, non-work tasks). 3. Reward Loop (must be powerful, immediate, and emotional).

### Heart vs. Hands vs. Head Framework for PM Culture (A comprehensive survey of Product Management)
A three-dimensional framework for categorizing how companies prioritize PM attributes: Heart (empathy, culture), Hands (execution), Head (intelligence)

How it works: Three dimensions: Heart = empathy, culture. Hands = execution. Head = intelligence. Companies are asked to stack rank the importance of each for PMs. Heart-focused companies: Asana, Spotify, WhatsApp. Hands-focused companies: Flipkart, Okta, PayPal, Quora, Tesla, Wayfair, Yelp. Head-focused companies: Coinbase, Uber, YouTube, Zynga. This framework can be used to assess PM culture fit or to define what kind of PM culture you want to build.

### Inverted Jobs-to-be-Done (What working at Figma taught me about customer obsession)
A reframing of the JTBD framework where instead of identifying the user's job to make your product better, you make the user's job YOUR job as an employee, and the product is just one way to fulfill it.

How it works: Traditional JTBD: Identify user job (e.g., 'manage a design system') → Use it as a lens to improve your product. Inverted JTBD: Your job as an employee IS to help people do great design → Software is just one tool to fulfill that job → Other tools: direct answers, community building, connecting designers to each other. Key insight: Think about your user's higher-level goals in layers. Why do they want to accomplish a task? And why do they want to do that? Consider all levels of user goals when balancing feature investments.

### Merchandising Progress (Blacked-Out Windows Analogy) (Scott Belsky)
Product leaders are driving their teams cross-country with blacked-out windows — if the team doesn't know about progress, they go stir-crazy. Leaders must create micro-milestones and narrate progress to sustain motivation.

How it works: Problem: Biologically, humans need constant rewards to stick with something (life expectancy was 25 years old 100+ years ago). Building products takes many years. Solution: 1) Short-circuit the reward system with micro-goals and milestones mutually agreed upon. 2) Celebrate these even though they don't matter in the grand scheme. 3) Be the steward of the narrative — tell the team when traffic is clearing, when state lines are crossed. Research basis: progress begets progress; progress is a source of motivation.

### Might Be Wrong But Not Confused (Tomer Cohen)
A leadership philosophy with two pillars: clarity of thought (getting aligned on the problem, having principled opinions, distinguishing disagreement from misunderstanding) and clarity of execution (ensuring priorities are reflected in actual resourcing and talent allocation)

How it works: Two pillars: 1) Clarity of Thought: Define the problem with extreme specificity, form opinions based on first principles with real tradeoffs, distinguish disagreement from misunderstanding (ask: 'Are you disagreeing or misunderstanding?'). 2) Clarity of Execution: Ensure stated priorities match actual resource allocation, put top talent on top priorities, verify that decisions actually manifest in the organization.

### Momentum Reflexivity in Startups (Ryan Hoover)
The concept that momentum in startups is self-reinforcing in both directions—high momentum breeds more high momentum, and low momentum creates a downward spiral.

How it works: High momentum (shipping, performing well) creates energy that motivates the whole team to do the same. Low momentum (not shipping, underperforming) leads others to feel less motivated. Momentum is reflexive and arguably one of the most important dynamics in early-stage startups. Related to Mark Andreessen's concept of companies becoming centers of gravity (or losing it).

### Most Charitable Interpretation (Patrick Campbell)
A behavioral framework for handling internal conflict by assuming the best possible intent behind a colleague's actions or words.

How it works: When confronted with a potentially offensive or annoying comment, employees are trained to assume the person didn't know better or meant no harm, and address it calmly rather than escalating to HR.

### Most Generous Interpretation (MGI) (Dr. Becky Kennedy)
A mental model for reframing negative assumptions about someone's behavior to maintain a collaborative relationship.

How it works: When frustrated by someone's behavior, ask yourself: 'What is the most generous interpretation of why they are doing this?' This shifts you from judgment to curiosity and helps you formulate a supportive intervention rather than a punitive one.

### Nubank's 5 Core Values (Jag Duggal)
A set of cultural tenets that guide employee behavior and product decisions.

How it works: 1. We want our customers to love us fanatically. 2. We are hungry and we challenge the status quo. 3. We build strong and diverse teams. 4. We pursue smart efficiency. 5. We think and act like owners, not renters (do what is right even when no one is telling us to do it).

### Output Over Performative Hours (Tom Conrad)
A culture principle that celebrates productive output regardless of hours worked rather than rewarding visible presence and late nights

How it works: Origin story: At Apple, Tom worked 14-hour days with much unproductive time and got celebrated. A female colleague showed up at 8:30, shipped more code, left on time, and got less recognition. At Pandora, Tom built culture around: 1) Don't reward performative contribution, 2) Celebrate the work itself, not the hours, 3) If you can do great work and go home and have other things in your life, that's the ideal, 4) Create space for those who naturally want to work long hours but don't make it the expectation. Key nuance: 22-year-olds may want to work through the night and that should be allowed too.

### Product Values as Decision-Making Guide (This Week #11: What should new PMs over-index on, and empowering product in a sales-driven org)
Using product values (analogous to company cultural values) to guide product work and provide qualitative success measures alongside metrics

How it works: Concept: Product values provide high-level guidance on how to approach problems and serve as a subjective, qualitative measure of success to complement metrics goals.

Outschool's product values examples:
- Stand for learners
- Embrace variety
- Promote relationships
- Personalize

Usage: Apply them like cultural values guide a company — they help teams make consistent decisions about product direction and evaluate whether solutions align with the product's identity.

### Resourcefulness vs. Resources (Scott Belsky)
A mental model for resource-constrained environments: if resources are carbs, resourcefulness is muscle. Resources give temporary energy; resourcefulness builds lasting strength and intuition.

How it works: Instead of: throwing money at servers, hiring more headcount for scaling problems. Do: refactor the database, refactor how the team handles requests, leverage AI for efficiency. Key insight: the last 7-10 years trained teams to throw resources at problems; now constraint forces refactoring. The muscle of resourcefulness stays with you and improves performance over time.

### Reverse Anna Karenina Principle (John Cutler)
A mental model for understanding team performance and dysfunction.

How it works: Dysfunctional companies are all the same (easy to identify anti-patterns), but high-performing companies can be vastly different in how they achieve success.

### Seven Product Principles for Building Great Products (How Ramp builds product)
Ramp's core product philosophy covering speed, empowerment, selling, first principles, bets, simplicity, and culture

How it works: 1. Speed is a competitive advantage. High-velocity decision-making, keeping things simple, reducing team sizes and dependencies, extreme focus and work ethic. If shipping speed is extremely high, the cost of being wrong is much lower.

2. Empower the folks closest to the problem to own the decision. Give teams resources, hold them accountable. When the lowest level can make the call: better decisions, higher velocity, culture of ownership.

3. Sell your first customer. PMs must find, pitch, and close the first 5-10 customers for their products. Forces working with customers from the start. If you can't find 10 customers, you haven't built something people want. PMs need to learn to sell — the company vision, the product, new candidates.

4. Think from first principles. Three situations where deep diving matters:
   a. A critical user experience depends on it (e.g. payments PM understanding ACH file lines)
   b. A high-cost business decision depends on it (e.g. reading legal statutes yourself)
   c. An important business process needs to be defined for scale (e.g. exhaustive hiring guides)

5. Take bets with asymmetric upside. If 98% chance of failure and 2% chance of massive benefit, take the bet. Applies to hiring, investors, products, vendor selection.

6. Simplify, simplify, simplify! Build complex products that are simple at the surface. Sweat every pixel, cut complexity. Why two clicks when you need one? Better yet, no clicks.

7. Taking risks demands humor, kindness, and gratitude. Publish assumptions, take accountability, don't take yourself too seriously, stay kind, express gratitude, own mistakes.

### Shared Experiences Circle (Jerry Colonna)
A format for peer groups to drop the facade of constant success and build resilience through vulnerability.

How it works: Gather a group of peers. Ask deep questions like: 'Who would you be without the story of who you are?' and 'What is it that you wish people knew about you but you're too afraid to tell them?' The key rule is to hold space without trying to fix each other.

### Snowflake's Eight Core Values (How Snowflake builds product)
The company values used to evaluate candidates and guide decision-making, with 'Put Customers First' as the deliberate first value

How it works: Eight core values published at snowflake.com/company. The first value is 'Put Customers First' — Frank Slootman shared this is the first value for a reason. Values are used as evaluation criteria in PM interviews. When prioritization decisions need to be made, customer value (making customer experience better) is the primary lens.

### Switch Framework (Rider, Elephant, Path) (Shishir Mehrotra)
Chip and Dan Heath's behavior change framework applied to organizational rituals: direct the rider (tell people), motivate the elephant (name/brand things), shape the path (templatize).

How it works: Three levers for change: 1) Direct the rider = Tell people what to do. For rituals: teach employees in onboarding (Bing's rule: 'known by first Friday'). 2) Motivate the elephant = Give things emotional resonance. For rituals: give it a name (Bing's rule: 'named'). Names let people anchor identity and brag. 3) Shape the path = Make the desired behavior the default/easy path. For rituals: templatize (Bing's rule: 'templated'). Book: Switch by Chip and Dan Heath, subtitle 'How to Change Things When Change Is Hard'.

### Team Autonomy / Mini-PM Framework (Product manager is an unfair role. So work unfairly.)
A set of principles and micro-interactions for building a self-reliant product team that operates without the PM being a bottleneck.

How it works: Principles:
1. Tell new teams: 'Incredible products have been built without a PM' and 'The most impactful, money-printing initiatives my teams have ever shipped were never my ideas.'
2. When people ask you for decisions, remind them they are the expert. Ask: 'What context do you need from me to make your recommendation?'
3. Shower teammates with positivity when they do something that's technically your job (e.g., QA doing data analysis, support agent proposing a feature, designer going right to a stakeholder, data analyst identifying a problem to solve, engineer directly interacting with a customer).
4. DMs are the devil: Anytime somebody sends you a DM (and it's not confidential), ask them to repost in a public channel.
5. Use word choices that frame product as a team, not a role.

Quote from Max Eulenstein: 'Turn everyone into a mini-PM. That's the way that you maximize leverage from your entire team. Don't view your cross-functional peers as stepping on your toes as a PM.'

### Tech Worker Sentiment Framework: 8 Key Dimensions (How tech workers really feel about work right now)
A comprehensive framework for measuring and understanding tech worker sentiment across 8 key dimensions, each revealing different aspects of workplace health

How it works: The 8 dimensions measured in the survey:
1. Career Outlook - Optimism/pessimism about career future and job function future (especially re: AI)
2. Role Satisfaction - Current job enjoyment levels
3. Burnout - Degree of burnout from 'not at all' to 'completely'
4. Quitting Intentions - Commitment to current role vs. actively looking
5. Career Path Clarity - How clear workers are about skills and career development paths to reach professional goals
6. Leadership/Manager Effectiveness - Rating of direct manager effectiveness
7. Work Setup Impact - How work arrangement (remote/hybrid/in-office) impacts feelings about job
8. Engagement & Belonging - Interest, involvement, connection to work and sense of belonging in organization

Additional segmentation dimensions: Company size, role type, experience level, gender, work arrangement, employment type (full-time vs self-employed), industry sector

### The 'Hot Seat' Game (Mihika Kapoor)
A team-building icebreaker where one person gets 2 minutes on the clock to answer any questions from the team.

How it works: Put 2 minutes on the clock; the team can ask anything; the person in the hot seat can decline to answer. Great for offsites and onboarding new members.

### The Mexican Standoff of Tech Roles (Marc Andreessen)
A mental model for understanding the current dynamic between Product Managers, Designers, and Engineers.

How it works: Each role believes they no longer need the other two because they can use AI to do those tasks. The reality is that the most successful people will use AI to become proficient in all three, aiming their 'guns' up the org chart at management.

### The Neurochemistry of Laughter (On being funny at work)
Scientific framework for why humor works in business — laughing swaps cortisol with three beneficial chemicals

How it works: When someone laughs, cortisol (stress hormone) is replaced by three chemicals:
1. Dopamine — enhances learning, motivation, and attention
2. Oxytocin — the 'empathy hormone' that creates feelings of connection and relatability to the person producing the effect
3. Endorphins — trigger feelings of pleasure

Additional benefits: stress relief, reduced anxiety, sense of safety, improved mood.

Implication for leaders and presenters: Making your audience laugh literally primes their brains to be more receptive, trusting, and engaged with your message.

### Three Brain Systems (Evan LaPointe)
A mental model for understanding the underlying motivations driving human behavior at work.

How it works: 1. Safety System: Seeks to restore standing and reduce threat/anxiety. 2. Reward System: Transactional pursuit of benefits. 3. Purpose System: Activated by understanding the impact of actions on other people.

### Three Criteria for Good Values (Will Larson)
A litmus test for evaluating whether company/team values will actually be useful: they must be honest, applicable, and reversible.

How it works: 1) Honest: You actually do what the value claims. If you say 'users first' but decisions clearly aren't users-first, the value undermines trust. 2) Applicable: The value helps people make real decisions. Example: Stripe's 'optimize globally' tells you to sacrifice local team benefit for org-wide benefit. Uber's implicit 'optimize for your team' tells you the opposite. Both are applicable. 3) Reversible: A reasonable company could choose the opposite. 'We build good software' fails because no one would choose the opposite. 'We're a family' vs Netflix's 'we're a sports team' passes. If a value isn't reversible, it's just an identity statement that doesn't help anyone make decisions.

### Three Myths of Corporate Sabbatical Programs (Three myths about sabbatical programs)
A myth-busting framework that addresses the three most common objections leaders have to sabbatical programs, each paired with research findings and case studies

How it works: Myth #1: Everyone will leave!
- Reality: 80% of employees return from sabbaticals more loyal, more creative, and more personally fulfilled
- Context: Average tenure at top tech companies is less than 3 years; by year 5 sabbatical eligibility you've nearly doubled expected seniority
- Uber cautionary tale: Backtracked on sabbatical policy due to departures, but this was during worst period of company culture — sabbatical was a canary in the coal mine, not the cause

Myth #2: The company will fall apart without me!
- Reality: Sabbaticals reveal hidden organizational dependencies and develop emerging leaders
- Key insight: 'Wouldn't you rather proactively root out your company's skeletons?'
- Principle: Successfully offloading and onboarding responsibilities should be honed like a competitive advantage

Myth #3: I don't need a sabbatical — I'm fine!
- Reality: Functional workaholism (like functional alcoholism) keeps you productive while depleting personal resources — you don't realize it until you step away
- Key finding: Takes 6-8 weeks to fully disconnect from work life
- Contagion effect: The 'work devotion norm' spreads top-down; employees are more likely to take leave if their managers do
- Studies show managers who took family leave experienced fewer promotions and smaller salary increases

### Three Rules of Golden Rituals (Bing Gordon's Framework) (Shishir Mehrotra)
A test for whether a company ritual qualifies as a 'golden ritual' that defines culture, with three criteria that map to the Switch framework for behavior change.

How it works: Three rules: 1) Named - the ritual has a specific name (maps to 'motivate the elephant' in Switch). 2) Every employee knows it by their first Friday (maps to 'direct the rider'). 3) Templated - there's a standardized way to do it (maps to 'shape the path'). Examples: Amazon has six-pagers, Google has OKRs, Salesforce has V2MOM, Coda has Dory/Pulse.

### Trust Battery (Farhan Thawar)
A metaphor for interpersonal trust that can deplete or charge over time based on interactions.

How it works: Start people at 50% (or 100% for high-performers) and let their actions charge or deplete the battery. Use IRL events to recharge the battery for remote teams.

### Values Definition Workshop (Barbra Gago)
A structured team exercise for defining company values without getting bogged down in committee writing.

How it works: 1. Brain dump ideas onto a shared board (e.g., Miro). 2. Group ideas into themes. 3. Vote on the most important themes to create a shortlist. 4. Assign a single strong writer to draft the actual definitions and operating principles based on the shortlist, rather than writing as a group.

### Values vs. Obedience Leadership Matrix (Kunal Shah)
A 2x2 matrix based on Indian mythology used to categorize leadership archetypes and explain the success of non-founder CEOs.

How it works: Y-axis: Values (High/Low). X-axis: Obedience (High/Low). Rama = High Values, High Obedience (Sustainer). Krishna = High Values, Low Obedience (Creator/Innovator). Successful CEOs toggle between Rama and Krishna while maintaining the founder's original 'dharma'.

### Work Setup Sentiment Matrix (How tech workers really feel about work right now)
A comparison of how remote, hybrid, and in-office work setups affect different dimensions of worker sentiment

How it works: Metrics that are THE SAME across all work setups:
- Job enjoyment
- Burnout levels
- Quitting intentions
- Engagement levels
- Sense of belonging

Metrics that DIFFER by work setup:
- Direct job satisfaction: Hybrid (mostly from home) is 21% higher than in-office
- Career optimism: In-office workers slightly more optimistic about career future
- AI resilience optimism: In-office workers slightly more optimistic about job function vs AI

Conclusions:
1. Hybrid workers are the happiest overall
2. Remote workers are surprisingly happy
3. In-office workers show hidden frustrations when asked directly
4. Optimal balance: remote work with some in-person interaction

Confounding variables to watch: Age (remote skews older), company size (hybrid more common at larger cos), role distribution

## Templates

### Life Story Friday Ritual (How Notion builds product)
A weekly team-building ritual where one team member shares their personal life story over Friday lunch, serving as a 'working with me' guide through personal narrative

How it works: Origin: Ivan Zhao was asked to write a 'how to work with me' handbook (inspired by Claire Johnson's handbook in Elad Gil's High Growth Handbook). Instead of sharing the doc, he told his life story for 45 minutes—family, childhood interests, passions, Notion's early days. Team realized the life story WAS the handbook.

Format:
- **When**: Every Friday
- **Setting**: Over lunch
- **Who**: One person per week
- **Content**: Personal life story — how they got to where they are
- **Duration**: ~45 minutes (based on Ivan's original)
- **Purpose**: Helps colleagues understand how to work with each person by understanding their background, motivations, and journey

Key insight: A personal narrative can be more effective than a formal 'working with me' document because it provides the context and reasoning behind someone's working style.

### Miro Connect (Biweekly Demo Event) (Varun Parmar)
A biweekly informal event where product teams set up at tables and demo work in progress, creating serendipitous collaboration opportunities

How it works: Format: Every other Friday, 4:00-7:00/8:00 PM (sometimes later). Setup: Teams at tables in the office, like a pitch event or science fair. Atmosphere: Casual—drinks, light music, people walk table to table. Content: Each team shows work in progress, upcoming features, problems they're solving. Purpose: Cross-team awareness, serendipitous problem-solving, building shared context. Currently works best in-person; hard to replicate over video conferencing. A/V bridge is available but most interactions are live.

### PXD API (How to Work with Me Doc) (Peter Deng)
A personal operating manual Peter has carried across companies that outlines five attributes of people who are most successful working with him, including the six-month autonomy principle.

How it works: Contains five attributes/principles for working with Peter. Known contents include: the autonomy principle ('In six months, you're telling me what to do, not the other way around'), growth mindset expectations, and direct/unsugared feedback norms. Peter mentioned potentially open-sourcing this document.

### Personal Blueprint (Luc Levesque)
A document shared with new team members outlining a leader's personal quirks and working style to fast-track alignment.

How it works: A simple list of personal quirks, communication preferences, and working styles given to new hires to avoid months of awkward calibration.

### Personal User Manual ('How to work with Keith' doc) (Keith Yandell)

### 5 Highest-Leverage Moves for Companies to Improve Tech Worker Sentiment (How tech workers really feel about work right now)
Company-level strategic actions backed by the survey data to improve retention, engagement, and satisfaction

How it works: 1. Invest in leadership development - Only 26.6% rate managers as highly effective. Great leadership correlates with 48% more engagement, 63% more belonging, 31% less burnout, 62% more job enjoyment. Workers with ineffective leaders are 4.3x more likely to leave.
2. Inspire and motivate 'founder mode' for everyone - Founders showed the most positive sentiment across nearly every metric. Enable teams to have maximal autonomy, ownership, and purpose. Founders are 3-4x more optimistic, 2x more engaged, almost 3x happier than everyone else.
3. Embrace flexible work - Core job metrics don't significantly differ between setups, but hybrid arrangements are viewed most positively (21% higher sentiment). Focus on creating connection regardless of physical location.
4. Think small, even when you're big - Company size is one of the strongest predictors of positive sentiment (significant across 9 of 11 metrics). Create tight-knit teams and communities within large orgs to combat 'big-company blues.'
5. Target mid-career support - Clear U-shaped sentiment pattern. Mid-career workers (7-14 years) struggle most with career clarity, show higher burnout and lower enjoyment. They need attention before they head for the exit.

### Sabbatical Policy Action Items by Myth (Three myths about sabbatical programs)
Specific action items for leaders organized by each myth they need to overcome when implementing sabbatical programs

How it works: Action Items for Myth #1 (Everyone will leave):
1. Determine your priorities and design your sabbatical policy(ies) accordingly:
   - To retain staff through Cycle 1: design shorter sabbatical (~3+ months) within first ~4 years of employment
   - To attract and retain staff for a decade+ (Cycle 2): aim for longer cycles between eligibility (~7+ years)
2. Think critically about what message your sabbatical policy sends and who will actually take it:
   - If unpaid, may not be accessible to lower-earners or those with many dependents
   - Sends a strong signal about who the policy was designed for

Action Items for Myth #2 (Company will fall apart):
1. If you're a leader: introduce (or find a board advocate for) a sabbatical policy for your leadership team
2. Balance needs of direct reports with importance of leading by example in taking time off
3. Make a plan to cycle leadership out on sabbatical with:
   - Enough buffer time to properly prepare the organization
   - Integration time upon return to digest learnings before returning to business-as-usual

Action Items for Myth #3 (I don't need one):
1. Design the policy: provide benefits (salary/stipend + insurance), make it long enough (ideally 3+ months)
2. Before AND after: designate off-ramping responsibilities 90+ days in advance, organize re-integration
3. During: provide resources (benefits + coaching), enforce disconnection

### Sabbatical Program Design Best Practices (Three myths about sabbatical programs)
Three-phase best practices for designing an effective corporate sabbatical program covering policy design, pre/post sabbatical logistics, and during-sabbatical support

How it works: 1. The Design of the Policy:
   - Provide benefits (salary/stipend + insurance)
   - Make sure it's long enough (ideally at least 3 months)
   - If unpaid, consider accessibility for lower-earners and those with dependents — the policy sends a signal about who it was designed for
   - Determine your priorities: Cycle 1 retention (shorter sabbatical ~3+ months within first ~4 years) vs. Cycle 2 retention (longer cycles ~7+ years between eligibility)

2. Before AND After Sabbatical:
   - Designate off-ramping responsibilities 90+ days in advance
   - Organize re-integration into company/team upon return
   - Especially important if responsibilities have changed during absence
   - For leadership: build in buffer time to properly prepare the organization
   - Integration time upon return to digest learnings before returning to business-as-usual
   - Debrief performance of the organization during leader's absence (as ALA did with board)

3. During Sabbatical:
   - Provide resources — benefits and coaching
   - Enforce disconnection from work

### Seven Palantir Principles for Disrupting an Industry (The unconventional Palantir principles that catalyzed a generation of startups)
A complete checklist of unconventional principles distilled from Palantir's approach to building world-changing technology companies.

How it works: 1. Forward-deploy your engineering — Embed engineers in customer environments, do their actual work, stay for months not minutes
2. Hire the absolute greatest people who exist — Target the literal #1 person on Earth for each critical discipline, validate through community reputation
3. Hire no salespeople — Have engineers and domain experts do sales; delay professional salespeople as long as possible
4. Experiment with live products — Use working code as the iteration unit instead of designs; operate in daily build-share cycles (requires exceptional engineers)
5. Build features that magnify value over time — Design features so each user's actions increase value for all subsequent users through data assets and network effects

7. Solve the hardest problems first — Target problems that appear in news/annual reports; let competitors handle the easy ones

### Company Sabbatical Program Examples (Three myths about sabbatical programs)
Collection of real company sabbatical program designs across industries, showing different approaches to policy structure

How it works: 1. McKinsey/Bain/BCG (Consulting): 'Externships' — partially paid multi-month stints at approved nonprofits. Goal: extend average tenure of newly minted graduates who typically churn after a few years for MBA programs.

2. Endeavor (Social impact investment): Employees get 'sabbaticals' living and working from investee offices in a different country. Still working near capacity but feels like fresh air and growth opportunity.

3. Brighton Jones (Investment advisory): Fully-paid 6-month sabbatical after 10 years of employment. CEO Jon Jones wants to reward long-standing client relationships and track records, not retain people a couple years out of college.

4. US Treasury (Government): Extended leave without pay (LWOP) after 7 years of service. Unpaid but employees retain benefits and tenure clock (crucial for retirement and pay raises).

5. Summit Partners (Venture Capital): Established sabbatical policy specifically to give founding/zombie partners opportunity to step away. All three founding partners left after their sabbaticals. Policy remains as recruiting tool.

6. Uber (Tech): Backtracked on sabbatical policy when too many people didn't come back. However, this was during darkest months of Travis K's reign with all-time high turnover — sabbatical was symptom, not cause.

7. Citi (Financial services): Offering workers a 12-week sabbatical plus extra vacation days.
