# Defining Your ICP - Frameworks, Templates & Checklists

## Frameworks

### 9 ICP Attribute Categories (How to identify your ideal customer profile (ICP))
A comprehensive list of the 9 dimensions along which you can define your ideal customer profile, with examples for each.

How it works: Pick the 3 most unique and important from these 9 categories:

1. Company size — e.g. 1,000-5,000 employees, 2-5 people, 20-60 people
2. Job title — e.g. engineering manager, social media manager, CTO, head of data
3. Pain point you're solving — e.g. compliance, internal transparency, can't close books on time
4. Company's unique way of working — e.g. design-driven, operationally heavy, values internal transparency
5. Specific tech used — e.g. data warehouse, GitLab, GitHub, dbt, Node.js
6. Type of business — e.g. B2B SaaS, e-commerce, software companies
7. Price point — e.g. sells software that's $10k ARR, $1K-$100K deal sizes
8. Geo — e.g. urban centers, LatAm, California, U.S.
9. A unique place the user spends time — e.g. Node.js community, NPM ecosystem, dbt community

Key insight: Every successful B2B company in the research landed on at least 3 attributes. Some had more (Gusto had 6), but none had fewer.

### AI Adoption Curve Segmentation (Embracers vs. Skeptics) (Counterintuitive advice for building AI products)
A framework for segmenting AI product users by attitude toward AI technology rather than just functional needs, with corresponding testing methodology

How it works: Key insight: The most meaningful segmentation for AI products depends on attitudes toward technology itself, not just functional needs. Two segments:

1. AI Embracers: Quick adopters driven by novelty. Risk: phantom PMF with steep churn cliff as novelty wears off.
2. AI Skeptics: Reject AI products that meet their needs because they don't trust AI. Opportunity: With right messaging and onboarding, can become superusers.

The two groups behave very differently and averaging their engagement creates 'tepid tea'—a product that satisfies no one.

Three-part testing approach for AI products:
1. Longitudinal validation: Test for long enough to understand how engagement changes once novelty wears off.
2. High-touch testing: Stay close enough to users to understand daily attitude changes. Experiment with user Slack groups instead of traditional surveys and 1:1 interviews.
3. Attitudinal segmentation: Include both AI embracers and AI skeptics in early testing groups. Segment them carefully to avoid averaging out their engagement.

### B2B Segmentation and Persona Layering Model (Summary: April Dunford on product positioning, segmentation, and optimizing your sales process)
Framework for distinguishing between market segmentation and buyer personas in B2B, and how they work together

How it works: Layer 1 — Segmentation (account-level):
- Start with firmographics: number of employees, revenue, geography
- Refine with actionable criteria: e.g., 'three-member or more creative agencies that follow a specific firmographic profile, have a budget of 20K, and use Notion'
- The tighter the segment, the more effective the marketing team

Layer 2 — Personas (people-level within the account):
- In enterprise B2B, 5-7 people are involved in buying decisions
- Identify the internal champion: e.g., 'John, head of Sales Ops'
- The champion's job: build consensus and galvanize the deal, including getting buy-in from their boss (the actual check writer)
- The champion is the gatekeeper — if your product is not differentiated, you don't get on their shortlist

Key insight: Segment first (accounts), then layer personas on top (people within accounts). The champion persona is the most critical to understand.

### Concentric Circles of Target Audience (Arielle Jackson)
A model for narrowing down who a product is for.

How it works: Outer circle: Total Addressable Market (TAM). Inner circle 4: Target Audience (who you will outwardly try to acquire for the next 18 months, e.g., 'Tech-savvy dads'). Center dot: Model Persona (a specific, named individual with detailed demographics and psychographics).

### Customers Who Care (How to kickstart and scale a consumer business—Step 2: Identify your super-specific who)
April Dunford's framework for identifying best-fit customers by finding who cares most about your differentiated value.

How it works: Process:
1. Identify your differentiated value (what you do that competitors don't)
2. Recognize there's a wide range of buyers who care about that value
3. Determine which customers care A LOT more than others
4. Identify the characteristics that make them care so much
5. Those characteristics define your best-fit target customers

Test: 'Some prospects will say, Yeah, your stuff is cool, but others will jump out of their chair and yell, That's amazing—I need that right now! You are going for the second group.'

### Four Signs You've Found Your ICP (How to identify your ideal customer profile (ICP))
A framework of four observable signals that indicate you're converging on the right ideal customer profile.

How it works: Four signs you're on the right track with your ICP:

1. INCREASED CONVERSION RATE: Your conversion rate jumps significantly when you target a specific persona. Example: Stytch saw 5-10% conversion with growth product personas vs 40-50% with technical buyers. Retool found outreach to CTOs was much more successful than to ops teams.

2. INCREASED ENTHUSIASM: A specific persona is noticeably more excited, more of a 'fan.' Example: Census noticed the data persona was 'just much more enthused' than biz ops. Canva saw social media managers 'really get excited about it.'

3. DESIRE TO TAKE ACTION NOW: Prospects want to buy/implement immediately, not 'in a few months.' Example: Robinhood told Sprig 'We love it. We'll install tomorrow' and signed a large contract even though the product was early.

4. THE NOD: When you describe the pain point, prospects physically nod in recognition — a knowing 'Yep, that's us too.' Example: Hex's CEO would pitch the problem of screenshotting charts and pasting in Google Docs, and look for that specific nod. If he got it, the rest of the conversation would go really well.

### High-Expectation Customer (HXC) (How to kickstart and scale a consumer business—Step 2: Identify your super-specific who)
Julie Supan's framework for identifying the most discerning person in your target demographic whose expectations, if exceeded, mean you'll meet everyone else's.

How it works: The High-Expectation Customer (HXC) is the most discerning person within your target demographic. Characteristics:
- They acknowledge and enjoy your product for its greatest benefit
- They look things up and research things
- They have ideas for new products/services that save money, gain time, get healthier, or make their team more productive

Key insight: If your product exceeds the HXC's expectations, it can meet everyone else's.

Full guide: https://review.firstround.com/what-i-learned-from-developing-branding-for-airbnb-dropbox-and-thumbtack

### ICP + Persona Definition (Pete Kazanjy)
Two-part framework for defining who to sell to: account-level characteristics (company attributes) and persona-level characteristics (the humans involved in the buying decision).

How it works: Account-level ICP: Industry/type, company size, number of relevant users/team members, technology stack. Example (Amplitude): Makes software products, has 2+ product managers. Persona-level: User persona (who uses the product day-to-day), Technical stakeholder (who evaluates integration), Budget holder (who has purchasing authority). Example (Amplitude): PM = user, Engineering = technical eval, VP Product or CTO = budget. Map all three for your product before starting outbound.

### ICP Expansion Readiness Framework (from Gusto) (How to identify your ideal customer profile (ICP))
A two-part test for knowing when you're ready to expand your ICP beyond the initial narrow definition.

How it works: You're ready to expand your ICP when two conditions are met:

1. You're getting customer love from your current narrow ICP (high satisfaction, retention, enthusiasm)
2. You have sufficient engineering bandwidth to build new features/products for the expanded audience WITHOUT letting go of the previous customer audience

Gusto's expansion sequence: Started with only CA companies with ≤5 salaried employees, no benefits, no deductions, 8-day payroll delay → added hourly employees → added benefits → expanded to additional states → eventually grew to 300,000+ businesses with 1-500 employees in 120+ countries over 11 years.

### ICP Fray Detection and Response (Lessons learned from a startup that didn’t make it)
A decision framework for what to do when you encounter demand outside your defined ICP

How it works: When you encounter demand outside your ICP, you have two options:

OPTION A — VALIDATE AND PIVOT: Treat the new use case as a potential new market segment. Validate it rigorously as its own ICP before committing resources.

OPTION B — STICK TO YOUR GUNS: Reject the customer/use case and stay focused on your defined segment.

DANGER ZONE: Trying to support all new use cases without validating them as distinct market segments. This is 'ICP fray' — it confuses every decision because you no longer know who you're building for.

WARNING: Encountering demand outside your ICP may seem like a good thing, but be very careful. Yes, you may discover a whole new market, but recognize you're allowing your customer focus to fray.

### ICP Specificity Test (Lessons learned from a startup that didn’t make it)
A framework for evaluating whether your ICP is specific enough, based on two criteria: the value of solving the problem is roughly the same across the segment, and the segment can be reached in roughly the same way.

How it works: A quality ICP must be a single market segment defined by two properties:
1. VALUE HOMOGENEITY: The value of solving the problem is roughly the same for everyone in the segment.
2. REACHABILITY: The people in the segment can be reached in roughly the same way.

BAD ICP example: 'People who use Excel' — says nothing about value of their problem or how to reach them.
BETTER ICP example: 'Marketers who use Excel to build content calendars at Fortune 500 companies' — defines the role, the problem, and the company type.

RED FLAG TEST: If your ICP can accommodate wildly different use cases (e.g., a scooter company managing location data AND an HR team managing employee data AND a retailer analyzing distribution), your ICP is too broad.

KEY PRINCIPLE: Every decision is downstream of a clear ICP definition. To know what problem you're solving, you need to know who you're solving it for. On the journey to PMF, products often change quickly, but if the market is changing too, the chances of finding fit drop dramatically.

### Outbound vs. Inbound Signal Quality Framework (How to identify your ideal customer profile (ICP))
A mental model for why outbound sales data produces cleaner ICP signals than warm introductions from investors and network.

How it works: Why outbound signal is purer than inbound/warm intro signal:

Warm intro path (noisy data):
- Ask investor to forward intro email to potential customers
- Some recipients reply, but NOT because of product interest
- Their real motivations: 'I want to meet another founder', 'I'm lonely and want to make friends', social obligation to investor
- This creates 'fake interest' that feels good but muddies the experiment
- If they churn later, you can't tell if the product failed or they were never interested

Outbound path (clean data):
- Cold outreach with no other incentives attached
- Response = genuine interest in the problem/solution
- Non-response = genuine lack of interest
- Conversion data directly reflects ICP-product fit

Recommendation: Pay closest attention to signal from pure outbound when determining your ICP.

### Persona's Anti-Specialization Strategy (How to identify your ideal customer profile (ICP))
A deliberate approach to avoid over-specializing in one vertical by finding diverse customers early and generalizing the product.

How it works: Strategy: Once you have success with one type of customer, DON'T go after more of the same type immediately. Instead:

1. Find success with one customer in a specific space
2. Don't pursue more customers of that same type yet
3. Find another customer of a VERY DIFFERENT type
4. Rewrite/generalize your product so it works for BOTH customer types
5. Repeat for your first 10 customers

Rationale: If you focus on just a narrow ICP and keep specializing, you build your entire platform around them. The process of later branching off into different niches becomes 'borderline impossible without a fair degree of foresight.' This approach builds generality from the start.

Also recommended: Instead of retrofitting your ICP ('this person bought us, let's find more like them'), think through 'what are the common characteristics of people who've bought us (e.g. company size, pain points), and what are our theories about the NEXT set of people we could get?'

### Power User Profiling Method (How to make an impact in your first 90 days)
A step-by-step approach to identifying and profiling power users for PLG strategy optimization

How it works: Steps:
1. Build a working definition of your power users
2. Start with a cohort of your best-retained users
3. Search for trends in their product usage that tell a story
4. Add qualitative research to create a demographic and behavioral profile
5. Even a rough profile is valuable—don't over-optimize for perfection
6. Overshare this profile with marketing and product leaders

Application: The fastest path to better conversion, higher monetization, and stronger retention is focusing acquisition and product optimization on the segment that naturally fits your product best.

Compounding effect: As you run experiments to activate and engage this group, the results compound over time.

### Pressure = Force / Surface Area (Lulu Cheng Meservey)
A physics analogy applied to marketing focus.

How it works: To increase impact (pressure) with limited resources (force), you must decrease the target audience size (surface area). Hyper-target diehards instead of appealing weakly to everyone.

### Psychographic Persona Targeting (Zoelle Egner)
For horizontal products, identify a psychographic profile (e.g., 'tinkerer') that cuts across job titles, then find proxy interests to target

How it works: Problem: Horizontal products can be used by anyone, so generic messaging fails and pure vertical targeting misses opportunities. Solution: 1) Identify a psychographic type (Airtable's was 'tinkerer'—likes new tech, enjoys building with Lego-like blocks). 2) Find proxy interests that cluster with this type (specific podcasts, media, personal development topics). 3) Target Facebook/ad campaigns around those interest clusters. 4) Build lookalike audiences from successful conversions. Note: This specific Facebook targeting no longer works due to platform changes, but the psychographic identification principle applies to any channel.

### Six-Step Consumer Business Launch Framework (How to kickstart and scale a consumer business—Step 2: Identify your super-specific who)
Lenny's end-to-end framework for kickstarting and scaling a consumer business, presented as a sequential six-step process.

How it works: Step 1: INSIGHT — Come up with your idea
Step 2: AUDIENCE — Identify your super-specific who
Step 3: HOOK — Craft your pitch
Step 4: REACH — Find your early adopters by doing things that don't scale
Step 5: RETAIN — Iterate until enough people stick around
Step 6: SCALE — Build your growth engine

Each step builds on the previous. You must nail your audience before crafting your pitch, and nail retention before scaling.

### Super Consumers (Christopher Lochhead)
A customer segmentation model focusing on the small percentage of buyers who drive the majority of profits and cultural influence.

How it works: Identify the 8-10% of buyers responsible for the vast majority of profits. Recognize them as the industry zeitgeist and thought leaders. Target them in native digital communities with educational content about a reframed problem to ignite viral word-of-mouth.

### Technology Adoption Curve for Product Builders (Uri Levine)
Using the adoption curve to understand why product builders (innovators/early adopters) cannot intuitively understand the early majority — and why simplicity is the bridge.

How it works: Groups: 1) Innovators (~2%) — use new things because they're new. 2) Early adopters — try when they see value, leave if no value. 3) Early majority (~33%) — win this group, win the market. Afraid of change. State of mind: 'What's wrong with Excel?' Fear: looking like an idiot. 4) Late majority — only use if forced. Solution for early majority: Simplicity. Remove features people aren't using — they add complexity. Leonardo da Vinci: 'Simplicity is the ultimate sophistication.' Key insight: If you're an innovator/early adopter building the product, you CANNOT understand the early majority without watching them.

### The 'You Act Differently' Segmentation Test (Madhavan Ramanujam)
A mental model to determine if you have a real segmentation strategy.

How it works: If you have a segmentation strategy, your product, sales, marketing, and finance teams must ACT differently for each segment. You must productize differently (e.g., water in a fountain vs. bottle vs. minibar) rather than building one product and just changing the marketing persona.

### Three Reasons to Keep Your ICP Narrow (What to do if your product isn’t taking off)
Three strategic reasons why your initial target market should be almost comically narrow with at least three narrowing characteristics

How it works: 1. Focus: It's hard enough to solve one big problem for one person. Focus all efforts on one use case to have the best chance of solving a problem better than anyone else. Get one company to love your product, then expand. (Geoffrey Moore: 'The goal is for a fledgling start-up to take customers away from an established incumbent by focusing all their power at a single point of attack.')

2. Distribution: It's easier to get people excited if the product feels made for them. It's also easier to reach people with the same interests or problems. Starting niche counterintuitively helps you grow more quickly.

3. Early adopters get your flywheel spinning: Most people aren't early adopters—they wait for social proof. Early adopters love new stuff and tell others. That's how you cross the 'chasm.' (Geoffrey Moore: 'Until I get the fire started somewhere, I shouldn't try to spread my heat.' Sarah Tavel: 'It's a lot easier to boil a thimble than the ocean.')

### Wedge Selection Criteria (Leo Polovets) (Picking a wedge)
Decision framework for intentional wedge selection based on three key dimensions

How it works: Wedge selection should be very intentional. Don't just build for the first customer persona you think of or the closest city to where you live.

Build for the group that has:
1. The most acute pain point, OR
2. The highest willingness to pay, OR
3. The fastest sales cycles

Key insight: 'A product that's 10x better on average might actually be 50x better for some groups but only 1.1x better for others. Picking the right group will determine whether you quickly find product-market fit or face an uphill battle.'

The more carefully you pick your wedge, the easier it will be to succeed and to expand to the larger market.

### X/Y Axis Segmentation Matrix (Jeanne Grosser)
A method for segmenting a customer base by plotting size/complexity against revenue-driving attributes.

How it works: X-Axis: Company size (SMB, Mid-market, Enterprise). Y-Axis: Growth potential, business model (B2B, B2C, Marketplace), or usage metrics (e.g., Crux score/web traffic, workload type). Used to dictate sales motion and product roadmap.

## Checklists

### 9-Step Process to Identify Your ICP (How to identify your ideal customer profile (ICP))
A step-by-step checklist summarizing the entire ICP identification process from the newsletter.

How it works: Steps to identify your ICP:

1. Get super-specific and super-narrow with your ICP. Almost comically narrow. Aim for at least 3 attributes.
2. Start by paying attention to WHO gets most excited about what you're building.
3. Pay attention to which ROLES are most successfully implementing your product.
4. Pay attention to the SIZE of the company that's the best fit for your solution.
5. Look for patterns in the unique WAYS companies work (e.g. design-driven, operationally heavy, specific tech stack).
6. Be careful not to pattern-match too quickly from one or two customers.
7. Pay the most attention to signals from OUTBOUND SALES, instead of leads coming through your network (investor intros create 'fake interest').
8. Keep at it — it often takes many months (Zip took 6 months, Notion took years).
9. If all else fails, focus on what you can sell THIS MONTH. Quote high prices, negotiate down, prioritize people who can act now.

### Five Questions to Identify Your Super-Specific Who (How to kickstart and scale a consumer business—Step 2: Identify your super-specific who)
Five diagnostic questions to help founders pinpoint their ideal early adopters by working from real people and real signals.

How it works: Answer these five questions to identify your super-specific who:
1. Who are three real people you know who will be EXCEPTIONALLY excited about what you're building? What do they have in common?
2. Of your existing users, who are HAPPIEST and MOST COMMITTED? What do they have in common?
3. Who do you know whose life will become INSTANTLY better once they use your product?
4. Who do you know whose life will become INSTANTLY less painful?
5. Who's asking to PAY for this product?

You are looking for people who 'jump out of their chair and yell, That's amazing—I need that right now!' (April Dunford)

### How to Choose Between VSB and SMB as Your Initial Target (GTM motions of 30 B2B SaaS companies)
Three criteria for deciding whether to target very small businesses or small/midsize businesses as your initial market segment

How it works: 1. Default to SMB: Prioritize customers who are likely to survive; very-early-stage startups (VSBs) are not known for their long-term survival.

2. Follow the pain: Which scale of company, and who at this company, is most feeling the pain you're solving? The more pain, the easier the sell.

3. Find the early adopters: Who is most likely to give your newfangled, half-baked, hard-to-trust product a try? Is it engineers at VSBs? Eng managers at SMBs? Helping a few of them be successful will provide social proof to future, less-early-adopter types.

Bonus: You can also experiment with both segments to see where you feel the stronger pull.

Exception - Go straight to Enterprise ONLY IF the pain point you're solving is specific to (or far more acutely felt within) Enterprise businesses. Examples: scaling data workflows (Snowflake, Databricks), keeping tabs on expanding teams (Workday), tracking leads (HubSpot), staying in compliance (Vanta), procurement.

### Segmentation Heuristics Checklist (The definitive guide to mastering product sense interviews)
Six criteria dimensions to consider when creating meaningful user segments beyond basic demographics

How it works: When developing segments, consider these dimensions: 1. Primary motivations: What fundamental goals drive their behavior? 2. Behavioral patterns: How frequently and in what ways do they interact with similar products? 3. Context of use: Where, when, and how do they engage with the product/experience? 4. Expertise level: Are they novices, intermediate users, or experts? 5. Resource constraints: What limitations (time, money, knowledge, space) affect their usage? 6. Goals and outcomes: What specific results are they trying to achieve? Key test: Segments must be mutually exclusive — if someone could easily fit into several segments at once, your segmentation isn't precise enough.

### Target Segment Formula (Geoffrey Moore)
Criteria for choosing the perfect initial beachhead market.

How it works: The segment must be: 1) Big enough to matter (room to double/triple revenue to reach $100M in 5 years), 2) Small enough to lead (can capture 30-50% market share to become the standard), 3) A good fit with your crown jewels. The target must share the same geography, industry, and profession.

### Linear's Sign-Up Flow ICP Filtering (How to identify your ideal customer profile (ICP))
How Linear used their sign-up flow to ask qualifying questions and cherry-pick the best early customers from their waitlist.

How it works: Sign-up flow questions that enabled ICP filtering:
1. What's the size of your company?
2. What's your role?
3. Which tools are you using? (GitHub or GitLab? Slack or Microsoft Teams?)

How they used it:
- Cherry-picked customers off waitlist who matched narrow ICP (Google Auth + GitHub users in 2-5 person product companies)
- Only allowed Google authentication initially
- Only integrated with GitHub to start (not GitLab)
- Deprioritized consultancies even though they wanted to use it
- Only invited founder-led product companies off waitlist

Result: Stayed very narrow initially and ensured every early user was within their target ICP.

### PayPal's Wedge Discovery Story (Picking a wedge)
The story of how PayPal discovered its eBay wedge through organic user behavior, told by David Sacks

How it works: Before the wedge: PayPal was vaguely aware of multiple use cases — splitting dinner tabs, student allowances, auction payments. They thought 'emailing money' was a terrific idea but had no idea who would use it.

Discovery: Customer service rep forwarded an email from an eBay power seller who had turned the PayPal logo into a button for her auctions, asking permission to use it. eBay sellers were desperate for a solution because their alternative was waiting a week for checks to arrive and clear.

Action: Rolled out features specifically for auction sellers, dropped other plans, and went all-in on the eBay use case.

Results:
- End of 1999: <10,000 users
- End of January 2000: 100,000 users
- A few months later: 1 million users
- Summer 2000: 5 million users
- Growth curve: 'perfect hockey stick'

Key lesson: The wedge was discovered through organic user behavior, not top-down strategy. Users were already pulling the product into the wedge use case.

### Ramp's ICP Pivot from Early-Stage to Series A+ (How to identify your ideal customer profile (ICP))
How Ramp discovered that shifting from early-stage startups to Series A+ companies yielded 10-100x more revenue and different competitive dynamics.

How it works: Early-stage startup asks: 'I want a sign-up bonus. I want lounge access. I'm not paying myself enough.' (competing on perks like the rest of the market)

Series A+ / mid-market asks: 'Points aren't going to change my business. I am spending too much. I want to be more profitable. I want to close my books on time.' (competing on value)

Key insight: Revenue from Series A+ group was 'an order to two orders of magnitude more' than early-stage.

Decision: Made explicit choice to focus on Series A, B, and mid-market companies. Would welcome customers outside ICP but were clear about who they were building for.

Competitive reframing: Wasn't competing against credit cards anymore. Was competing against 'I hate Expensify or Concur' and 'I can't close my books on time.' This enabled competing differently and growing differently.

### Wealthfront's 'Fire Non-Target Customers' Principle (This Week #9: Breaking into growth, leading with influence, and (not) stepping on toes 🦶)
A real example of an extreme product principle around target customer focus

How it works: Product principle: 'Design exclusively for our target customer.'
