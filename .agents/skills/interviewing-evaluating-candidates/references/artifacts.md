## Frameworks

### 10 Jobs of a PM Interview Framework (My favorite PM interview questions)
A structured interview framework that maps one behavioral/situational question to each of the ten core PM jobs: Impact, Collaboration, Ownership, Leadership, Execution, Strategy, Customer Insights, Vision, Planning, and Communication, plus a bonus Decision Making question. Each question includes specific 'what to look for' criteria and red flags.

How it works: The framework maps 11 questions (10 core + 1 bonus) to PM competency areas:

1. IMPACT: 'What's the most important or impactful product you shipped? What made it so important or impactful?'
   Look for: Significant launch, how they measure impact, how much responsibility they had.
   Flags: Example isn't very important; didn't have much influence.

2. COLLABORATION: 'Tell me about a time you disagreed with an engineer on your team, and how you resolved it.'
   Look for: Convincing with information while preserving relationships; genuinely hearing others out; working backward from shared outcomes.
   Flags: Backing down immediately; railroading others.

3. OWNERSHIP: 'Share a time you shipped a product that failed. Why did it fail and what did you learn? Can you give me one other example?'
   Look for: Ownership of failure; identifying source of failure; lessons that inform current work.
   Flags: Throwing others under the bus; not taking failure seriously.

4. LEADERSHIP: 'Tell me about a time when your team didn't gel. What was the issue, and how did you deal with it? Has this happened on other teams you've been on?'
   Look for: Taking it upon themselves to fix team dynamics; articulating root cause; resolving it.
   Flags: 'It's not my job'; pattern of dysfunctional teams.

5. EXECUTION: 'Pick a project you're proud of that took 3-9 months. Walk me through it from beginning to end. I'll ask questions along the way. [Give this ~7-10 mins]'
   Look for: How they aligned on priorities/success criteria/timeline; dealt with delays/disagreements; kept everyone aligned; kept execs onboard; launch smoothness.
   Flags: Told what to build and just built it; zero issues (likely not honest).

6. STRATEGY: 'Pick a product you worked on in the past year—talk me through your product strategy for it.' [Alt: Take-home to come up with a strategy to 10x growth of existing product.]
   Look for: Excitement/energy; clear, succinct, cohesive strategy; deep insights into the problem.
   Flags: Lack of strategy; hard to follow.

7. CUSTOMER INSIGHTS: 'Tell me about a time you did user research on a product/feature, and that research had a big impact on the product.'
   Look for: Talking to customers before launch; research impacting what they launched; experience with different research modes.
   Flags: Haven't talked to customers; can't think of an example.

8. VISION: 'Tell me the vision for one of your recent projects or teams.'
   Look for: They have a vision; they're excited about it; it's at least somewhat inspiring.
   Flags: Only sharing planned impact or features vs. a vision of the future; no examples.

9. PLANNING: 'How do you get your team to commit to a deadline?'
   Look for: Co-creating deadlines with teammates; using deadlines to motivate/focus vs. forcing endless work; working backward from hard deadlines.
   Flags: Mandating deadlines without involving team; expecting deadlines to be hit or else.

10. COMMUNICATION: [Evaluated through all other questions]
    Look for: Clarity, conciseness, convincingness.
    Flags: Rambling; answers aren't convincing.

11. BONUS - DECISION MAKING: 'What's the biggest one-way-door product decision you've ever had to make?'
    Look for: Decision-making process and inputs; balancing business and user needs; bringing everyone along; de-risking.
    Flags: Working in a silo; relying too heavily on own opinion.

### 25 High-Signal Interview Questions Framework (Grouped by Theme) (How to learn the most about a candidate from a single interview question)
A structured collection of 25 interview questions organized into 5 categories, each with specific evaluation criteria from the leader who recommends it. Designed to maximize signal-to-noise in a 45-minute interview.

How it works: Categories and questions:

**Category 1: How do they handle the hard stuff? (7 questions)**
1. Talk me through your biggest product flop. What happened and what did you do about it? — Look for: brutal honesty about how bad it was and why it failed (Annie Pearl, Microsoft/Calendly)
2. What's the hardest thing you've ever done? — Look for: what 'hard' means to them, how they overcame it, how they worked with others, how much agency they had (Geoff Charles, Ramp)
3. Tell me about a time you've been in a challenging or highly ambiguous situation, and how you navigated that ambiguity. — Look for: seeking structure, seeking help and inputs vs 'this is the way' (JZ, Linktree/Webflow)
4. Describe a time when you were part of a controversial product decision, and what you did. — Look for: ability to represent both sides, even-keeled delivery, taking on different perspectives (Yuhki Yamashita, Figma)
5. Tell me about a time something went wrong. What happened and what did you do about it? — Look for: mindset when things fail, how they relate to evaluating the situation, self-perception during difficulty (Paige Costello, Asana)
6. What is the worst product that you've ever shipped? — Look for: humor, humility, ability to point out mistakes, learning from mistakes (Maggie Crowley, Toast)
7. Tell me about a time when you needed to disagree with your manager or fight for a position against higher leadership. — Look for: backbone + ability to disagree and commit (Ethan Evans, Amazon); influential communication skills, willingness to stand ground (Inbal Shani, GitHub)

**Category 2: How do they think? (4 questions)**
8. What's something that everyone takes for granted that you think is hogwash? — Look for: authenticity, genuine opinions, breaking the 'interview mindset'; can be adapted to domains like AI, management (Nikhyl Singhal, Meta)
9. What's an unfair secret you've learned to improve a product team's velocity and energy level? — Look for: original learnings not from Medium, how they learned and apply it (Noah Weiss, Slack)
10. Tell me about something you did that worked out but not for the reason that you thought it would. — Look for: introspection, reflective decision-making, incorporating learnings into mental models (Ayo Omojola, Carbon Health)
11. What is an experiment you launched that had a very unexpected result? And what did you do after that? — Look for: deep thinking, customer understanding; if result should have been expected based on their description, they're not thinking deeply enough (Hila Qu, growth advisor)

**Category 3: How do they build, ship, and drive impact? (6 questions)**
12. Tell me about your most significant professional accomplishment. — Look for: situation to result, who they influenced, how they influenced, decisions made, roadblocks encountered (Bill Carr, ex-Amazon)
13. Ask about a product they shipped that is NOT cherry-picked by them. — Look for: frameworks not just outcomes, how they build products, how they know if they're going to succeed (Laura Schaffer, Amplitude)
14. What's something that would not exist without your initiative? — Look for: ability to define something specific and tangible; getting startled is a warning sign (Upasna Gautam, CNN)
15. Tell me about a time when you delivered something that was impactful. — Look for: how they define impact, intrinsic motivation about business impact (Lauryn Isford, Notion)
16. Talk about a big problem that you worked on. — Look for: do you feel compelled to work on that problem after hearing them describe it? Tests storytelling and communication (Yuhki Yamashita, Figma)
17. Tell me about a piece of work that you're most proud of. — Look for: taste, judgment, motivations, what good looks like to them, what they like to do (Katie Dill, Stripe; Karri Saarinen, Linear)

**Category 4: Who are they as people? (6 questions)**
18. When I ask people you've worked with about you, what will I hear? What would they say are your superpowers and weaknesses? — Look for: self-awareness calibrated against actual references (people are usually accurate about strengths but not critiques); focus more on superpowers than weaknesses (Andrew Bosworth, Meta CTO)
19. To what do you attribute your success? (Can't say 'luck') — Look for: self-awareness, curiosity, reflection on why they are where they are (Eeke de Milliano, ex-Retool/Stripe)
20. Tell me about something you're really proud of that you accomplished. Take me through the process. — Look for: motivations, work ethic, what they care about, what good looks like (Camille Hearst, Spotify)
21. Fast-forward three years. What's different about you then? — Look for: humility, self-awareness about growth areas; red flag if they default to role/title aspirations only (Ben Williams, growth advisor)
22. Imagine you had a really great day at work and you're telling your partner about it. What are you telling them? — Look for: natural reward mechanisms, highest/best use of the person (Tom Conrad, Zero)
23. What frustrates you the most about where you're working right now? — Look for: what they're running away from, what they couldn't fix, whether they'll encounter the same issue at your company (Nilan Peiris, Wise)

**Category 5: Unexpected curveball questions (2 questions)**
24. A group of scientists have invented a teleportation device. They've hired you as their business counterpart. What two questions do you ask those scientists? — Look for: ability to find Eigenquestions that get to the heart of the problem (Shishir Mehrotra, Coda)
25. What question should I have asked you? — Look for: honest, off-guard answers; the person is an expert in themselves (Christina Wodtke, Stanford)

### 6-Step PM Interview Process (How to interview product managers)
A complete end-to-end PM interview pipeline with recommended durations, pass-through rates, and what to focus on at each step

How it works: Step 1: Recruiter Phone Screen [30 min, optional] — Minimum requirements, expectations, personality fit (not an asshole), timelines. ~50% pass rate.
Step 2: Hiring Manager Phone Screen [30 min] — Get to know the person, discuss the role, test 1-2 most important skills/attributes. ~40-50% pass rate.
Step 3: Full-Day Interview [4-5 hours] — Two parts: (a) Project presentation (done at home or on-site), shared with potential colleagues; (b) 3-6 one-on-one interviews with potential colleagues, each testing a key PM skill.
Step 4: Post-Interview Panel Discussion [30-60 min] — Each interviewer blind votes: Strong No, No, Yes, or Strong Yes. Share votes, discuss, hiring manager decides.
Step 5: Reference Checks — Contact references and backchannel to verify.
Step 6: Make an Offer — Put together compelling offer, share it, and work hard to close.

### Andrew Bosworth's Superpower vs. Weakness Reference Check Triangulation (How to learn the most about a candidate from a single interview question)
An interview technique that asks candidates to predict what their references will say about their strengths and weaknesses, then triangulates against actual reference checks.

How it works: Question: 'When I ask people you've worked with about you, what will I hear? What would they say are your superpowers and weaknesses?'

Method:
1. Ask the question in the interview
2. Note their predicted strengths and weaknesses
3. Follow up with actual reference checks
4. Compare predictions to reality

Key insight: People are rarely accurate about what critiques others have of them, but they are usually pretty accurate about their strengths.

Evaluation focus: Prioritize understanding superpowers over weaknesses. Ask: 'What is the thing I can hitch a wagon to and ride?' That matters more than downside risk.

Acceptable weakness responses:
- 'You'll hear this critique, I don't think it's fair' (acceptable if they're robust about it)
- 'This is something I'm working on' (shows growth mindset)

### Bar Raiser Program (Bill Carr)
A hiring mechanism to ensure objective, high-standard candidate evaluations.

How it works: Requires one designated 'Bar Raiser' per loop who does not report to the hiring manager. They run the debrief meeting, ensure behavioral questions map to objective company principles, and hold technical veto power.

### Candidate Convincing Framework for Work Trials (Adding a work trial to your interview process)
Five strategies from Automattic for getting candidates excited about investing time in a work trial

How it works: Two reasons candidates agree to participate:
1. It's a chance to get to know the company better than a typical interview—especially for distributed/async work
2. The format lets them showcase skills better than interviews—some people are better at doing than interviewing

Five enablers to support candidates:
1. Tailored trial projects: Not live work, but representative; sometimes synthetic, sometimes past challenges
2. Flexibility: Don't expect time off from current job; when/how much time is a conversation, not a mandate; can pause for life events
3. Continuous feedback: Not a take-home—ongoing feedback so candidates can adapt and learn
4. Set clear expectations: From first interview, explain the process, what it entails, time commitments
5. Dedicated Trial Leads: Specialized point of contact who knows the teams and work well

A multi-step interview process designed to test both hard and soft skills.

How it works: Includes a technical coding screen followed by a shortened real-world business case where the interviewer intentionally introduces wrong assumptions to test the candidate's reaction, adaptability, and ability to make decisions with imperfect information.

### Drivers vs. Passengers Hiring Framework (How Snowflake builds product)
Snowflake's hiring philosophy for finding high-impact PMs who drive outcomes rather than passively follow

How it works: Core principle: Hire 'drivers' not 'passengers'

What to look for:
- People who get satisfaction from making things happen
- Strong sense of ownership
- Already driving impact and being successful in their current roles
- Adaptability, humbleness, and desire to learn

Interview process:
1. Candidate presentation: Almost every PM interview starts with a presentation from the candidate to the interview team and area leaders. Topic can be anything (often a product they delivered). Evaluates communication skills, passion, and pride in work
2. Values-based evaluation: Evaluate candidates across each of Snowflake's eight core values throughout the process
3. Back-channel references: Before extending an offer, tap into networks for references on candidate's impact and strengths

### Eigenquestion Test for Interviews (How to learn the most about a candidate from a single interview question)
A creative interview technique using a hypothetical scenario (teleportation device) to test whether candidates can quickly identify the one or two most important questions that get to the heart of a problem.

How it works: Prompt: 'A group of scientists have invented a teleportation device. They've hired you to be their business counterpart and bring this to market. What two questions do you ask those scientists?'

Concept: An Eigenquestion is the question that, when answered, most reduces the space of possible answers for all other questions. Sharp product managers and engineers quickly find the 1-2 Eigenquestions on any topic. This is a coded test of that ability.

What to look for: The candidate's two questions should dramatically narrow the problem space and reveal first-principles thinking about what matters most for commercializing a new technology.

### First-Round Interview Time Breakdown (How to pass any first-round interview (even in a terrible talent market))
The standard time allocation for a first-round tech interview from the employer's perspective

How it works: Standard breakdown:
- 10% intro
- 70% questions from the interviewer
- 20% questions from the candidate

For a 45-minute interview:
- 3-5 minutes intros
- 35 minutes questions from interviewer
- 5-7 minutes questions from candidate

Most first-round interviews are:
- 30 minutes to 1 hour
- Phone or video chat
- One interviewer (not panel)
- Conducted by hiring manager, peer, or someone familiar with the role
- Focused on behavioral questions
- Not comprehensive (1-2 core competencies + culture fit)

### Five-Step Analytical Thinking Interview Framework (The definitive guide to mastering analytical thinking interviews)
A linear flow for structuring a 35-minute analytical thinking interview to maximize signal delivery across all evaluated dimensions

How it works: Five sequential steps with recommended time allocation:

1. **Assumptions and Game Plan** (~1 min): State assumptions to narrow scope, share your game plan for the interview
2. **Product Rationale** (~2-3 min including think time): Articulate product context, market positioning, company-product alignment
3. **Metric Framework** (~12-15 min, largest chunk): Define ecosystem players, map value/actions to metrics, define North Star metric with guardrails
4. **Goal-Setting** (~5 min): Altitude shift from product-level to team-level, pick highest-leverage ecosystem player, score goals on impact and ability to influence
5. **Tradeoff Evaluation** (~10 min): Navigate real-world tradeoff questions by connecting back to mission, metrics, and strategy

Key signals interviewers evaluate: Strategic thinking, Analytical rigor, Structured communication, Decisive recommendations

### Growth Mindset Interview Question (Peter Deng)
Peter's go-to final interview question used across OpenAI, Uber, Instagram, and Airtable to assess growth mindset as the most critical hiring criterion.

How it works: Question: 'Think about one of the biggest mistakes you've made — truly, the more painful the better. Describe the situation, and tell me how you actually think differently now, work differently as a result. How has that turned into a core principle of yours?' Evaluation criteria: Look for genuine vulnerability (not 'I worked too hard'), depth of self-reflection, evidence that the mistake changed actual behavior or principles, passion in recounting. Second-order benefit: if hired, you've already established a relationship baseline of openness and psychological safety.

A simple decision framework for hiring: if you're not excited, don't hire.

How it works: Only hire people you feel 'hell yes' about. If it's a maybe, it's a no. The people you bring in determine the company you become. Reference: https://sivers.org/hellyeah

### Hiring Quality Triangle for Fast-Growth Companies (Ray Cao)
Three key qualities to evaluate when hiring for high-autonomy, fast-growth environments: curiosity, discipline, and ability to prioritize

How it works: Three qualities: (1) Curiosity: Naturally curious about new things, don't get stuck in their own domain. (2) Discipline: Follow guidance on collaboration ('reactive doing'), avoid creating chaos when given broad context. (3) Ability to Prioritize: Nobody can do everything — push the right agenda at the right time. Context: These traits are essential for 'context, no control' culture to work. Without them, broad autonomy creates chaos rather than innovation.

### Home Court / Away Court / Neutral Court Interview Design (Shishir Mehrotra)
A framework for balancing interview questions across three domains to avoid biased evaluation.

How it works: Home Court: Questions about your company/product ('What would you do about X at Coda?'). Risk: Tests if candidate reached same answer as you, not their actual ability. Away Court: Questions about their past work ('How did you solve this?'). Risk: Can't verify difficulty, attribution, or completeness. Neutral Court: Novel problems neither party has worked on (e.g., teleporter question). Safest for evaluation. Recommendation: Majority of interviews should be neutral court. Include a candidate presentation as the away court component — let them upper-bound themselves by presenting whatever they want ('the brag session').

### Interview Brainstorming Exercise for PM Candidates (Vikrama Dhiman)
An interview technique where you pick a product the candidate uses frequently and brainstorm product choices together in a collaborative, deepening conversation

How it works: Process: (1) Pick a product the candidate uses most often — choose one you don't have strong opinions on. (2) Ask: 'What if this product were to do X? Does it make sense?' (3) Go deeper: 'How would it evolve in 6 months? 12 months?' (4) Develop the idea together as a two-way conversation. Look for: Can they abstract out overall product goals? Can they identify users? Do they reason backwards from strategy? Are they obsessed with the feature or what it enables? Are they willing to consider alternative options?

### Interview Question Evaluation Rubric (What to Look For) (How to learn the most about a candidate from a single interview question)
For each of the 25 questions, a specific set of signals and evaluation criteria to determine a strong vs. weak answer. Can be used as a scorecard during interviews.

How it works: Key evaluation signals by category:

**Handling Hard Stuff — Strong signals:**
- Brutal honesty about failures (not deflecting)
- Clear sense of personal agency in overcoming difficulty
- Seeking structure and help through ambiguity (not 'I had all the answers')
- Ability to represent both sides of a controversy evenly
- Humor and humility about mistakes
- Backbone to disagree AND ability to commit after

**Thinking — Strong signals:**
- Genuine, authentic opinions (not rehearsed/predictable)
- Original insights learned from experience (not from blog posts)
- Introspection: reflecting on WHY things worked, incorporating into decision model
- Deep customer understanding revealed through unexpected experiment results

**Building/Shipping/Impact — Strong signals:**
- Can trace situation → actions → influence → decisions → roadblocks → result
- Has frameworks for building, not just outcome stories
- Can name specific tangible things that wouldn't exist without them
- Intrinsic motivation about business impact (not just personal achievement)
- Makes you feel compelled to work on the problem they describe (storytelling test)
- Reveals taste, judgment, and what 'good' means to them

**Who They Are — Strong signals:**
- Self-awareness calibrated with reality (especially about strengths)
- Openness about growth areas without defensiveness
- Natural reward mechanisms align with the role
- Can articulate what they're running from AND toward
- Focuses on personal/professional growth over title/role in 3-year vision

**Red flags across all categories:**
- Only telling you what they think you want to hear
- Unable to name a specific failure or worst product
- Expected experiment results described as 'unexpected'
- Defaulting to title/role aspirations for growth questions
- Unable to define something specific they initiated

### Linear's Hiring Process for Product Roles (How Linear builds product)
A multi-stage hiring process that evaluates product sense and culminates in a paid work trial.

How it works: What to look for:
- Product sense, taste, and judgment for ALL product roles (engineering, design)
- Ability to form AND articulate opinions on products/features (not just personal preference — must explain rationale)
- For non-product roles: look for adjacent skills beyond role requirements (e.g., storytelling for marketing, user understanding for operations)
- Overall: people who look and care beyond their immediate role

Interview stages:
1. Recruiter screen
2. Hiring manager interview
3. Skills assessment
4. Cross-functional or 'beyond' interviews (e.g., CEO interviews engineers for product thinking)
5. Paid work trial (1-5 days)

Product Thinking Assessment:
- Ask about past product work: 'Did you disagree with something you built at [past company]? Why?'
- Probe whether they can articulate rationale, not just state preferences

Work Trial Details:
- Paid sessions, 1-5 days
- Candidate works on a real project related to their function
- Access granted to: docs, codebase, designs, Slack
- Invited to relevant company and team meetings
- One-on-one chats with team members
- Present work at the end
- Engineers: build a feature from a spec
- Leadership candidates: develop response to a challenge/area prompt

Purpose: Understand how candidates approach problems, handle uncertainty, and communicate. Candidates get a realistic sense of the company and team.

### Nikhyl Singhal's 'Save Question' Technique (How to learn the most about a candidate from a single interview question)
A technique to break candidates out of the 'interview mindset' where they predict what you want to hear, using a question that forces genuine, opinionated responses.

How it works: Core question: 'What's something that everyone takes for granted that you think is hogwash?'

Purpose: Acts as a 'save question' for candidates who may be high-quality but aren't being genuine in the interview. The question forces authenticity because it starts with 'What is the thing that YOU think...'

Variants for different contexts:
- For managers: 'You've managed hundreds of people. What's conventional wisdom about management that you bet against, that you've found is actually inaccurate?'
- For AI roles: 'What do people think about AI that's inaccurate, that everyone believes?'
- For any domain: Adapt the format to domain-specific conventional wisdom

When to use: When you suspect the candidate is giving rehearsed answers and you're at risk of dismissing a genuinely strong candidate.

### Nilan Peiris's 'Running Away From' Assessment (How to learn the most about a candidate from a single interview question)
A technique to understand a candidate's limits and potential fit issues by probing what frustrates them at their current role, rather than why they want to join your company.

How it works: Question: 'What frustrates you the most about where you're working right now?'

Rationale: What candidates are running away from is more interesting than what they say they're running toward. The frustration reveals:
1. Something broken they encountered
2. Something they were unable to fix (their limit)
3. A pattern that may or may not exist at your company

Evaluation: After hearing their frustration, ask yourself: 'Are they going to run into this here every day/every week? Or should they be fine?' Use this as a fit assessment.

### PM Interview Project Decision Matrix (How to interview product managers)
A framework for deciding how to deliver and scope the project portion of PM interviews, with pros/cons for each option

How it works: DELIVERY OPTIONS:
Option 1: At Home Before Interview
- Pros: More time to think, closer to real life, gives more interview time during the day
- Cons: Unfair for people with less time outside of work, some people don't like 'working' on their own time, more confounding variables
- ~2/3 of hiring managers prefer this option

Option 2: Day of Interview (e.g. 90 minutes)
- Pros: Equalizes interview conditions, doesn't suck up personal time
- Cons: Less time to think, more pressure, less time for other interviews

PROJECT TOPIC OPTIONS:
Option 1: A real problem you've faced (Lenny's preference — best signal)
Option 2: A hypothetical problem related to your product (most popular)
Option 3: A hypothetical problem unrelated to your product

Majority of hiring managers go with Option 1 or 2.

KEY EVALUATION CRITERIA (what to look for):
- How they break down the problem
- How they structure their solution
- How they communicate it
- NOT how close they got to the right answer
- The discussion afterward is often more valuable than the presentation

### PSHE (Problem, Solution, How, Execution) (Shishir Mehrotra)
A talent evaluation framework that assesses at which level of the problem-solving stack a person operates, replacing scope-based leveling that creates perverse incentives.

How it works: Levels from junior to senior: E (Execution) - Given problem, solution, and how; just execute the playbook. H (How) - Given problem and rough solution; figure out organization, milestones, go-to-market, rituals, meetings. S (Solution) - Given a problem; come back with creative, effective solutions. P (Problem) - Tell us what problems to solve; identify what actually matters. Key insights: 1) Scope is an input (assigned by manager), not an output to evaluate. 2) Mid-career 'trough of dissolution' where evaluation shifts from scope to PSHE is confusing for both employee and manager. 3) P-level thinking is correlated with eigenquestion ability. 4) Applies to all roles including sales (great salespeople are P-level, not just quota-hitters). The relationship between scope and PSHE forms a curve: early career grows in scope at E level, mid-career grows in PSHE, senior career grows in scope at P level.

### Post-Interview Blind Voting Scale (How to interview product managers)
A standardized voting system for post-interview panel discussions to reduce bias

How it works: Each interviewer independently (blind) votes one of four options before any discussion:
1. Strong No
2. No
3. Yes
4. Strong Yes

Process: Vote blind → Share votes → Discuss → Hiring manager makes final decision. The blind voting prevents anchoring bias from senior voices influencing others.

### Work Trial Realism Spectrum (Adding a work trial to your interview process)
A spectrum for how realistic your work trial should be, from take-home projects to co-working in the real codebase, correlated with company size

### Top 10 Accomplishments Interview Question (Barbra Gago)
An interview question used to gauge a candidate's values and balance of quantitative vs. qualitative achievements.

How it works: Ask the candidate: 'What are your top 10 accomplishments?' Look for whether they list personal vs. professional achievements, and how they balance quantitative metrics with qualitative milestones.

### Worst Product Shipped Interview Question (Maggie Crowley)
An interview question used to test a PM candidate's experience, humility, and ability to learn from failure.

How it works: Ask 'What's the worst product you've ever shipped?' Look for candidates who laugh, admit a real mistake, and explain what they learned from the failure.
