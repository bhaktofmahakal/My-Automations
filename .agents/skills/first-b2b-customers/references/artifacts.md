## Frameworks

### 4 Pillars of Early User Love (Claire Butler)
A strategy to get initial individual contributors to deeply care about a new tool.

How it works: 1. Establish credibility (avoid traditional marketing, focus on technical depth). 2. Build the product with users (extreme customer obsession and rapid bug fixing). 3. Meet them in their existing channels (e.g., Twitter) rather than making them come to you. 4. Be extremely transparent and honest.

### 7-Step Sequence for Finding Early B2B Customers (How to find and win your first 10 B2B customers)
A prioritized sequence of channels to pursue when finding your first 10 B2B customers, ordered from highest trust/lowest scale to lowest trust/highest scale

How it works: Recommended sequence:
1. Start by reaching out to your personal network, looking for people who match your ICP
2. Go cold outbound, but be strategic about it
3. Tap your investors' networks
4. Participate in relevant communities — and network
5. Put out compelling content and build a following online
6. Get press
7. Just launch

Simplified version:
1. Start with former colleagues (yours, and those of your early employees)
2. Collect your investors' contacts who match your profile and then go cold outbound (creatively)
3. Find a community where your ICPs might be hanging out, and go participate

### Cap Table as a Growth Strategy (Sri Batchu)
Getting influential founders, operators, and advisors onto your cap table so their companies become early customers and advocates

How it works: Steps: 1) Identify influential founders/operators in your target market, 2) Get them onto the cap table as angel investors/advisors, 3) Their companies become initial customers, 4) They provide word-of-mouth and credibility. Examples: Eight Sleep founder, Pod founder, VC firms that are both investors and customers.

### Concentric Circles of Trust Model (How to find and win your first 10 B2B customers)
A mental model for prioritizing early customer acquisition channels based on the level of innate trust between you and the prospect, working outward from highest to lowest trust

How it works: Visualize concentric circles radiating outward from the founder:
- Inner circle: Friends and family (highest trust)
- Next ring: Former colleagues and early employee networks
- Next ring: Investor portfolio companies and batch-mates
- Next ring: Community members and networking contacts
- Next ring: Content audience and followers
- Next ring: Press readers
- Outer ring: Cold outbound and public launches (lowest trust)

Key insight: Start with the channels that have the most innate levels of trust, and work your way outward. Trust is your secret weapon because (1) early products are janky so people need to tolerate imperfection, (2) prospects need to take a risk on an unproven startup, and (3) trusted contacts give more honest feedback.

### Design Partner Development Process / 'Slice' Strategy (How Gong builds product)
A methodology for developing new products and features through continuous iteration with design partners, from embarrassingly early prototypes through limited availability to general availability

How it works: Step 1 — Identify the problem: Know the problem to solve but not necessarily the solution (e.g., 'increase predictability and improve operational rhythm').
Step 2 — Recruit design partners: Reach out to 6-30 design partners (companies/users) who have the identified need. More partners for bigger products (6 for Forecast, 20-30 for sales engagement split across 3-4 PMs).
Step 3 — First 'slice': Develop a first slice of the product and make it available in 'limited availability' (alpha/beta). Give access in an 'embarrassing state' — very far from being a product.
Step 4 — Weekly iteration: Meet with design partners weekly. Gather ideas and suggestions, synthesize across multiple partners, show different approaches the following week.
Step 5 — Expand limited availability: Features can stay in limited availability for months. Customers who express need can be put in limited availability for any given feature (hundreds of customers possible before GA).
Step 6 — Graduation: Feature moves to generally available only when impact is proven.
Key principles: Literally every feature is designed with design partners. Very little work is done 'inspirationally' without involving customers. Even incubated capabilities go through this cycle. For AI/data products, mocks aren't sufficient — customers need to see their own data in context.

### Friction as Validation (Raaz Herzberg)
The principle that creating friction in a sales or evaluation process actually validates genuine customer commitment, rather than scaring prospects away

How it works: When Wiz sent a long technical questionnaire to a Fortune 10 prospect before starting a POV (partly to buy time to build the product), they feared scaring the customer away. The questionnaire came back filled out the next day. Lesson: At early stage, you don't want to push someone into a POV — you need their commitment. Creating friction filters for genuinely committed customers. If they won't do the work, they're not committed enough for an early-stage product journey. Connected philosophy: Raaz's mother taught that friction indicates where you need to invest more — 'if there's blood when you brush your teeth, brush harder there.'

### Friends vs. Strangers First Decision Framework (How to find and win your first 10 B2B customers)
Two contrasting perspectives on whether to sell to friends or cold prospects first, to help founders decide based on their situation

How it works: Perspective 1 — Sell to strangers first (Zip's approach):
- Rationale: 'If we sell to friends, they might buy it because they feel bad or whatever. It'll confuse us.'
- Goal: 'They should buy it because they see value in it, and that's how we'll know it's a good idea.'
- Best for: When you need clean signal on whether the idea is viable

Perspective 2 — Sell to friends first (Coda's approach):
- Rationale: 'For a product like Coda, you can't force anybody to use it. If they don't like it, they will stop using it, regardless of how close they are to you.'
- Benefit: 'The ability for them to give you honest feedback is actually higher if they know you.'
- Best for: Products with ongoing usage where adoption can't be faked

Lenny's recommendation: Start with friends/network (highest trust) and work outward, but be aware of the bias risk.

### Lighthouse Users Program (10→100→1000) (Tanguy Crusson)
A staged approach to early customer development where you deliberately limit user exposure and define different success criteria at each stage

How it works: Stage 1 (10 users): Prove problems match solutions. Focus on explaining WHY these 10 are proxies for all future customers. Evidence = video snippets of user conversations about problems and solutions. Whole team (PM, design, engineering) meets users on Zoom, users join team Slack. Stage 2 (10→100): Test variations in scenarios, security needs, edge cases. Still high-touch, not self-service. Stage 3 (100→1000): Solve self-service gaps - reduce need for Slack support, Zoom onboarding calls, support tickets. Stage 4 (1000+): Graduate. Product is self-service ready for broad distribution.

Step 2: Ask your customers to share their success with others by putting a few simple programs in place to allow them to evangelize.

Key insight: Leverage not just your personal network, but your customers' and investors' networks as well.

### Three B2B Early Customer Acquisition Strategies (How today's fastest growing B2B businesses found their first ten customers)
The only three sourcing strategies that account for every B2B company's very early growth, used to decide how to find your first 10 customers

How it works: Strategy 1: Tap your personal network 👋
- Key question: Who in your network could be a potential customer?
- Sub-segments: Friends, former colleagues, investors, incubator/accelerator peers
- Most important if you need to 'sell' your product (not bottom-up)
- Companies that used this: Slack, Asana, Salesforce, Carta, Gusto, Stripe, Amplitude, Workday, Looker, Coda, Okta, Intercom

Strategy 2: Seek out your customers where they are 🧗‍♀️
- Key question: Who are your target customers, and where are they currently spending time, online or offline?
- Channels: online communities, forums, IRC, mailing lists, Twitter, Hacker News, offline events, meetups, door-to-door
- Companies that used this: Shopify, New Relic, Plaid, Figma, Square, Atlassian, Segment, Airtable, Dropbox

Strategy 3: Get press 🗞
- Key question: What's a unique and untold story you can pitch press?
- Rarely the primary way to get started
- Companies that used this: Canva, Twilio, Slack

Key insight: Almost every B2B business uses BOTH Strategy 1 AND Strategy 2. The question is how far your network takes you before you move on.

A structure for writing cold emails or LinkedIn DMs that get high response rates.

How it works: 1. Relevancy (Why reach out to them specifically). 2. Counterintuitive insight (Shock value or different perspective). 3. Focus on the problem (Do not mention the solution). 4. Concise (3-4 sentences max, no scrolling on mobile).

### Okta's Monthly Outreach Quota System (How today's fastest growing B2B businesses found their first ten customers)
A target-based system for systematically working through your network to find early B2B customers

How it works: System:
1. Set a monthly target of net new contacts to reach (Okta used 15-18 new IT folks at different companies per month)
2. Sources to mine:
   - Personal contacts from current/past jobs
   - LinkedIn connections and alma mater networks
   - Angel investors' LinkedIn networks
3. Duration: First 6 months of the company
4. Track quota attainment (Okta hit 85%+ of quota)
5. Focus each conversation on the prospect's pain points and how your product addresses them

## Examples

### Airtable's Organic Path to First Payment (How today's fastest-growing B2B startups turned their early users into paying customers)
How Airtable's users hunted down hidden payment options and their first customer paid by mailed check

How it works: Airtable always technically had paid plans and credit card entry, but they were 'super buried' and the team didn't think anyone would find them. Users literally hunted down the hidden menu because they wanted to support the product. The team didn't build the ability to charge credit cards until they realized people had been entering them. Simultaneously, they did proactive outreach to early active users to ensure success and build mental models of use cases. Relationships naturally evolved to users asking when they should pay. Their first money came from a fire department that wanted to mail a check, at which point they set up invoicing. From there, they evolved a sales motion.

### Cohort-Based Beta Rollout Strategy (Karri Saarinen)
Linear's strategy for onboarding their first users over a year-long private beta.

How it works: 1. Launch waitlist with a survey (tools used, company size). 2. Manually select ~10 users/week matching the target profile. 3. Onboard cohort and collect feedback. 4. Fix bugs/issues found by that cohort. 5. Invite the next cohort.

### Cold Emailing Sheryl Sandberg to Close Facebook at Glassdoor (Sahil Mansuri)
A detailed case study of how Sahil closed Facebook as Glassdoor's most-wanted account by creating a custom research report and emailing it directly to Sheryl Sandberg

How it works: Context: Facebook was Glassdoor's #1 target account—most visited page, repeatedly said no to standard outreach from CEO down to reps. Approach: 1) Analyzed every Facebook employee review on Glassdoor. 2) Had data scientist create word cloud and sentiment analysis. 3) Pulled salary comparisons vs Google, Amazon, Microsoft for software engineers. 4) Compiled Mark Zuckerberg's 96% CEO approval rating vs peers. 5) Created 9-page custom research report. 6) Cold-emailed Sheryl Sandberg (guessed email address, put 15+ variants in BCC). 7) Subject line: 'Mark's approval rating on Glassdoor.' 8) Sent ~3-4PM Sunday. Response by 6PM from Sheryl CC'ing executive leadership team. 9) Met at Facebook HQ next day, met Sheryl then Mark in 'the fishbowl.' Outcome: Report got added to weekly ELT packet. Closed massive deal. Key lesson: Teach something genuinely valuable rather than pitch product.

### Figma: First customer obsession and trust (A guide for finding product-market fit in B2B)
Dylan Field's mindset of treating the first unpaying alpha user (Coda/Krypton) as a critical customer, framing it as a trust obligation.

How it works: Situation: Figma hadn't shipped yet — still in alpha. A bug caused the product to not work well over a weekend.
Dylan's response: Called a 'red alert' — insisted on fixing the bug immediately.
Team pushback: 'We haven't shipped yet, there's no such thing as a red alert.'
Dylan's reasoning: 'You don't understand. There are customers depending on us. I convinced this guy to use our product. They're trying to use it for work, and if we fuck it up for them, I don't want that on my conscience. They believed in me.'

Key insight: Frame early customer relationships as trust. A lot of founders want to understand customers just enough to figure out what features they'd pay for. Dylan thought about it in terms of holding up his end of the bargain.

Result: That first customer (Coda) is still a Figma customer.

### Retool's Crunchbase Outbound Strategy (How to find and win your first 10 B2B customers)
A specific, replicable cold outbound tactic using Crunchbase data filtering to identify and reach ideal prospects

How it works: Step-by-step approach:
1. Buy a Crunchbase account (Retool shared one account across 5 companies to save money)
2. Filter companies by: (a) when they last fundraised, (b) what verticals they're in
3. Target operationally heavy verticals (delivery startups, fintech) — NOT SaaS companies (SaaS companies don't build many internal tools)
