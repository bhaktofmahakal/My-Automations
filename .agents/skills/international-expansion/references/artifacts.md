# International Market Expansion - Frameworks, Templates & Checklists

*8 artifacts extracted from Lenny's Podcast and Newsletter*

## Frameworks

### Divide and Conquer Liquidity Cycle (This Week #12: Expanding your business internationally 🌏)
A geographic expansion tactic for achieving marketplace liquidity city by city in new international markets

How it works: Cycle: Constrain geo → Achieve liquidity → Review geo (split or move)

Example walkthrough:
1. Want to launch in the UK? Start with London only.
2. Achieve liquidity in London — suggested benchmark: $10k in GMV over a rolling 30-day period (may differ for your business).
3. Once liquidity is achieved, either:
   a. Split London into 4-5 subregions and repeat the cycle in each, AND/OR
   b. Move to the next cities: Birmingham, Manchester, Leeds & Liverpool
   c. Or expand to other countries' primary cities: Berlin, Paris & Amsterdam
4. Repeat the cycle in each new geo.

### International Expansion Four-Part Framework (This Week #12: Expanding your business internationally 🌏)
A structured framework for planning and executing international marketplace expansion, organized from most challenging to least challenging workstream

How it works: Four categories sorted from most to least challenging:

1. BUSINESS MODEL
   - What changes to our business model will we have to make?
   - What are the most important regulatory aspects that allow us to thrive in our home market and how do these differ?
   - Can I run a transaction fee marketplace and will I have to adjust take rates?

2. PRODUCT
   - How do we globalize, internationalize, localize?
   - How do we set up our infrastructure to make our product fast everywhere?
   - Payments!

3. DISTRIBUTION
   - Retrain the organizational 'liquidity muscle': dust off v1 of your old market development playbook, interview early employees and make a list of specific growth tactics tried in the early days
   - Be open to new ideas as technology and markets change: some things that worked a few years ago may no longer work, others could work better now
   - Use a divide and conquer cycle: Constrain geo → achieve liquidity → review geo (split or move)
   - Example: Launch UK → start with London → achieve liquidity ($10k GMV over rolling 30 days) → split London into 4-5 subregions and/or expand to Birmingham, Manchester, Leeds & Liverpool or Berlin, Paris & Amsterdam

4. ORGANIZATION
   - You are restarting a marketplace in a far away geography where team members may not speak local languages and cultural barriers are higher than expected
   - Use data AND do things that don't scale: speak to customers, ensure data infrastructure reports on local progress
   - DON'T over-invest in long-term processes (for now)
   - DO invest heavily in documentation, data gathering, and knowledge sharing

### Rover's 'Activated Markets' Expansion Strategy (All the ways to grow your product)
A framework for deciding which geographic markets to expand into, based on monitoring organic growth signals

How it works: 1. Start in one city, keep it focused, learn about market dynamics.
2. Enter new cities by spending on Google AdWords.
3. Maintain a concept of 'activated markets' and a target list of markets to activate.
4. Monitor for organic growth bubbling up in new markets — let that inform where to go next.
5. Accept that non-activated markets will have bad performance due to low liquidity.
6. Be willing to 'let it ride' in non-activated markets until they show organic traction.
7. When ready, 'flip the switch and go national.'

### Two-List Market Prioritization Method (This Week #12: Expanding your business internationally 🌏)
A method for choosing which international markets to expand into first by creating and merging two ranked lists

How it works: Step 1: Create a SHORT-TERM ranked list based on:
  - Existing demand/supply in the market
  - Web/app traffic from that market
  - Local product advantages (ease of language localization, payment localization)

Step 2: Create a LONG-TERM ranked list based on:
  - Largest TAM (total addressable market)
  - Which regions/countries are easiest to execute in

Step 3: Merge these two lists into a single ranked list of top choices.

Note: Top markets that typically emerge include UK, Germany, France, China, Canada, and Australia.

## Checklists

### Why Expand Internationally — Considerations Checklist (This Week #12: Expanding your business internationally 🌏)
Key factors to evaluate when building your case for international expansion

How it works: Consider these factors when building your WHY:
1. Market size opportunity — the rest of the world is larger than the US alone
2. Equity story for future investors
3. Access to great talent
4. Strengthening network effects (especially for marketplace businesses with cross-border usage)
5. Competitive dynamics — could international competitors threaten your home market?
6. Impact on existing customers
7. Impact on employees (morale and recruiting boost — people will want to relocate)
8. Impact on investors
9. Building a globally recognized brand with significant international revenues

## Examples

### Airbnb European Expansion Blitz (Growth inflections)
Tactical playbook for how Airbnb rapidly expanded to Europe to fend off a copycat competitor

How it works: Context: European copycat received $90M funding and ramped to 400 employees in 2 months. Airbnb needed to move fast.

Localization (3 months, Sept-Dec 2011):
- Purchased top-level domains for 9 countries
- Assembled global translation team
- Contracted Akamai to reduce site load time
- Restructured UI for long text strings (e.g., 'average' → 'durchschnittliche')
- Shifted to multi-currency payment platform

Supply & Demand:
- Opened offices in 8 European cities
- Hired 'missionaries not mercenaries' (culture-focused)
- Acquired Accoleo (Germany) and Crashpadder (UK) to seed supply
- January 2012: PR blitz including 'Rent the country of Liechtenstein on Airbnb'

Key lesson: When competition comes after you in a marketplace (winner-take-all), move ridiculously fast. Speed > perfection.

### Algorithmic Country Launch Process (Dmitry Zlokazov)
An example of reverse-engineering a complex, regulation-heavy process into a scalable playbook.

How it works: Steps to launch a bank branch in a new country: 1) Comply with local regulations, 2) Report data to authorities, 3) Register with local payment systems. Revolut reverse-engineered successful launches (Ireland, Netherlands) to create an algorithmic process with defined steps, quality gates, assignees, and hiring timelines.

### Competitive dynamics: International competitors threatening home markets (This Week #12: Expanding your business internationally 🌏)
Real-world examples of international companies outcompeting domestic players, used as a reason to consider proactive international expansion

How it works: Example 1: HelloFresh (German, $4bn market cap) vs. Blue Apron (US, $50m market cap) — the German company massively outgrew the American competitor.

Example 2: Spotify (Swedish, 100m+ paying subscribers) vs. Apple Music, Pandora, Tidal, Rdio — a Swedish company became the global dominant player over multiple US competitors.

Key insight: Domestic competitors can use their own international expansion to raise more growth capital, hire faster, and become a serious threat in your home market.
