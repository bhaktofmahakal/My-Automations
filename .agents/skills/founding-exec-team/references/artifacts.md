# Founding Executive Team Building - Frameworks, Templates & Checklists

*20 artifacts extracted from Lenny's Podcast and Newsletter*

## Frameworks

### 2004 Red Sox Team Building Model (Brian Halligan)
A strategy for building an executive team by mixing homegrown talent with a few experienced outsiders.

How it works: Rely heavily on homegrown, inexpensive talent that rises through the ranks, supplemented by a few high-priced, experienced 'free agents' from the outside to balance the culture.

### Advisor Equity Structure (Luc Levesque)
A compensation model designed to perfectly align a growth advisor's incentives with the founder's goals.

How it works: 100% equity-based. Front-loaded vesting to encourage immediate knowledge transfer. Includes a 3-month cliff so either party can walk away risk-free if it's not working. Must include a 'long tail' expiration (10+ years) for options.

### Early Hiring Sequence Framework (Employee #1, First 3, First 10) (Hiring your early team)
A data-driven framework showing what roles to hire at each stage of early startup growth, based on analysis of 25+ successful B2B startups

How it works: Employee #1: ~67% hired an engineer. Exceptions: compliance SME (Vanta), recruiter (Coda), AI data scientist (Sprig), customer success (Segment, Amplitude). Rule: If founding team can build V1, hire to de-risk or un-bottleneck.

First 3 hires: 100% hired at least one engineer. ~25% of non-engineer hires were customer success/support. Subject-matter experts common in regulatory markets (Gusto, Zip, Vanta). 40%+ had designer as co-founder or early hire. Very few salespeople. Three companies hired a PM (Coda, Persona, Snyk).

First 10 hires: Sales becomes 2nd most common after engineering. 25% hired a PM. Recruiters surprisingly common (Linear, Figma, Ramp, Coda).

### Four Channels for Finding Early Employees (Hiring your early team)
Ranked channels for sourcing your first hires at a startup

How it works: Channel 1: Friends and former colleagues — By far the biggest channel. Multi-time founders and YC alumni have advantage. Downside: may not work if you're fresh out of college or have been failing publicly.

Channel 2: Cold outreach — Surprisingly second most common. Primarily through LinkedIn and GitHub. Look for people with significant public experience in your domain, contributors to relevant open-source repos. Available to anyone regardless of network.

Channel 3: Job boards — AngelList was most mentioned. Also Triplebyte, Facebook groups, VC internal job boards.

Channel 4: Networks of your early employees — Former colleagues of your first hires. A force multiplier that compounds with each hire.

### Late-Joining Co-Founder Equity Framework (This Week #8: Splitting equity with late-joining co-founders, favorite roadmap templates, and small changes that improve your org)
A framework from Hunter Walk (Homebrew) for determining equity grants for late-arriving co-founders and early team members at pre-funded startups

How it works: Three key components:

1. **Determine the CTO's value/role level:**
   - True co-founder: Start around 10% equity, up to equal share with existing founder
   - Senior engineer / founding team member (not co-founder): 1-5% of the company
   - These are averages; situations differ

2. **Structure the grant properly:**
   - Stock grant should be COMMON shares (not preferred)
   - Standard vesting schedule (typically 4 years with 1-year cliff)
   - Vesting protects the founder by giving time to evaluate the new team member
   - Any venture investor will likely begin a vesting schedule for existing founders too

3. **Consider termination protection:**
   - The CTO may ask: if terminated without cause before the vesting cliff, grant some negotiated amount of stock
   - Hunter considers this a fair ask since they're taking risk by starting work during the vesting period

### Managerial Leverage (Ben Horowitz)
A mental model for evaluating if an executive is performing well.

How it works: Criteria: If the CEO has to generate the ideas and push the department forward, there is zero leverage. If the executive tells the CEO what to do and how to push the company forward, there is high leverage.

### The 3 Executive Archetypes (Jonathan Lowenhar)
A model for categorizing leaders based on the stage of company growth they are suited to execute.

How it works: 1. Architect (builds the playbook from scratch by shadowing the founder), 2. Optimizer (takes the playbook, makes it efficient, and hits targets with a growing team), 3. Scaler (finds leverage to 10x the output and team size).

### The Lieutenant Sourcing Strategy (Gokul Rajaram)
A method for finding high-potential executive leaders.

How it works: 1. Identify 3-4 best-in-class companies at the specific function you are hiring for. 2. Build an org chart using LinkedIn. 3. Target the lieutenants (or lieutenants of lieutenants) reporting to the head of that function.

### Trust Is Given vs. Trust Is Earned Model (When they hired their first PM)
A mental model for how founders should approach trust with their first PM hire, contrasting two approaches and their outcomes

How it works: Trust Is Earned (Low Trust): Founders don't trust employees by default and expect them to earn respect over time. Employees operate at a disadvantage without founder-level commitment/experience. Gaps look like unsolvable character flaws. Mistakes feel like threats to safety. Employees tend to get fired. High churn, frustration, execs exit after a year or less.

Trust Is Given (High Trust): Founders choose to trust employees on principle. Gaps look like opportunities for coaching/feedback. Easy to repair mistakes and build increasing independence. Model is about the founder trusting themselves—to source right people, interview effectively, coach to high performance, promote or let go depending on fit. Founders trust themselves to deal with the discomfort of delegating product decisions.

Recommendation: Use the 'trust is given' model. Give plenty of feedback to build alignment over time. Plan for inevitable mistakes and have a plan to coach through them.

## Templates

### Co-Founder Check-In Framework (Rachel Lockett)
Structured check-in cadences (weekly, monthly, annual) with specific questions for co-founders to maintain relationship health, published with First Round.

How it works: Three cadences with relationship questions: Weekly check-in, Monthly check-in, Annual check-in. Core questions include: How's this working for you? Are we still aligned on vision and strategy? How are we working together? What am I doing that's frustrating you? What's gone unsaid that we need to talk about? Additional elements: Co-founder vows (how will we show up for each other?), Decision-making agreements, Conflict resolution approach, Regular in-person connection (dinner every other week, quarterly in-person). Setup exercise: Share strengths and weaknesses with each other, get feedback, identify gaps neither person covers.

### Co-founder Relationship Check-in (Ada Chen Rekhi)
A structured check-in plan for couples or close friends starting a business together.

How it works: Implement a 30, 60, and 90-day plan with explicit check-ins asking: 'Is this affecting our relationship?' Requires clear division of decision-making rights and a commitment to constructive conflict (attacking the problem, not the person).

## Checklists

### Advisor Interview Process (Elena Verna 3.0)
A step-by-step method for vetting potential advisors before bringing them on retainer.

How it works: 1. Identify a specific problem you are facing. 2. Pay the advisor for a one-off workshop. 3. Observe how they interact with your team and if they provide actionable patterns. 4. Hire on a monthly retainer if successful. 5. Evaluate every single month if they are still adding value.

### Co-Founder Conflict Resolution Steps (Healing your co-founder relationship)
Carole Robin's four-step summary for working through co-founder tension

How it works: If you are having trouble with your co-founder:

1. Stick with feelings (actual feeling words) — Identify and name your emotions precisely. Use a vocabulary of feelings. Don't say 'I feel that you...' (that's a thought, not a feeling).
2. Remain curious about the root causes — Explore whether the issue is organizational/external or behavioral/interpersonal. Don't jump to conclusions about the other person's motives.
3. Stay on your side of the net — Describe observable behavior and its impact on you. Don't attribute intent or make character judgments. Ask curious questions about what's driving their behavior.
4. Move into problem-solving, and make agreements — Find a superordinate goal you both care about. Answer three questions simultaneously: What do I need? What do you need? What's best for the organization? Treat solutions as trial periods with check-ins.

### Finding a Technical Co-founder (Summary: Lessons from working with 600+ YC startups | Gustaf Alströmer (Y Combinator, Airbnb))
Practical steps and principles for non-technical founders seeking a technical co-founder

How it works: Mindset reframe: Having an idea and looking for a technical co-founder is like having an idea for a song and looking for a musician to make it. Engineering is really hard and valuable.

Steps:
1. Start by asking the best technical people you already know if they're interested in starting a company with you
2. Ask those people for introductions to others who might be interested
3. Consider learning to code yourself (many successful founders have done this)

What doesn't work:
- Contracting an engineering team to build the product
- Reason: You can't spec your way to a great product; you have to be part of the iterations

Example: Calendly's success — a Ukrainian dev team built the first product and drove initial growth by using it within their firm and spreading it to others.

### Founder Prenup (Alisa Cohn)
A set of questions potential co-founders should discuss before starting a company.

How it works: Questions include: 1. What are your core values? 2. What is the vision of the company/what does success look like? 3. How do you handle conflict? 4. How do we decide when we disagree? 5. What kind of company culture do I think is important?

### Founding Team Evaluation Checklist (Evaluating a (marketplace) business idea)
Five characteristics of the right founding team for a startup

How it works: Look for:
☐ A+ founders
☐ Founders who deeply understand the customer's problem
☐ Founders who have a unique insight or an 'earned secret' about the opportunity
☐ Founders who have worked together before
☐ Founders who move fast

### Master Takeaways on Hiring Your Early Team (Hiring your early team)
Complete summary checklist covering all early hiring decisions for B2B startups

How it works: 1. Developers, developers, developers, developers.
2. If your founding team has enough horsepower to build the V1, consider hiring to de-risk, un-bottleneck, or lean into a part of the business that can differentiate you (e.g. customer success, subject-matter expert, design).
3. Within your first 10 hires, in addition to engineers and designers, consider a recruiter, a PM, or a GTM person.
4. To find your early employees, spend time in these four channels:
   a. Friends and former colleagues
   b. Cold outreach (mostly through LinkedIn and GitHub)
   c. Job boards
   d. Friends and former colleagues of your employees
5. To convince candidates to join your risky startup, invest in:
   a. A captivating vision
   b. An A++ early team
   c. Putting in the time
   d. Growing your network
   e. Love bombing

### Root Cause Diagnostic for Co-Founder Tension (Healing your co-founder relationship)
A list of questions to help distinguish between organizational/external causes and interpersonal/behavioral causes of co-founder conflict

How it works: When exploring why your co-founder relationship is fraying, ask yourself:

Organizational/External causes:
- How much of it has to do with organizational issues, shifting market conditions, or external factors?
- Are you faced with problems due to your very success?
- Has the excitement of meeting start-up challenges been replaced by the routine of a more established enterprise?
- Are you in disagreement about the company's future direction?
- Do some of you want to flip the company and others want to develop it for the long term?
- Are there disagreements about how fast to grow and in what direction?

Behavioral/Interpersonal causes:
- Does one of you perceive a significant power differential that complicates decision-making?
- Is one of you finding another especially difficult to influence?
- Have you discovered you have very different leadership styles?

Key distinction: Organizational issues can often be settled after full discussion or with departing founders leaving on good terms. Interpersonal/behavioral issues are harder because they feel more personal, but NOT raising them causes them to fester and complicates your ability to deal with business issues too.

## Examples

### Retool's Post-Seed Lean Team Strategy (Startup to exit: Lessons from a first-time founder)
Example of keeping the team minimal after raising seed funding to maintain agility

How it works: David Hsu kept Retool's team to only the co-founders for a full year after his seed fundraise. Logic: A nimble team can pivot quickly until achieving PMF. What's most important in the early days is to spend slowly until you find traction and have reason to pour gasoline on sales and product. Retool went on to become a low-code unicorn.

### Segment's First Customer Success Hire (Hiring your early team)
How Segment hired a unicorn customer success person as one of their earliest employees to handle technical support and free up co-founder bandwidth

How it works: Context: Four co-founders were round-robining support via Help Scout and Olark live chat. CEO Peter Reinhardt spent 8 weeks handling all support volume alone, which meant he couldn't do anything else. Hire profile: Jake Peterson had run his own analytics consultancy, had some technical ability to read code, wrote good docs, authored early blog content. He could handle most technical requests independently and synthesize new requests. Key insight: 'There's something really magical when you write in to a startup and they fix your issue within a few hours. It's something you'd never expect from a big company, and it was one way for us to differentiate.'
