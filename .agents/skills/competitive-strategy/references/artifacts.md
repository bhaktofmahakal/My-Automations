# Competitive Strategy - Frameworks, Templates & Checklists

*40 artifacts extracted from Lenny's Podcast and Newsletter*

## Frameworks

### 3 Pillars of Competition (Julia Schottenstein)
A codified philosophy for handling market competition.

How it works: 1. Hold true to vision (ignore noise/shade from competitors), 2. Grow the pie (work with partners to expand the market opportunity), 3. Lean into strengths (foster an ecosystem but hold ground on core standards).

### Battle Strategy for Moat-Building During Crisis (Leading your company through a pandemic - Issue 20)
Strategic mindset for companies with sufficient runway to use a downturn to build long-term competitive advantage

How it works: If you have the runway and ability:
1. Develop a battle strategy plan to build a moat during the crisis
2. Think about what the 'new normal' looks like
3. Plan to seize the opportunity now, for when things start to come back to normal
4. Don't focus only on the nitty-gritty of day-to-day survival
5. Goal: Come out of the crisis saying 'Here's what I've been working on this whole time, and now we're ready to seize the market'

### Build vs Buy Disruption Model for Vertical SaaS (Varun Mohan)
Why vertical/niche SaaS products are most vulnerable to AI disruption—domain experts can now build the 10% of features they actually need instead of buying 100% of a kitchen-sink product

How it works: Previous moat: Vertical SaaS companies had kitchen-sink features. Each customer only needed 10% but couldn't build/maintain custom software for just that 10%. What changed: Domain specialists (sales, legal, finance) now have access to build custom tools via AI coding assistants. Impact: Each customer can now build their exact 10% of features, more customized to their systems and integrations. Most vulnerable: Verticalized niche products (sales tools, legal software, finance software). Key insight: The SaaS companies existed not because of superior engineering but because their customers lacked engineering capability—that constraint is dissolving.

### Business Rules as B2B SaaS Moat (Shaun Clowes)
The insight that the real lock-in for B2B SaaS is not the UI or data model but the accumulated business rules and configuration that make the software specific to each customer.

How it works: Three layers of B2B SaaS value: 1) Data model (fields, objects) - modest value, 2) User experience - some value, 3) Business rules and configuration - THE real moat. Over time, software becomes less like the vendor's product and more like YOUR company's product. Even headless/agentic workflows need these rules. Agents must operate against the rules of the system. Implications: AI doesn't destroy these incumbents, it may make them stronger.

### Can't vs Won't Analysis for Competitive Moats (Roger Martin)
When assessing whether capabilities create durable advantage, ask both whether competitors CAN'T replicate them AND whether they WON'T replicate them

How it works: Two questions to assess capability durability: 1) CAN'T: Is there a structural reason competitors cannot replicate? (e.g., Westlaw's 100+ years of lawyer-written head notes would require 150,000 lawyers to replicate). 2) WON'T: Would replication require competitors to destroy their existing business? (e.g., Walmart wouldn't build e-commerce because it threatened 5,000 stores; Estée Lauder wouldn't put Clinique in mass channels because it would destroy prestige channel relationships; traditional airlines won't adopt Southwest's model because it means selling most aircraft, tearing up hub-and-spoke, firing travel agents). The 'won't' category aligns with Hamilton Helmer's counter-positioning and Porter's 'fault lines.'

### Chess Move Framework for Competition (Varun Parmar)
A mental model where every product release is evaluated as either a positive or negative chess move against competitors—products never remain the same, they only get better or worse relative to the competition

How it works: Core principle: Products never remain the same—every release makes the product better or worse from the customer's perspective. Since customers implicitly compare your product to competitors, every release is a chess move (positive or negative). Framework drives clarity on: 1) What you're building, 2) What impact it will have relative to competition, 3) Whether you're gaining or losing ground. Evaluate competition on three dimensions: solution quality (good enough?), distribution reach, and pricing/packaging.

### Competitor iceberg metaphor (Tanguy Crusson)
Mental model for why copying competitor features is a losing strategy

How it works: What competitors ship (features) is only the tip of the iceberg above water. Below the surface: research, customer understanding, strategy developed perhaps a year earlier. When you copy features, you're reacting to the manifestation of thinking from a year ago. Instead: check competition every 3 months max, disconnect creative/research process from competitors, anchor back to your own user interviews when team gets anxious about competitor moves.

### Horizontal Product Win Condition (Lessons learned from a startup that didn’t make it)
Conditions under which horizontal/platform products beat vertical solutions

How it works: Horizontal products win IF AND ONLY IF: A specific audience encounters enough use cases that they want ONE tool to address them all.

SUCCESS EXAMPLES:
- Figma won because it captured a much larger portion of an app designer's workflow than Sketch or Illustrator did (one buyer, many design tasks).
- Notion won because it addressed the vast array of documentation tasks faced by product managers in San Francisco (one buyer, many documentation needs).

FAILURE CONDITION: If each supported use case competes with a tailor-made vertical solution AND your buyer doesn't face enough diverse problems, vertical tools will win every time.

KEY INSIGHT: Customers do not care about your TAM. All they care about is their specific set of problems. Salivating over the aggregate TAM of all possible use cases is a founder trap.

WARNING: Horizontal tools often must survive a very long, uncertain journey toward PMF. During that time, customers will reject the product for ambiguous reasons, your team will second-guess strategy, and investors will tell you to pivot.

### Innovator's Dilemma Applied (Google Docs vs. Microsoft Office) (Sam Schillace)
Classic disruption pattern: enter a market the incumbent doesn't care about, trade most features for simplicity plus one killer new capability, and chip away from the bottom.

How it works: Incumbent (Office): High transaction cost (buy, install, maintain) → buyers choose the product with the most features → lock-in. Disruptor (Writely/Google Docs): Zero install, super convenient, stripped most features, added one transformational new feature (real-time collaboration). Trade-off was deliberate: 'We're going to take away most of the features, because we don't care about them that much.' Result: Google got market share, Microsoft kept the money. Sam notes the same pattern is available now with AI.

### Magnifying Glass Model of Strategic Power (Richard Rumelt)
A simple model for strategic action: you need a source of power, focus to concentrate it, and a target that can be affected.

How it works: Three elements required: 1) Source of power (the sun) — some asymmetry, advantage, resource, or knowledge you have that others don't. 2) Focus (the magnifying glass) — concentrating your efforts on a narrow point rather than diffusing across many targets. 3) Vulnerable target (the black thread, not white) — a challenge or opportunity that can actually be affected by your focused power. All three must be present. Missing any one means the strategy won't work.

### Meet the Bar and Be Different (Shaun Clowes)
The disruptive innovation playbook for startups: meet the average utility threshold of incumbents, then differentiate on a new dimension rather than competing feature-for-feature.

How it works: Most incumbents have overshot average utility. Startups can win by: 1) Meeting the bar on baseline features, 2) Being meaningfully different on a new dimension (e.g., data as first-class citizen in workflows). This is classic disruptive innovation applied to B2B SaaS.

### Niche Network Corollary to Disruption Theory (The Atomic Network)
An extension of Clayton Christensen's Disruption Theory applied to networked products: not only will the product look like a toy, but the initial target audience will look like a weird niche.

How it works: Original Disruption Theory (Christensen): Disruptive technologies undershoot user needs initially and are dismissed as toys.

Chris Dixon's framing: 'Disruptive technologies are dismissed as toys because when they are first launched they undershoot user needs.' Examples: telephone (only 1-2 miles), PC vs. mainframe, Skype vs. telecom.

Andrew Chen's extension: 'The next big thing will start out looking like it's for a niche network.' Not only does the product look like a toy, but the initial audience looks irrelevantly small. Atomic networks form in niches (teens, gamers, a single team) and it's not clear they'll reach mainstream.

Implication for evaluation: Underestimating niche networked products is 'the number one way to make dumb predictions in the tech industry.' The product doesn't need to change—the network needs to fill out to include relevant people.

Implication for builders: Nail the niche, establish an atomic network, then grow from there. Ignore market-size objections early on.

### Porter's Two Paths to Winning a Market (Differentiating your product)
Michael Porter's foundational framework that there are only two ways to win a market: operational effectiveness (doing the same things better) or differentiation (doing different things or the same things differently).

How it works: Two paths:
1. **Operational effectiveness** — Performing activities *better* than rivals. Necessary but rarely sufficient long-term because competitors copy and margins erode in a race to the bottom.
2. **Differentiating** — Performing activities *differently*, or performing *different* activities, than rivals. More sustainable because you're running a different race.

Key insight: 'Operational effectiveness means you're running the same race faster. But strategy is choosing to run a different race because it's the one you've set yourself up to win.' — Michael Porter

Even companies that initially win through operational effectiveness usually need to differentiate eventually.

### Power Progression (Hamilton Helmer)
A sequencing model that identifies which types of power are available at different stages of a company's lifecycle.

How it works: Startups should eliminate Branding, Process, and Resource power from their strategy. They should start with Counter-positioning during the substitution phase, and later develop Scale Economies, Switching Costs, and Network Economies as they scale.

### Self-Disruption Mindset (Innovator's Dilemma Applied) (Shweta Shriva)
A product leadership principle that market leaders must proactively disrupt their own products and business models before upstart competitors do, rather than defaulting to incremental improvements.

How it works: Key questions for product leaders: 1) Are we getting too complacent with incremental strategy? 2) What would a disruptive competitor build to take our market share? 3) Can we build that ourselves first? Example: Shweta built Amazon HoneyCode, a no-code app platform, in an infrastructure-focused company that had never entered that space.

### Strategic Advantage Identification Framework (The secret to Duolingo’s exponential growth)
A framework for finding and leveraging your product's unique strategic advantage, adapted from Good Strategy/Bad Strategy

How it works: Step 1: Identify a unique strength that your company/product has that aligns well with current opportunities in your environment.
Step 2: Determine how to leverage that strength in a way others cannot easily replicate.
Step 3: Focus on what sets you apart and use it to create a sustainable competitive advantage.
Step 4: Use the strategic advantage to point you toward specific product changes with outsized results.

Duolingo example:
- Unique strength: Users WANT to build a habit (unlike Instagram, TikTok, or Royal Match users)
- Leverage: This makes notifications well-received (users appreciate reminders), streaks valuable, and habit-building features effective
- Sustainable advantage: Led to market leadership in language learning and expansion into other learning categories
- Product direction: Invested heavily in notifications and streak systems

Recommended resource: Good Strategy/Bad Strategy by Richard Rumelt

### The Three Determinants of Business Value (Hamilton Helmer)
The exhaustive set of factors that actually create financial value in a company.

How it works: Every activity in a business that drives value falls into one of three categories: 1. Power (structural moats), 2. Market Size, 3. Operational Excellence (execution).

### True Competition Metric (Timothy Davis)
A method from PPC Hero for identifying genuine competitive threats using Google Auction Insights data rather than relying on anecdotal observations

How it works: Creates 4 new metrics from Auction Insights. Two key metrics: 1) Position above rate when we show. 2) Amount of times they show and we don't. Added together = how often a competitor ranks above you in all auctions you're available for. Use to: Establish baseline, track changes over time through bid strategy changes and optimizations, distinguish genuine threats from accidental close variants, and avoid reactive ego-marketing decisions.

### True Switching Cost Assessment (Lessons learned from a startup that didn’t make it)
A comprehensive model of switching costs that goes beyond time and money to include organizational and emotional factors

How it works: Teams typically think about switching costs as: time + money needed to install one solution and remove another.

TRUE switching costs include ALL of the following:
1. Installation time and money (the obvious ones)
2. Politics — internal power dynamics favoring the status quo
3. Emotions — comfort, familiarity, fear of change
4. Career ambitions — people whose careers are built on the existing tool will fight replacement
5. Esoteric business processes — deeply integrated workflows that are hard to replicate
6. Competing priorities — switching is never anyone's top priority
7. Sheer laziness — the default is to do nothing
8. Community and ecosystem — user communities, shared knowledge bases, support networks

VULNERABILITY MATRIX:
- Only USER is frustrated → Hard to win (users don't control budgets)
- Only BUYER is frustrated → Path to victory IF you can overcome switching costs
- Both frustrated → Best opportunity
- Neither frustrated → Don't bother

KEY INSIGHT: Many old-school companies seem ripe for disruption but are actually much stronger than they appear. Others appear strong but are secretly vulnerable. Only direct customer input reveals the true story.

## Checklists

### Competitive Analysis Output Components (Strategy Blocks: An operator’s guide to product strategy)
Three required components for the competitive and comparables analysis output.

How it works: 1. Head-to-head summary comparison of features and strengths of each player, with color coding or heat map to easily understand areas of strength and weakness.
2. Commentary on where each player seems to be focusing and their themes of investment.
3. Key feature deconstructs and screenshots.

### Differentiation Classics (Introducing the Foundation Sprint: From the creators of the Design Sprint)
A list of classic differentiator dimensions to score your product against the competition

How it works: Classic differentiators to evaluate (score your solution vs. competition on each):
- Faster
- Cheaper
- Better quality
- More convenient
- Simpler / easier to use
- More reliable
- Better designed
- More customizable
(Note: The exact full list is shown in the newsletter's image; these are representative based on context. Teams use dot voting to mark where their solution could stack up. Instructions say to be 'very optimistic and aggressive—but also realistic.')

### Moat / Defensibility Evaluation Checklist (Evaluating a (marketplace) business idea)
Seven types of competitive advantages to check for when evaluating a business's defensibility

How it works: Look for:
☐ Are there network effects
☐ Are there economies of scale
☐ Are there high switching costs
☐ Is there a potential for a strong brand
☐ Is there exclusive supply
☐ Is there proprietary technology
☐ Is there proprietary access (data, accounts, regulatory protection)

### Sources of Competitive Power / Asymmetry (Richard Rumelt)
A list of possible sources of power that create competitive advantage, compiled from Rumelt's discussion and book.

How it works: Ask: In what way is my company/team different? Sources include: 1) Reputation — trusted brand or track record. 2) Relationships — access to customers, partners, talent (e.g., Gerstner's IBM had relationships with every large corporation). 3) Knowledge asymmetry — knowing something others don't. 4) Being first — first to recognize something or enter a market. 5) User base / network effects — more users = more value (telephone systems, social media, Amazon). 6) Specialized focus — owning a niche that the market leader doesn't prioritize. 7) Institutional skill — organizational capabilities built over time. 8) Data advantage — more data to train on (e.g., Google search vs. Bing). 9) Talent attraction — being the place top people want to work (e.g., OpenAI attracting top AI researchers). 10) Inherited assets — things from your history that others can't replicate. Warning: Inherited strengths can become weaknesses when the environment changes (e.g., IBM's big-company customer base became a liability in the cloud era).

### The 'To Be or Not To Be' Test for Power (Hamilton Helmer)
A two-part criteria to determine if a company has true strategic power.

How it works: 1. Benefit: Does the business have a structural lower cost or higher price advantage? 2. Barrier: Is there something durable that makes it impossible for competitors to mimic that benefit?

## Examples

### Booking.com's Long-Tail Market Strategy (Top 5 most interesting things about Booking.com's early growth strategy – Issue 46)
How Booking.com competed against Expedia (100x bigger) by focusing on underserved secondary and tertiary destinations

How it works: Problem: No access to chain hotels in big markets (unlike Expedia, Orbitz)

Strategy:
1. Built inventory in secondary and tertiary destinations where competitors had no hotels
2. Achieved virtual monopoly in these markets since competitors couldn't/didn't compete there
3. Created landing pages with bounding boxes pulling all nearby hotels + map + POIs + search box
4. Constrained the marketplace view to make it look like they had much more supply than they did
5. Translated pages into many languages (Japanese, Korean, Russian, Arabic, etc.) with hundreds of translators on payroll
6. Where they couldn't compete geographically, they competed on non-English language queries

Result: When people landed on a city-specific landing page, they saw what appeared to be a comprehensive hotel marketplace — even though Booking.com had no supply elsewhere. 'No one knew we didn't have any supply anywhere else.'

### Competitive Wargaming Exercise (Annie Pearl)
A quarterly team exercise to deeply analyze competitors and present findings to the broader team.

How it works: Assign groups to own a specific competitor for a quarter. Conduct a SWOT analysis. Evaluate where the competitor is going and how it relates to the company's strategy. Culminates in a presentation day with prizes.

### DoorDash vs Postmates Strategy Comparison (Sarah Tavel)
Case study of how DoorDash's focused suburbs strategy outperformed Postmates' broad multi-city multi-category approach in the food delivery wars

How it works: Postmates: Went after all big cities simultaneously, expanded beyond restaurants to Apple/retail/bicycle delivery, spread thin across geography and category. DoorDash: Controversially focused on suburbs where no competitor operated, lost money initially on long delivery distances, but had no competition, made both sides of marketplace happy, could tip the market. Result: DoorDash became the market leader. Lesson: Focus ambition like a laser beam on a constrained market, not like the sun warming the ocean.

### DoorDash's Niche Market Foothold Strategy (Differentiating your product)
How DoorDash used Tier 2 and Tier 3 markets as a differentiation foothold before expanding to the broader market.

How it works: DoorDash started by focusing on Tier 2 and Tier 3 markets (suburbs, smaller cities) while competitors like Grubhub, Uber Eats, and Postmates fought over big cities. This gave them an underserved niche with less competition. They then used this foothold to expand to the broader market. Demonstrates the 'niche underserved market' differentiator as a launch strategy that can evolve.

### Incumbents That Win Despite Inferior UX (Lessons learned from a startup that didn’t make it)
Examples of products that retain market share despite being clunky or frustrating

How it works: Products referenced as examples of inferior products that still win due to switching costs and entrenchment:
1. Alteryx — clunky Windows desktop app, $5K+/user/year, but deeply integrated into processes with strong community moat
2. Bill.com
3. Concur
4. Salesforce

KEY INSIGHT: These forces (politics, emotions, career ambitions, esoteric business processes, competing priorities, laziness) ensure that inferior products can still win.

### Netflix UI vs. Blockbuster (Hamilton Helmer)
An example illustrating the difference between operational excellence (a shack) and true power (a castle).

How it works: Netflix spent massive resources optimizing their UI and recommendation engine. However, when Blockbuster pivoted to digital, they easily copied the UI. The UI was operational excellence (mimicable), whereas Netflix's true power was scale economies in content costs.

### Olay vs Clinique Counter-Positioning (Roger Martin)
Example of exploiting a competitor's fault line — Estée Lauder couldn't bring Clinique to mass channels without destroying their prestige business

How it works: Situation: P&G repositioned Oil of Olay upmarket into skincare (Olay ProV, Regenerist) in mass channels — essentially doing what Clinique did but at mass price points. Competitor threat: Clinique (Estée Lauder) could have killed Olay by entering mass channels. Why they didn't: Estée Lauder had Bobby Brown, Mac, Estée Lauder brand all in prestige channels — bringing Clinique to mass would have enraged prestige channel partners and damaged all their brands. Result: Olay became the biggest brand in all of skincare, overtaking Clinique. Key insight: Your biggest strategic friend can be your competitor's distribution channel constraints.

### Peregrine — Compounding Value via Public Safety Data Sharing (The unconventional Palantir principles that catalyzed a generation of startups)
Palantir-alumni-founded company demonstrating compounding value through software-defined sharing arrangements between public safety organizations.

How it works: Company: Peregrine (peregrine.io), founded by Palantir alumni.
Focus: Helping public safety organizations collaborate on real-time information.

Compounding feature: Different public safety organizations form software-defined sharing arrangements where authorized records can be shared without requiring someone to pick up the phone first. Agencies can share a living report with organizations that have the right to access the data but who aren't customers. Through this pathway, the new agency can see an easy pathway to joining the collective. When they do, sharing becomes even more compelling for original members, increasing product stickiness for all.

### Pets.com Failure (Failure)
A case study of an over-funded startup in a zero-sum market.

How it works: Raised $50M+ in 1999, engaged in an unwinnable arms race of TV advertising against two competitors, and shut down in 19 months, returning remaining capital to investors.

### Pets.com Overfunding Arms Race (Tom Conrad)
Three overfunded pet e-commerce companies each raised $50M+ and engaged in an unwinnable arms race on broadcast TV advertising, illustrating how excess capital can lead to irrational decisions

How it works: Three overfunded pet e-commerce sites ($50M+ each) engaged in arms race on national broadcast TV advertising. Zero-sum game thinking drove irrational spending. Company went from founding to IPO to shutdown in 19 months. Leadership chose to wind down and return remaining capital to investors rather than spend to zero. Key lesson: excess investment can be an albatross. Timing matters enormously—Chewy proved the model worked 15+ years later (acquired for $3B, now worth $9B).

### Rover Late Expansion into Dog Walking (What They'd Do Differently 🔮 Kickstarting and Scaling a Marketplace Business)
How Rover's delay in expanding into dog walking created an opening for Wag to capture the same demand base

How it works: Rover's demand base for dog sitting nearly entirely overlapped with dog walking needs. Dog walking had better economics. They delayed expansion due to competitive focus against DogVacay. Result: Wag launched and captured the adjacent market. Advice: If you have conviction in the opportunity, as soon as the org can do it — do it. Expanding at seed stage is probably a mistake when trying to focus, but once past that, if PMF is there, go for it.

### Stripe - 'Secret Sauce' Was Just First Principles (First-principles thinking)
How Stripe's competitive advantage was simply rethinking every piece of payments from the ground up

How it works: When asked 'What's your secret sauce?' by a VC friend, Greg's answer was simply: 'We just make payments really good.'

The real approach: 'We rethought every single piece of what we were doing from the ground up, from first principles. Not locked into the way that people had been doing it. We asked how should it be? Where's the pain and does it need to be there?'

Critical balance: Also become expert in how things have been done. 'If you just throw everything out, you're also just going to be starting from scratch, in a not-helpful way.'

### Vampire Attack: Sushiswap Forking Uniswap (A product manager’s guide to web3)
Example of how open-source code and composability eliminate traditional competitive moats in web3

How it works: Sushiswap forked Uniswap's open-source code and dropped tokens to all Uniswap users to jump-start usage — a 'vampire attack.' This illustrates that in web3, anyone can fork your product and compete on tech/product instantly. Combined with portable user data, there are no lock-in effects. Web3 PMs must continuously compete with better products, stronger communities, and new value rather than relying on IP moats or switching costs.

### Westlaw Competitive Advantage (Roger Martin)
Example of cumulative capability that creates an insurmountable barrier to entry

How it works: Product: Online legal search for litigators. Advantage: 100+ years of lawyer-written head notes summarizing every US legal case with proprietary keywords. Returns 5 relevant cases vs Google returning 500 that might matter. Cost to replicate: 150,000 full-time lawyers + new numbering/keyword system + 50 years of giving product free to law schools. Current annual maintenance: 1,500 full-time lawyers just for new cases. Result: Nobody has even tried to compete directly. Source: Roger's 14 years on Thomson Reuters board.

### Windows Mobile vs iPhone (Sriram and Aarthi)
Case study of Microsoft's Windows Mobile team dismissing the iPhone because 'carriers control the market,' despite team members instinctively knowing the iPhone was better

How it works: Sriram worked on Visual Studio for Devices (2005-2006), building developer tools for Windows Mobile. The internal codename 'Crossbow' was a weed killer (kills blackberries) — the whole strategy was competing with Blackberry via carrier relationships. When iPhone launched in 2007, everyone dismissed it because 'carriers won't allow it.' Lessons: 1) The market is bigger than your team — you can't overcome a bad market with great execution. 2) Trust your product instincts when something feels clearly better.
