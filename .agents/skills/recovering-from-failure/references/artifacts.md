# Recovering From Failure - Frameworks, Templates & Checklists

*62 artifacts extracted from Lenny's Podcast and Newsletter*

## Frameworks

### AFOG (Carole Robin)
An acronym used to reframe failures and mistakes into learning moments.

How it works: Stands for 'Another Fucking Opportunity for Growth'. Used as a prompt after a failure to immediately ask, 'So what did you learn?'

### Conviction Test for Persevere vs. Pivot (Scott Belsky)
A decision framework for founders questioning whether to continue: compare your current conviction (knowing everything you now know) to your original conviction. More conviction = stay. Less conviction = pivot or quit.

How it works: Ask yourself: 'In the beginning, before I knew what I know now, I had tons of conviction. Now knowing all I know — customer validation, product failures, market realities — do I have MORE or LESS conviction in the problem and solution?' If more: you're in the messy middle, stick with it. If less: quit, pivot, do something completely different. Caveat: don't make bold decisions on a bad day, but if conviction generally dissipates, be open to other options.

### Dalton Caldwell's Pivot Equation (The art of the pivot, part 2: How, why, and when to pivot)
A pseudo-code formula for deciding whether to pivot from Y Combinator's Managing Director

How it works: if ((how well things are working) / (# of months of concerted full-time effort)) < (excitement to work on another idea) * (confidence that you can find an idea that works better)) { you_should_pivot() }

### Dual Mindset for Startups (Commit and Shift) (Richard Rumelt)
The entrepreneurial skill of simultaneously being fully committed to your current bet while remaining willing to pivot when evidence shows it's not working.

How it works: Start with: A target market + a solution to that market's problem. Research finding: Initial target usually doesn't work. Successful startups pivot — different customer, slightly different product — over 1-2 years until something clicks. Required mindset: Be convinced you're going to win AND be willing to shift when things aren't working. This is a 'double-jointed exercise' — almost a human skill that some people have and some don't. Action agenda for startups = quick adaptation to changing conditions.

### Five Dimensions of a Pivot Analysis (The art of the pivot, part 1: The definitive list of successful pivots)
A structured framework for analyzing any startup pivot across five key dimensions, useful both for studying past pivots and planning your own.

How it works: For any pivot, analyze: 1. Why they pivoted — What signals or conditions triggered the decision? 2. How long it took to pivot — Timeline from initial product launch to pivot decision. 3. How they discovered the new idea — Was it a feature showing pull, internal tool, customer insight, or something else? 4. What they kept from the original idea — Did they retain technology, team, customers, brand, domain expertise, or nothing? 5. The story behind the pivot — Full narrative context and sequence of events.

### Five Patterns of Successful Pivots (The art of the pivot, part 1: The definitive list of successful pivots)
Five empirically derived patterns from analyzing 30+ startup pivots, serving as a mental model for founders evaluating their own pivot.

How it works: Pattern 1: Direction is one-way toward B2B — Companies only pivot INTO B2B, never out of it. B2B startups stay B2B. No successful B2B-to-B2C pivots found. Pattern 2: Timing varies widely but skews early — 25% of companies pivoted less than 3 months after launch. Median time to pivot was 1 year. Outliers: Notion took 4 years, Lyft took 5 years. Pattern 3: Many pivots kept nothing — Counter to Eric Ries's advice to 'keep one foot in the past and place one foot in a new possible future,' many successful pivots retained nothing from the original idea. Pattern 4: Narrowing to pull is the most common source — The majority of pivots emerged from founders narrowing focus to a single feature or piece of technology that was showing organic traction/pull. Pattern 5: Internal tools become the product — Over 25% of successful pivots came from finding pull for a tool the team built for themselves while building their original, unrelated business.

### Five Tips for Managing Communication About a Failed Project (Communicating bad news - Issue 26)
A five-step framework for how PMs should communicate negative project results to leadership and stakeholders, grounded in empathy and proactive leadership

How it works: Core principle: Imagine being on the other side of the table. Leaders want to know everything, as soon as possible, plus exactly how to move forward.

1. GET AHEAD OF IT: Make sure external stakeholders hear the bad news from you first. Don't let the first time they hear about your project be through the grapevine (e.g. 'Hey, did you hear that big project Jane came in to lead is tanking?').

2. HIGHLIGHT THE GOOD: Rarely is a project a 100% fail. Identify what good came out of the experience — what did you and the team learn? What will you now avoid in the future? Build confidence that you are learning and will avoid the same mistakes.

3. SHARE THE CONTEXT: When sharing results for the first time, remind leaders why they decided to give this a shot in the first place. Put together a brief deck (or doc) that includes: (a) Background on the project, (b) The initial hypothesis, (c) How it came together, (d) The positive qualitative feedback. Only THEN get into the results.

4. HAVE A CRYSTAL CLEAR POV ON WHAT TO DO NEXT: It is essential that you provide leaders with a recommendation for how to proceed. Be the solution to the problem, not just the messenger. Options to consider: Should you iterate one more time? Kill it? Ship it anyway and claw back the negative impact? You're in the best position to make a recommendation, and leaders will look to you to inform their decision.

5. YOU AND YOUR PROJECTS ARE NOT THE SAME THING: Don't build your identity around your projects (both internally or in the eyes of leaders). Projects come and go, some succeed, some won't. Create separation between you and your projects.

### Four Strategies for Finding a Better Idea (The art of the pivot, part 2: How, why, and when to pivot)
Four proven paths to finding a pivot idea, derived from analysis of 40+ startup pivots. Three of the four are about noticing what's already working.

How it works: Strategy 1: Go all in on ONE FEATURE that's showing pull. Ask: Is anything being used an order of magnitude more than anything else? Is there anything people love? Are people using any part in an unexpected way? Strip away everything else. Examples: Loom (screen recording), Notion (wiki/docs), Yelp (reviews), Pinterest (collections), Instagram (photos), Flickr (photos), Okta (identity). Strategy 2: Go all in on an INTERNAL TOOL or piece of tech that's showing pull. Something you built for yourself that others want. This was the MOST COMMON strategy. Examples: Amplitude (analytics tool), Slack (IRC + customizations), Shopify (e-commerce platform), Hugging Face (BERT port), Discord (chat feature), Segment (Analytics.js). Strategy 3: Go all in on an ADJACENT BIGGER MARKET showing pull. Listen to customers asking for enterprise/bigger use cases. Examples: Coinbase (wallet → buy button), PayPal (5 pivots in 15 months from encryption → email payments), Box (consumer → enterprise), Twitch (general streaming → gaming), Lattice (OKRs → performance management). Strategy 4: IDEATE INTERNALLY (only for ideation-phase startups). Organize hackathons, ask all employees, talk to everyone about their work pains. Look for big markets with low-NPS incumbents in a space you have experience with. Examples: Twitter (hackathon), Lyft (hackathon), Brex (YC partner brainstorming), Retool (pattern recognition across own projects), Vanta (talking to startups about security).

### Hypothesis-Driven Pivot Approach (This Week #7: Effectively communicating about a failure to execs, managing founder expectations, and hiring a Director of PM)
A method for systematically testing the assumptions behind a founder's conviction to determine if a pivot is warranted

How it works: Step 1: Listen carefully to what keeps the founder convinced the idea is great.
Step 2: Identify the specific assumptions behind their conviction.
Step 3: Together with the founder, design a set of cheap tests to vet each assumption.
Step 4: Ask the founder: 'What would convince you that maybe this isn't right?' (establish exit criteria).
Step 5: Run tests one by one, sharing results and takeaways after each.
Step 6: Maintain a running set of conclusions.
Step 7: At some point, you'll either run out of things to test, or you'll be able to demonstrate the idea is/isn't working.

For building the case:
- Work backward from what a compelling case for pivoting would look like
- Identify what data points you'd need (quantitative)
- Identify what user quotes you'd need (qualitative)
- Go collect that evidence systematically

### Managing Founder Expectations — 7 Tactics for Driving a Pivot (This Week #7: Effectively communicating about a failure to execs, managing founder expectations, and hiring a Director of PM)
Tactics for convincing a founder or CEO to change direction when the core product idea isn't working

How it works: 1. Bring them on the journey with you — Share key data points, experiences, and learnings as you experience them, with your takeaways. Always pair with 'here's what we're doing next to continue learning.'
2. Align on the key hypotheses and test them one by one — Listen to what keeps the founder convinced. Identify the assumptions behind their conviction. Together, design cheap tests to vet these assumptions. Ask: 'What would convince you that maybe this isn't right?'
3. Stay optimistic — Come across as being on the same side as the founder. Be the person who proposes hypotheses, suggests next steps after each test, and maintains a running set of conclusions.
4. Show them irrefutable data — Work backward from what a compelling case for pivoting would look like (what data points, what user quotes), then go get that evidence.
5. Have someone they trust convince them — It's hard for an IC to convince a founder of something they don't want to believe. Find a trusted advisor the founder respects, pitch them your perspective, and work together to influence the founder.
6. Give the founder a chance to save face — Frame it as evolution, not failure: 'You made a decision based on prior information, and it was the right one given what you knew, but now that we know more, it makes sense to change direction.'
7. Accept they may never change their mind — Recognize when a founder is unconvinceable. Step one: recognize it. Step two: decide if you want to continue working in that environment.

### Minus One to One Product Turnaround Strategy (Tomer Cohen)
A playbook for turning around underperforming products within large organizations where entrenched metrics and processes resist change

How it works: Steps: 1) Redefine the product's purpose (what it IS vs what it is NOT — e.g., 'not a springboard for other products'). 2) Build a unified cross-functional team (especially bringing AI/eng into the product team). 3) Carve out a small user cohort (e.g., 2M users) to build the new experience without disrupting company metrics. 4) Run experiments freely within the cohort including negative tests. 5) Once you have strong evidence of behavior change, use it to convince the broader org. 6) Scale to the full user base.

### Pivoting as Opportunity Cost (The art of the pivot, part 2: How, why, and when to pivot)
Dalton Caldwell's mental model for thinking about pivoting as getting more shots on goal to find product-market fit

How it works: Core insight: Pivoting gets you more shots on goal to find product-market fit. 'If you made something and you launched it and it's like, Meh, not really working, a dang good reason to pivot is you get another roll of the dice. I've seen people use these opportunities really well. It's much easier to be lucky when you get half a dozen shots on goal than one.'

### Scott Belsky's Conviction Test (The art of the pivot, part 2: How, why, and when to pivot)
A simple diagnostic question to determine if you should pivot or persevere, used by Adobe's CSO in conversations with founders

How it works: Ask yourself: 'How much conviction do you have in the solution you're building?' In the beginning, before you knew what you know now, you had tons of conviction (that's why you started). Now, knowing all you know, do you have MORE or LESS conviction in the problem and solution? If MORE conviction: 'You're just in the messy middle. Stick with it. This is par for the course.' You've gotten validation from customers, you just haven't figured it out yet. If LESS conviction: 'Then quit. Your life is short. You have a great team. Pivot. Do something completely different. If you've lost conviction, you should not be doing what you're doing in the world of entrepreneurship.'

### Seven Options When Your Product Isn't Taking Off (What to do if your product isn’t taking off)
A sequential framework of seven actions a founder can take when their startup or product isn't gaining traction, roughly ordered from easiest to most drastic

How it works: 1. Talk to more users (actually do it) — Talk to 5-10 potential customers this week, looking for pain and pull signals
2. Change your target audience/ICP — Try pitching a different, narrower group of people
3. Change your positioning/messaging/pitch — Reframe how you describe your product without changing the product
4. Try a 'kickstarter' to get the word out — Use unscalable tactics to acquire first 1,000 users
5. Find something that IS working and pivot fully to that — Identify a feature showing strong pull and go all-in
6. Give it a bit more time — PMF takes longer than expected (B2B median: 2 years, B2C: 6-18 months)
7. Give up and move on — Sometimes quitting is the right and smartest move

### Strategic Inflection Points (Drew Houston)
A mental model for identifying when market dynamics permanently shift, requiring an all-in pivot.

How it works: Based on Andy Grove's 'Only the Paranoid Survive'. Involves asking: 'If we were consultants to ourselves, what would we do?' and then putting all eggs in one basket rather than hedging bets.

### The Hill Climb Metaphor (Ami Vora)
A narrative tool to explain strategic pivots and manage team expectations during difficult transitions.

How it works: Use this metaphor to explain the shift from a local optimum to a global optimum. Acknowledge that the team is currently on a comfortable hill, but there is a bigger mountain in the distance. Set the expectation that to reach the mountain, the team must first go down into the valley (a period of slog, trade-offs, and difficulty) before they can climb higher.

### Three Options When You Lose Conviction (Startup to exit: Lessons from a first-time founder)
Framework for deciding what to do when you lose belief in your market or product

How it works: When you lose conviction in your market, you have three options:

1. **Shut Down**: Let go of your team and close your doors. Simplest but returns nothing.

2. **Restart**: If you still have cash remaining, start from scratch on something totally new. Challenges: after years focused on one problem, you must restart with a likely downsized team. Hard to pull off but there are success stories (e.g., Slack was a failed gaming startup that morphed its in-game communications technology into an enterprise tool).

3. **Sell/Acquisition**: Most desired by founders but least in their control. Benefits: returns for shareholders, landing spot for team, product lives on.

Critical advice from Louis Beryl: 'It sounds like you've lost belief. The worst thing you can do now is do nothing. What you do is up to you, but by the next time we talk, you should have made a move.'

Note: Having a team makes pivoting harder — team size limits the products you can pivot to if you want to keep everyone. This is another reason to stay lean pre-PMF.

### Three-Bucket Business Triage (Leading your company through a pandemic - Issue 20)
Framework to quickly categorize your business into one of three crisis modes to determine strategy

How it works: Categorize your business into one of three buckets:
1. You are seeing growth → Accelerate mode (land grab for market share, CAC is low, LTV is forever, broad audience prospecting)
2. You are falling apart → Survival mode (pause prospecting, focus on retention)
3. You are chugging along → Tweak mode (pivot messaging around how businesses are working now)

Kevin Barry's data on B2B SaaS distribution: ~20% in growth markets, ~20% in hard-hit markets, ~60% doing OK.

### Two Signs It's Time to Pivot (The art of the pivot, part 2: How, why, and when to pivot)
Two clear signals from data analysis of 40+ pivots that indicate you should strongly consider pivoting

How it works: Sign 1: Persistent Lukewarm Interest. Indicators: very low retention, consistent growth plateau, largely lukewarm interest in your idea, AND you don't have any more good ideas to drive growth. Timeline: give it 2-3 months during ideation phase, 6+ months if you have a live product with meaningful traction. Sign 2: Realizing It's Never Going to Be as Big as You Thought. This is more qualitative—with time and experience building the thing, you realize your idea is simply not good enough to justify the investment (team size, capital raised, opportunity cost).

### Two Types of Pivots Framework (The art of the pivot, part 2: How, why, and when to pivot)
A classification system for understanding which type of pivot situation you're in, which determines your timeline and approach

How it works: 1. Ideation Pivots: Early-stage startup changes idea before having a fully formed product or meaningful traction. Characteristics: easy to make, happen quickly after launch (typically within 3 months), new idea often completely unrelated. Examples: Brex (VR → business banking), Retool (Venmo for UK → no-code internal tools), Okta (reliability monitoring → identity management), YouTube (dating site → video streaming). 2. Hard Pivots: Company with a live product and real users/customers changes direction. Characteristics: you keep one element and double down on it, typically happen within 2 years (most around 1-year mark). Examples: Instagram (check-in app → photo sharing), Slack (game → internal chat), Loom (user testing tool → screen recording).

## Checklists

### Freemium-to-Paid Recovery Playbook (Lessons from going freemium: a decision that broke our business)
Steps Equals took to recover from a failed freemium experiment, applicable to any SaaS reverting from free to paid

How it works: Steps to recover from failed freemium:

1. Kill the free plan — stop new free signups
2. Don't force existing free users to pay retroactively (honor the original promise)
3. Introduce a time-limited free trial (e.g., 14 days) instead of indefinite free
4. Require credit card upfront to create commitment and urgency
5. Re-introduce critical onboarding friction (e.g., mandatory data source setup) that drives users to the aha moment
6. Keep self-serve access if it aligns with your product vision (don't necessarily go back to sales-only)
7. Shift North Star metric back to revenue/ARR from vanity engagement metrics
8. Refocus the entire company on who to support, build for, and message to — paying customers

### Pivot Timeline Decision Points (The art of the pivot, part 2: How, why, and when to pivot)
Key milestones and check-in points for evaluating whether to pivot based on analysis of 40+ startup pivots

How it works: 1. At the 3-month mark after launch: Have a hard conversation with your co-founder. If ideation stage and things aren't clicking, strongly consider changing the idea. If things show promise, re-commit. 2. At the 6-month mark (for live products): If you see persistent lukewarm interest (low retention, growth plateau) AND you're out of good ideas to drive growth, it's probably time. 3. At the 1-year mark: Schedule a yearly check-in. If things are clicking, full speed ahead. If things feel 'meh,' spend a few days talking about other potential directions. Most hard pivots happen around this mark. 4. At the 2-year mark: The small number of companies that took longer to pivot likely regret not changing course earlier. 5. Remember: You're probably waiting too long. The 'next feature fallacy' is real—a few additional features rarely change everything.

### Questions to Ask When Looking for a Pivot Idea (The art of the pivot, part 2: How, why, and when to pivot)
Diagnostic questions to identify pivot opportunities within your existing product or through exploration

How it works: For Strategy 1 (Feature showing pull): Is anything in your existing product being used an order of magnitude more than anything else? Is there anything about your product that people love? Are people using any part of your product in an unexpected way? For Strategy 2 (Internal tool): Have you built anything internally that other companies or users are asking about? Did you build a tool for yourself that solves a broader problem? For Strategy 3 (Adjacent market): Are customers asking for enterprise/bigger use cases? Is there a larger market segment with the same foundation but different needs? For Strategy 4 (Ideation): Have you organized hackathons? Have you asked all employees for ideas? Have you talked to everyone you can about their work pains? Are there big markets with low-NPS incumbents in a space you have experience with?

### Seven Signs It Might Be Time to Give Up (What to do if your product isn’t taking off)
Evaluation criteria for deciding whether to shut down a startup or product

How it works: 1. Steve Jobs mirror test: 'If today were the last day of my life, would I want to do what I am about to do today?' If the answer has been 'No' for too many days in a row, you need to change something.
2. Scott Belsky conviction test: 'Do you have more or less conviction in the problem and the solution you're building?' If you'd say 'If I knew then what I know now, I would not have done this'—then quit.
3. You have an instinct that this isn't going to work. Trust your instinct.
4. You've run out of good ideas.
5. You don't want to work with your co-founder any longer.
6. You're running out of money.
7. You're exhausted.

### Startup Failure Warning Signs Checklist (Lessons learned from a startup that didn’t make it)
Warning signs derived from Cascade's experience that indicate a startup may be heading toward failure

How it works: CHECK YOURSELF AGAINST THESE WARNING SIGNS:

□ Your ICP can accommodate wildly different use cases without a framework to accept or reject them
□ You justify a broad approach by thinking about aggregate TAM across all use cases
□ Your competitive thesis is primarily 'their product is old and clunky'
□ You assume the product will speak for itself against an entrenched competitor
□ Your earliest deals are all driven by founder relationships rather than inbound demand
□ Things get a lot more difficult once founders step out of the room
□ You're reading investor interest as validation of your strategy (not just a bet on the team)
□ Your target buyer's role is shifting in ways that reduce the diversity of problems they face
□ Each of your use cases competes with a dedicated vertical solution
□ You've been on a horizontal product journey for 3+ years without clear PMF signals
□ Your team second-guesses strategy regularly
□ Customers reject the product for ambiguous reasons

### Three Legitimate Reasons to Keep Going (Not Pivot) (The art of the pivot, part 2: How, why, and when to pivot)
Valid excuses to stay the course even when things look uncertain

How it works: 1. You're building complex tech that simply takes time. 2. You have a clear vision of what needs to exist in the world, and you need to see it through. 3. You're having a lot of fun and aren't concerned about the business outcome.

### Three Routes to Quitting (What to do if your product isn’t taking off)
Practical options for winding down a startup once you've decided to stop

How it works: 1. Try to sell your company. Difficult these days. First Round has a guide on running an M&A process as a founder.
2. Keep trying new ideas until you run out of cash. Sometimes it's best to know you did every possible thing you could.
3. Stop now and give your remaining money back to your investors. Most investors prefer this versus running out of cash.

## Examples

### Airbnb COVID-19 crisis turnaround (Sanchan Saxena)
How Airbnb went from planning an April IPO to losing 90%+ of revenue in six weeks, then recovered through crisis management principles.

How it works: Timeline: January 2020 — planning IPO in April with road show deck. February/March — countries shutting down one by one, travel dashboard showing zero bookings country by country. Brian initially: 'We built this for a crisis, we have $2B in bank.' Six weeks later: laid off 1,900 employees, raised $2B in debt at 50% valuation cut, revenue dropped to single-digit % of prior year. Recovery tactics: 1) Two-week planning cycles, 2) Dissolved all sub-teams into one #Airbnb team, 3) Moved engineers/PMs across functions as needed, 4) Pivoted marketing — 'In an Airbnb, the air you breathe is only you and your family' vs hotels with shared lobbies, 5) Shifted to belief obsession, 6) Honest/vulnerable leadership communication. Outcome: successful IPO later that year.

### Airbnb Plus (Failure)
A case study of jumping to a solution that doesn't fit company strengths or unit economics.

How it works: Attempted to solve user trust by physically inspecting homes (a heavy operational solution) instead of using platform strengths like reviews, lockboxes, or cleaner partnerships, leading to broken unit economics.

### Airbnb Plus Launch Failure (Jiaona Zhang)
A case study on the dangers of jumping to solutions and ignoring unit economics when chasing a big vision.

How it works: Airbnb tried to solve user trust by manually inspecting homes (Airbnb Plus). It failed because it ignored unit economics and Airbnb's core strength (platform vs operations). The better, scalable solution was leveraging existing reviews or sending lockboxes.

### Artifact App Shutdown Case Study (Mike Krieger)
A real-world example of evaluating when to shut down a product based on market headwinds and effort-to-reward ratios.

How it works: Three criteria that led to shutdown: 1. Deteriorating mobile web ecosystem (ads/popups ruining the reading experience). 2. Lack of organic, word-of-mouth sharing for news compared to photos. 3. Friction of fully remote work during hard pivots. The ultimate metric was the '10 units of input for 1 unit of output' energy drain.

### B2B Startup Pivot Origins (How to validate your B2B startup idea)
A list of famous B2B companies and the very different ideas they started with before pivoting to their current products

How it works: Original ideas of successful B2B startups:
- **Retool** → Started as Venmo for the U.K.
- **Amplitude** → Started as a voice recognition app for sending/receiving text messages by talking to your phone
- **Segment** → Started as a university classroom lecture tool
- **Vanta** → Started as B2B Alexa
- **Notion** → Started as a no-code website builder
- **Loom** → Started as a marketplace for companies to hire subject-matter experts
- **Slack** → Started as a game called Glitch
- **Box** → Started as a 'box' to put photos and content in on Facebook

Key stat: ~40% of B2B startups pivoted at least once (some up to 10 times), compared to ~20% in B2C.

### Bing social integration failure (100 person-years) (Ronny Kohavi)
Microsoft spent 100 person-years integrating Twitter and Facebook into Bing search. Hundreds of experiments were run, all negative to flat. Eventually aborted after ~1.5 years.

How it works: Integrated Twitter firehose and Facebook into Bing. 100 person-years invested. Hundreds of experiments, none breakthrough. Ronny sent statistics to Qi Lu showing it was time to abort. Continued a few more months before final abort. Similar failures at Netflix (social) and Airbnb (social/friend stays). Key lesson: fair to try big bets but use data to know when to stop.

### Bravado Flex - Pivoting Business Model in a Downturn (Sahil Mansuri)
Case study of how Bravado created a fractional/commission-only sales marketplace when their full-time recruiting business slowed, resulting in their best month ever

How it works: Problem: Full-time sales recruiting marketplace slowing dramatically—companies freezing hiring, candidates flooding market. Innovation process: Whiteboard exercise—'If I'm a customer in this world, what would I need?' Insight: Companies still want revenue growth but can't commit to full-time hires. Candidates can't find full-time jobs but have skills and networks. Solution: Bravado Flex—marketplace for non-full-time sales work. Models include: 100% commission, contract-to-hire, fractional work, small stipend plus milestones. Result: Went from massive slowdown to best month in company history. Surprise learning: Many sales reps actually prefer fractional work—they can work for 3 companies, skip meetings and CRM updates, use broader tool set. Key principle: 'You can't optimize your way out of a problem. You have to completely change the rules of the game.'

### Cascade Startup Post-Mortem (Lessons learned from a startup that didn’t make it)
Complete case study of Cascade's journey from founding to soft landing, with specific details on what went wrong

How it works: COMPANY: Cascade (cascade.io)
FOUNDED: 2019
SHUT DOWN: 2023 (soft landing)
FUNDING: $5.3M seed round from First Round Capital, Redpoint
TEAM SIZE: 10
FOUNDERS: Both repeat entrepreneurs with prior exits

PRODUCT: No-code tool for non-technical teams to import, analyze, and visualize large data sets and present findings via interactive data apps. Sat at intersection of no-code wave and data space.

TARGET COMPETITOR: Alteryx — Windows-only desktop app, $5,000+/user/year, ~$1B annual revenue, only player in its esoteric market.

WHAT WENT WRONG:
1. ICP too broad: 'nontechnical business analysts using Excel to crunch big data sets' — didn't specify WHY they were using Excel or the value of the problem
2. ICP fray: Ended up serving scooter company (location data), HR team (employee data), retailer (distribution analysis) with no framework to accept/reject
3. Horizontal trap: Each use case competed with vertical solutions (Samsara, Onfleet, etc.)
4. Market shifted: Business analyst role changed — big data work moved to technical data teams
5. Underestimated switching costs: Alteryx power users had career-defining expertise; community moat was strong
6. False signal: Early deals driven by founder relationships, not market pull
7. Assumed product would speak for itself against entrenched competitor

MARQUEE CUSTOMERS: Had some, but couldn't find path to breakout growth.

### Codeium Pivot from GPU Infrastructure to AI Coding (Varun Mohan)
Codeium pivoted overnight from a profitable GPU virtualization company ($2M+ revenue, 10K+ GPUs managed, 8 people, free cash flow positive) to an AI coding product when generative models made their infrastructure thesis invalid.

How it works: Before: GPU virtualization and compiler software, $2M+ revenue, managing 10K+ GPUs, 8 people, FCF positive. Trigger: Mid-2022, generative models made bespoke classifier models obsolete (why train custom sentiment classifier when GPT-N can do it?). Decision: Pivot overnight (not quarters). After: Used inference infrastructure to build Codeium (free autocomplete for developers), trained own models, scaled to enterprise customers like Dell and JPMorgan Chase. Key lesson: They went in with the assumption that model architectures would be heterogeneous—when everything converged on transformers, their infrastructure thesis broke.

### Etsy Studio Launch and Kill Decision (Tim Holley)
Case study of launching and shutting down a craft supplies marketplace that didn't align with new strategic priorities

How it works: Concept: Intersection of Pinterest-style inspiration + craft supply marketplace (like Michael's) + tutorials. Brand fit was strong. Launch coincided with 2017 strategic pivot. Case study of Atlassian's failed attempt to win enterprise communications market, leading to sale of HipChat/Stride to Slack

### Instagram Close Friends: From Failure to Success (Robby Stein)
A 2-3 year journey of fixing a failed feature through root cause analysis, renaming, redesign, and understanding the emotional job to be done

How it works: Initial failure: Called 'Favorites' → mistranslated as 'best friend' in many markets → users added only 1 person → probability of getting a DM reply was near zero → product was broken. Also tried mixing Close Friends posts into the feed (confusing) and hiding the green ring inside the story (unclear). Fix process: 1) Identified the job was emotional (feeling connected to friends) not just utility. 2) Found that 20-30 person lists worked because 2+ people would reply to DMs. 3) Renamed to 'Close Friends' to encourage larger lists. 4) Built algorithmic list builder recommending people. 5) Moved green ring to outside of story circle for visibility. 6) Removed feed integration, kept only in Stories. 7) Tested in Australia first. Result: Became one of the most popular Stories features.

### LinkedIn Feed Turnaround (Tomer Cohen)
Case study of transforming LinkedIn's feed from a promotional activity tracker to an engaging knowledge-sharing platform

How it works: Problem: Feed was promotional/activity-based, used as springboard for other products. Strategy: Redefined purpose to 'people that matter talking about things I care about professionally.' Unified the AI team with the feed team. Carved out 2M member cohort to experiment freely. Shifted algorithm objective from CTR to downstream conversations. Outcome: Dramatic behavior change in cohort, eventually scaled to all users. LinkedIn now has 7 hires/minute, 140 hours of learning every minute. Key insight: Competing for right views, not volume.

### Mixpanel Refocus: 40% Churn to 10% Churn (Vijay)
Case study of Mixpanel cutting products to refocus on core analytics, resulting in retention going from 60% to 90% and NPS from 16 to 50

How it works: Context: By 2018, ~40% revenue churn. Action: 1) Hard no to messaging and data infrastructure products. 2) Entire engineering team refocused on analytics. 3) Threw away all existing plans. 4) Sorted churn reasons by ARR descending. 5) Top 10 became the roadmap. 6) Gave every engineer direct customer access. ### PayPal's Five Pivots in 15 Months (The art of the pivot, part 2: How, why, and when to pivot)

### Pivot Stories from Successful Companies (What to do if your product isn’t taking off)
