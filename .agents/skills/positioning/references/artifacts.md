### 2x2 Differentiation Chart (Loserville Framework) (Introducing the Foundation Sprint: From the creators of the Design Sprint)
A specific way to build a 2x2 competitive positioning chart where you aim to be alone in the top-right quadrant while competitors form an L-shape in the other three quadrants

How it works: Structure:
- X-axis: One of your two chosen differentiators
- Y-axis: Your other chosen differentiator
- Goal: Position your product ALONE in the top-right quadrant
- Competitors should fall into the other three quadrants, forming an 'L' shape (dubbed 'Loserville')

Rules:
- Focus on CUSTOMER PERCEPTION, not just objective features
- Be honest — differentiation only works if you can deliver what you promise
- Keep experimenting with different differentiator combinations until you find ones that achieve the desired positioning
- If you can't get alone in top-right, reconsider your differentiators

### 5-Step Positioning Framework (April Dunford, EOY Review)
A sequential method to define product positioning starting from alternatives to market category.

How it works: 1. Identify competitive alternatives (including status quo/spreadsheets). 2. List unique capabilities you have that alternatives don't. 3. Translate capabilities into differentiated value themes. 4. Define target account characteristics that care about this value. 5. Choose a market category that makes this value obvious.

### 6-Step Consumer Business Kickstart Framework (How to kickstart and scale a consumer business—Step 3: Craft your pitch)
Lenny's comprehensive six-step framework for kickstarting and scaling a consumer business

How it works: Step 1: INSIGHT — Come up with your idea
Step 2: AUDIENCE — Identify your super-specific who
Step 3: HOOK — Craft your pitch (this post)
Step 4: REACH — Find your early adopters by doing things that don't scale
Step 5: RETAIN — Iterate until enough people stick around
Step 6: SCALE — Build your growth engine

Each step builds on the previous one. Following the steps doesn't guarantee success but increases odds.

### April Dunford's 5-Step Positioning Framework (Summary: April Dunford on product positioning, segmentation, and optimizing your sales process)
A sequential methodology for figuring out product positioning in B2B SaaS. Each step builds on the previous one, creating a coherent positioning story.

How it works: Step 1: Competitive Alternatives — Who do you have to beat to win a deal? Identify the status quo (spreadsheets, pen and paper, interns, existing tools like Zendesk). In B2B, ~40% of deals are lost to 'no decision' which means you lost to these status quo alternatives.

Step 2: Key Unique Attributes — What makes you different? Identify key product capabilities, features, or functions that the competitive alternatives don't have.

Step 3: Translate into Value — Bucket your unique attributes into themes of differentiated value. These are the meaningful outcomes your differences enable.

Step 4: Target Customers Who Care — What are the characteristics of a target account that make them really care a lot about that differentiated value? Define the segment that cares most.

Step 5: Market You Win — What's the market context you position the product in that makes your value obvious to the people you're going after?

Timeline: The exercise takes approximately one week, depending on how contentious the existing internal alignment is.

### Cocktail Party Test for Positioning (Rahul Vohra)
Observing how users naturally pitch your product to others at cocktail parties to validate your positioning

How it works: Watch how users describe your product to others in informal settings. For Superhuman, the pitch was: 'Dude, you have to use it, it's really f***ing fast.' If the natural pitch aligns with your intended positioning, you've validated it. Three validation tests for positioning: 1) Is it unique and available? 2) Does it reinforce your product strategy? 3) Does it pass the cocktail party test?

### Cultural Erogenous Zones (Lulu Cheng Meservey)
A mental model for finding what an audience naturally cares about and bridging your message to it, rather than trying to change their worldview.

How it works: Identify what your natural audience cares about (X). Identify what you are offering (Y). Create the API/bridge from X to Y to convince them to care about your offering.

### Damming the Demand (Christopher Lochhead)
A competitive strategy where a company intercepts existing market demand by reframing the problem, rather than competing directly on product features.

How it works: Identify the status quo behavior (e.g., road biking). Identify the inherent risk or flaw in the status quo (e.g., getting hit by a car). Introduce a new category that solves the flaw while delivering the core benefit (e.g., indoor spinning classes). Shift the narrative from 'our product is better' to 'why risk the old way when you can do this new way'.

### Early-Stage Positioning Timing Framework (Fish Net Analogy) (Summary: April Dunford on product positioning, segmentation, and optimizing your sales process)
Decision framework for when to invest in formal positioning work relative to product-market fit stage

How it works: Pre-PMF stage:
- Don't nail positioning too early
- Allow the market and customers to pull you
- Stay flexible with broad positioning
- Analogy: If you're selling fish nets and your thesis is 'only for tuna,' if it fails, it fails hard. Instead, position for 'all kinds of big fish' and see where it goes. Maybe it's good for grouper.

Trigger to invest in formal positioning:
- You start seeing common patterns among customers
- Example patterns: 'They all have Zendesk, are 50-people strong, and are yet to hire their COO'
- These repeating characteristics signal it's time to formalize positioning

The 5-step positioning exercise itself takes about one week, depending on how contentious internal alignment is.

### Elevate a Profession, Not a Category (Zoelle Egner)
Instead of creating a new software category (Gartner quadrant), elevate or create a professional identity that people will fight to defend

How it works: Key insight: A job is an identity; a software category is a budget line item. People fight for identities, not line items. Examples: Gainsight creating 'customer success' as a profession; companies rebranding/elevating 'DevOps.' Approach: 1) Identify an existing or emerging profession your product serves. 2) Hold it up as important for broader business metrics. 3) Create community for those people that's about the job, not about you. 4) Become part of the identity of that career. Avoid: Trying to get your own Gartner quadrant or niche category—huge lift, often not how buyers actually work.

### Eyes Light Up (Wes Kao)
An audience reading technique where you watch for genuine visceral reactions (eyes lighting up, leaning forward) versus polite nodding to identify which parts of your message truly resonate

How it works: Steps: 1) Notice when someone's face/demeanor changes during your pitch or explanation, 2) Note what you were saying at that moment — the keyword, phrase, or angle, 3) Look for patterns across conversations (e.g., this person lights up when I talk about numbers, upside, or low effort), 4) Cut parts that make people go 'dead in the eyes', 5) Double down on parts that make eyes light up. Works for sales pitches, presentations, internal meetings, and conversations with managers.

### Feature Walkthrough vs. Positioning-Oriented Pitch (Anti-pattern vs. Pattern) (How to build a killer sales pitch)
A contrast framework showing the default anti-pattern (feature walkthrough) and why it fails, versus the improved positioning-oriented approach.

How it works: ANTI-PATTERN — Feature Walkthrough:
- Log in to product
- Methodically walk through as many features as possible
- Little talk about which features are unique vs. common to every solution
- Customer left to determine why features matter
- Rarely any discussion of other potential approaches
- Sometimes includes: company background slide, case study, customer logos
- Sometimes starts with a vague 'problem' slide that any competitor could also claim to solve
- Result: Does not help prospect decide why they should choose your product over alternatives

PATTERN — Positioning-Oriented Pitch:
- Starts with unique market insight (your point of view)
- Teaches about alternative approaches and their trade-offs
- Builds to a 'perfect world' description the prospect agrees with
- Features are ONLY shown in context of the value they deliver
- Designed to answer: 'Why pick us versus the other approaches?'
- Includes discovery throughout — it's a conversation, not a monologue
- Result: Helps customers make confident purchase decisions

### Foil-Based Positioning (Bad Alternative vs. Good Alternative) (How to kickstart and scale a consumer business—Step 3: Craft your pitch)
A comparison framework for isolating your product's differentiating element by contrasting the existing bad alternative with your good alternative

How it works: Steps:
1. Identify the 'bad alternative' — the current way users solve the problem (e.g., taxis, traditional brokerages, Blockbuster)
2. Identify the 'good alternative' — your product
3. Isolate the differentiating element — the specific thing that makes your solution not just better but different

Examples from Julian Shapiro's comparison table:
- Compare the existing solution's weaknesses with your product's unique strengths
- Use that gap as the foundation of your pitch

Arielle Jackson calls this finding your 'foil' — a well-known reference point that helps people immediately understand what you're NOT and therefore what you ARE.

### Four Strategies to Craft Your Hook (How to kickstart and scale a consumer business—Step 3: Craft your pitch)
A four-part framework for developing a compelling consumer product pitch/hook. Lenny advises trying all four and iterating.

How it works: Strategy #1: What's UNIQUE about your product?
- List products your users currently use to solve the same problem
- Compare those alternatives with your product
- Identify what's not just better but DIFFERENT about your solution
- Use those unique attributes to craft your pitch
- Examples: Netflix (no late fees), Robinhood ($0 commission), Amazon (millions of books at low cost), WhatsApp (fast, clean experience)

Strategy #2: How do your USERS describe your product?
- Pick your 10 most obsessed users
- Ask them how they describe your product
- Note what words they use and what functionality they focus on
- Mold those words into a pitch
- Examples: Dropbox ('Throw away your USB drive' — users called it a USB drive replacement), Behance ('exposure'), DoorDash ('Revenue for your restaurant, risk-free')

Strategy #3: What JOB is your product doing for people?
- Apply the Jobs-to-Be-Done framework
- Identify what users are 'hiring' your product to do
- Match user needs with your product: I need X + we have Y = magic
- Examples: iPod ('1000 songs in your pocket'), Uber ('Tap a button, get a ride')

Strategy #4: What about your product is likely to GRAB people's attention?
- Think of something bold and very specific
- Lead with an irresistible or curiosity-inducing claim
- Examples: Zillow ('How much is my home worth?'), Duolingo ('Learn a new language for free while translating the web'), Everydae ('Ace the SAT with 10 min/day'), Domino's ('30 minutes or free')

### Impact Communication Spectrum (Individual → Village → Country) (How GiveDirectly increased donations by over $3 million/year through experimentation)
A framework for deciding the right level of specificity when connecting users/donors to the impact of their action.

How it works: Levels of specificity (from most to least operational cost):
1. Individual matching: Highest personalization but highest operational cost (content gathering, QA, donor inquiries). GiveDirectly found NO conversion lift and only short-term retention benefits. Cost: 20+ hours/month of product team time + field team visits.
2. Village/group matching: Still compelling and specific. 58% higher email CTR and 2x positive responses vs. country-level. Much lower operational cost—no individual content gathering needed.
3. Country/program level: Lowest cost but least engaging.

Recommendation: Start with village/group level. Test before investing in individual-level matching. The incremental engagement from individual matching may not justify the operational burden.

### JTBD Pitch Formula: I need X + We have Y = Magic (How to kickstart and scale a consumer business—Step 3: Craft your pitch)
A simple formula for matching user needs with your product capabilities to create a resonant pitch

How it works: Formula: I need X + we have Y = magic

Steps:
1. Identify the core need or job your user has (I need X)
2. Identify what your product provides that fulfills that need (We have Y)
3. Combine them into a pitch that directly connects the user's need to your solution

### Messaging Abstraction Levels for Copywriting (SEO keywords, career ladders, backlog tools, copywriting, OnlyFans, AMA with Pete Kazanjy and much more)
A framework for mapping product benefits across multiple levels of abstraction to find the right message for each audience.

How it works: Principle: Messaging is far more important than exact wording. Map out benefits across multiple levels of abstraction, then figure out the right level for each audience. Example chain: Level 1 (Feature): Analytics tool has better automation functionality. Level 2 (Functional Benefit): You can finish all of your reporting faster. Level 3 (Strategic Benefit): You can spend more time on more strategic tasks. The lower the level, the more feature-focused; the higher the level, the more outcome/value-focused. Match the abstraction level to where your audience is in their journey and what they care about.

### Messaging Research via Wayback Machine (Kickstarting supply in a labor marketplace)
A technique for researching and optimizing marketplace messaging by studying how competitors' messaging evolved over time

How it works: Process:
1. Start with the principle that 'everything starts with language' (James Currier, NFX)
2. Set up landing pages and Facebook ads
3. Use the Wayback Machine (web.archive.org) to study the messaging used by every known labor and non-labor marketplace
4. Track how their messaging changed over the years to identify what evolved and what stuck
5. Use these insights to inform your first Facebook ad tests

Result: Incredible Health's first Facebook tests were 'quite successful and cost-efficient' because their messaging was 'influenced by predecessors.'

### Pitch Diagnostic: Three Reasons Your Pitch Isn't Working (How to kickstart and scale a consumer business—Step 3: Craft your pitch)
A troubleshooting framework for when your product pitch isn't resonating with anyone

How it works: If you've been pitching your product and no one seems to care, one of three things is happening:

1. Your pitch is bad
- This happens all the time; only in hindsight is the right hook obvious
- Netflix took 18 months to find the right combination; DoorDash took a handful of conversations
- Action: Go back and try a different strategy to come up with a new hook

2. You're pitching the wrong people
- Your product may be awesome and valuable but you haven't found the right audience yet
- Example: Pinterest's Ben Silbermann spent time pitching the wrong people before finding design-minded users
- Action: Revisit your super-specific who

3. Your product is not something people want
- The toughest pill to swallow
- Action: Be honest with yourself, seek outside feedback, consider pivoting

### Positioning Definition (Positioning)
April Dunford's concise definition of positioning that captures all the key elements.

How it works: "Positioning defines how your product is a leader at delivering something that a well-defined set of customers cares a lot about."

Key elements in the definition:
- 'leader at delivering' — you must be demonstrably the best at something
- 'something' — a specific, differentiated value
- 'well-defined set of customers' — not everyone, a targeted segment
- 'cares a lot about' — the value must matter deeply to those customers

Positioning is NOT: messaging, a tagline, a brand story, a vision, your 'why', or 'everything marketers cook up.'

### Positioning Statement Anti-Pattern (Positioning)
Why the traditional positioning statement (Mad Libs fill-in-the-blanks) is ineffective and potentially dangerous for product positioning.

How it works: Traditional positioning statement template: 'FOR [target customer] WHO [statement of need or opportunity], [product name] IS A [product category] THAT [statement of key benefit]. UNLIKE [primary competitive alternative], [product name] [statement of primary differentiation].'

Why it fails:
1. Assumes there is only ONE answer for each blank and you simply 'know' what it is
2. Most products could be positioned in MULTIPLE different market categories
3. Different categories mean different competitors, different value, different customers
4. The exercise would NOT have helped discover that a 'Microsoft Access killer' was really an 'embeddable database for mobile devices'
5. April Dunford calls this exercise 'not only pointless but potentially dangerous'

### Positioning as Context Setting (Positioning)
A mental model for understanding what positioning does — it sets off assumptions in customers' minds about competitors, features, target users, and pricing, just like the opening scene of a movie orients the audience.

How it works: Positioning works like the opening scene of a movie — it answers the big questions (Where are we? What's happening? How should I feel?) so customers can focus on the details.

When you position a product in a market, customers automatically make assumptions about:
- **Who your competitors are** (e.g., 'CRM' → they assume Salesforce)
- **What features your product should have** (e.g., 'CRM' → tracking deals and accounts)
- **Who the product is for** (e.g., 'CRM' → head of sales)
- **What the product should cost** (e.g., 'CRM' → less than Salesforce)

Good positioning = sets off assumptions that are TRUE about your product.
Bad positioning = sets off assumptions that are NOT true, forcing sales and marketing to undo the damage.

Example: Positioning the same product as 'email' vs. 'chat' creates completely different expectations. Email → spam filtering, organization, calendar integration. Chat → instant delivery, read receipts. Great chat is lousy email and vice versa.

### Positioning vs. Messaging vs. Branding Hierarchy (Summary: April Dunford on product positioning, segmentation, and optimizing your sales process)
Framework for understanding the relationship between three commonly conflated concepts

How it works: Hierarchy (must be done in this order):

1. Positioning (foundational layer): Defines how your product is the best in the world at delivering some value that a well-defined set of companies care a lot about. Must be figured out first.

2. Messaging (second layer): 'The text on the homepage.' You can't write this until you understand the value and for whom. Positioning is a fundamental precursor to messaging.

3. Branding (third layer): What the brand stands for. You can't figure this out until you know the target buyer and the differentiation for the brand to be distinct.

Key insight: These are distinct concepts. Most companies try to do messaging or branding work without having positioning nailed, which leads to ineffective output.

### Product Market Story Fit (Paul Adams)
An extension of product-market fit that includes the narrative used to explain the product.

How it works: Ensure you have the right product (what), the right market (who - people with a high-energy problem), and the right story (how you explain why it's better). A failure in the story will kill a great product in a great market.

### Sell the Horseback Riding, Not the Saddle (Product Positioning) (Essential reading for product builders—part 2)
A positioning framework from Stewart Butterfield's internal Slack memo: sell the transformation your product enables rather than the product's features.

How it works: The Acme Saddle Company thought experiment:

Option A (Selling saddles): Sell on leather quality, adornments, range of styles/sizes, durability, or price.

Option B (Selling horseback riding): 
- Grows the market for your product
- Gives perfect context for talking about your product's features
- Positions you as the category leader
- Opens different marketing and promotion opportunities (e.g., sponsoring programs, working on related causes)
- Lets you think big and potentially be big

Always choose Option B—sell the outcome and transformation, not the tool.

### Seth Godin's Purple Cow / Remarkable Test (How to kickstart and scale a consumer business—Step 3: Craft your pitch)
A mental model for evaluating whether your pitch/product is remarkable enough to succeed

How it works: Core principle: 'Competition is so fierce that you have to create something remarkable in order to succeed. Something remarkable is worth talking about. Worth noticing. Exceptional. New. Interesting. The opposite of remarkable is very good.'

Test: Is your hook something people would remark about to others? If it's merely 'very good,' it's not enough. It needs to be worth talking about, worth noticing.

### Seven Ways to Differentiate Your Product (Differentiating your product)
A comprehensive framework of seven distinct differentiation strategies a product team can use to stand out in a crowded market, with the option to combine multiple strategies for stronger positioning.

How it works: Seven differentiation strategies:

1. **Be the cheapest** — Offer the lowest price. Sub-variants: (a) be the cheapest option (ARCO, Geico, Wish), (b) be completely free (Robinhood, Chime), (c) make customers money (BlockFi, Compound). Warning: tough long-term because it's essentially operational effectiveness.

2. **Be the highest quality** — Sell the best experience or product (Apple, Gucci, Whole Foods, Tesla, MasterClass, Peloton). Justifies premium pricing.

3. **Be the most convenient** — Offer the easiest, fastest, most frictionless option (Coinbase, TurboTax, Eaze, Gopuff). Push the convenience lever as far as possible.

4. **Be the safest** — Become the most trusted, secure, or private option. Safety can mean physical safety (Volvo), trust, or privacy (DuckDuckGo, Apple, Signal).

5. **Sell something proprietary** — Offer exclusive supply or content no one else has (Netflix exclusive content, Airbnb exclusive homes, Cameo exclusive celebrity access). Innate differentiation.

6. **Sell something that makes people feel great buying** — Differentiate through mission, values, or emotional resonance (Patagonia's environmental mission, Toms' buy-one-give-one, Nike's athlete identity). Works best combined with other differentiators.

7. **Focus on a niche underserved market** — Laser-focus on a small segment competitors ignore (Wish: value-conscious shoppers in Florida/Middle America, DoorDash: Tier 2/3 markets, Chime: $35K-$70K earners). Can be a foothold to expand or a permanent strategy.

**Bonus: Combine differentiators** — Layer multiple strategies for stronger positioning:
- Patagonia: Highest quality + makes you feel good
- Apple: Highest quality + safest
- Airbnb & Uber: Cheapest (initially) + proprietary supply
- Etsy: Proprietary supply + niche market
- Figma: Highest quality + most convenient

### Strategic Narrative Framework (Andy Raskin)
A 5-step storytelling structure to pitch a product or company by defining a movement rather than just a problem and solution.

How it works: 1. Name the shift (old game to new game). 2. Name the stakes (show winners already playing the new game to make it life or death). 3. Name the object of the new game (a simple rallying cry or question). 4. Show the obstacles (reframe problems as barriers to the new game). 5. Present the magic gifts (how your product overcomes the obstacles).

### The 5 Components of Positioning (April Dunford's Positioning Flow) (Positioning)
A sequential methodology for defining product positioning by working through five interdependent components in a specific order, starting with competitive alternatives.

How it works: The five components must be worked through in this specific order:

1. **Competitive Alternatives**: What would customers do if your solution didn't exist? This includes doing nothing, using a spreadsheet, manual processes, or hiring someone. Ask: 'What would a customer do if our offering didn't exist?'

2. **Differentiated Features/Key Unique Attributes**: What do you have that the alternatives do not? List the features or capabilities that are unique compared to the competitive alternatives identified in step 1.

3. **Value for Customers**: For each differentiated feature, ask 'So what for customers?' What is the value those capabilities enable for buyers? Translate features into outcomes.

4. **Target Customer Segmentation (Customers Who Care)**: Among buyers who care about your value, which care the MOST? What are the characteristics of a customer that makes them care a lot about your differentiated value? This defines your best-fit customers.

5. **Market Category (Market You Intend to Win)**: The context you position your product in such that your value is obvious to your target customers. This is the market you intend to win.

The flow is sequential: Competitive Alternatives → Differentiated Features → Value → Customers Who Care → Market Category. Each step depends on the previous one.

### The Bar Test (Arielle Jackson)
A role-play exercise to ensure positioning copy sounds like natural human speech.

How it works: Pretend to be a target user at a bar talking to another target user. Say: 'Hey, I just started using [product]. It's this really great [category] that [benefit].' If it uses jargon like 'leverages' or 'empowers', it fails. It must be something a human would actually say out loud.

### The Dummy Explanation (Raaz Herzberg)
A principle for creating product messaging that assumes zero prior knowledge from the audience, avoiding internal jargon and bubble thinking

How it works: Rules: 1. Never assume knowledge about your company or product. 2. Never use internal terminology (e.g., 'Wiz Graph Database') without explaining it. 3. Don't use category acronyms (e.g., CNAP) — check what customers actually Google (e.g., 'cloud security solution'). 4. Remember you're in a bubble — your audience doesn't live in your product every day. 5. Don't change messaging too quickly — you're sick of it, but your customers are just starting to grasp it. Test: Would a common person in your market, who has never heard of your company, understand every word?

### The Fishing Net Analogy (April Dunford)
A mental model for keeping positioning loose in the early days of a startup.

How it works: Instead of launching a net strictly for 'tuna' (and failing if tuna don't bite), launch it as a net for 'big fish'. See what you catch (e.g., grouper), and once a pattern emerges, tighten the positioning around the proven catch.

### The Magic Triangle (Christopher Lochhead)
A mental model for building a legendary company by balancing three critical pillars simultaneously.

How it works: The three pillars are Product, Company, and Category. All three must be designed and developed equally at the right time; a great product will fail if the category is not designed alongside it.

### Two-Part Sales Pitch Structure (Setup + Follow-Through) (How to build a killer sales pitch)
A sales pitch framework that replaces feature walkthroughs with a two-part structure: the Setup (giving customers context to make decisions) and the Follow-Through (proving differentiated value). Developed by April Dunford based on IBM's pitch methodology, adapted for startups and tested with 200+ B2B companies.

How it works: Part 1 — THE SETUP (gives customers a way to think about the entire market):

1. INSIGHT: What does a customer need to know to understand why your differentiated value is important? This is your unique point of view on the market — not a generic problem statement or trend. It must be something competitors cannot also claim.
   - Example (Help Scout): 'Customer service is a growth driver, not a cost center.'
   - Example (Postman): 'Low-quality and difficult-to-consume APIs are putting customer experience and revenue at risk.'

2. ALTERNATIVE APPROACHES (Pros & Cons): Lay out the alternative approaches (not individual products) to the problem and teach the customer the advantages and disadvantages of each. This positions every alternative including the status quo. This is also the place for sales discovery.
   - Example (Help Scout): Approach A = Shared inbox (easy to start, but you'll outgrow it). Approach B = Traditional help desk (full-featured but hard to use, designed to reduce costs not deliver great experience).
   - Example (Postman): Approach = Siloed set of individual tools (lets teams choose task-specific tools, but makes it impossible to govern API quality across the lifecycle).

3. THE PERFECT WORLD: Conclude the discussion by describing what a perfect solution would look like, given the trade-offs just discussed. Ask the prospect: 'Would you agree with this?' If yes, the sale is yours to prove. If no, disqualify.
   - Example (Help Scout): 'We think what e-comm businesses really need is an easy-to-use tool that has advanced features so they never outgrow it, but was designed from the ground up to provide an amazing customer experience. Would you agree?'

Part 2 — THE FOLLOW-THROUGH (focused on differentiated value and proof):

4. DIFFERENTIATED VALUE: Show how you deliver each element of the 'perfect world' — this can be a live demo. Only show features in the context of the value they deliver. End with case studies demonstrating business outcomes and ROI.
   - Example (Help Scout): Show ease of use → Show advanced features (won't outgrow) → Show great customer experience features → Case studies on customer loyalty and revenue.
   - Example (Postman): Show integrated toolset and governance → Show collaboration → Show API consumer experience → Case studies with ROI.

Key principles:
- The setup should take only a few minutes but is crucial for context.
- The setup should be a conversation, not a monologue. At each step ask: 'Does this resonate? What are you using today? Have you considered other options?'
- The pitch answers 'Why pick us versus the other approaches?' not 'Look at all our features.'
- Never talk about features outside the context of the value they deliver.

### Three Common Positioning Traps to Avoid (Positioning)
Three mistakes companies commonly make when going through the positioning process, with guidance on how to avoid each one.

How it works: Trap 1: Defining competitive alternatives as any possible competitor
- WRONG: Listing every company that could theoretically compete with you
- RIGHT: Ask 'What would a customer do if our offering didn't exist?'
- The answer might be 'do nothing' which really means: stick with current way (spreadsheet, manual process, hiring an intern)
- In enterprise software, 25% of deals are lost to 'no decision' — you must position against the status quo

Trap 2: Creating 'phantom competitors'
- WRONG: Listing companies that theoretically could compete but you never actually see or lose to in deals
- RIGHT: Only include competitors your customers actually see as alternatives
- This is not a test of your internet research skills
- Product team can monitor phantom competitors as future threats
- If you start seeing them in deals, adjust positioning then
- Including phantom competitors waters down and weakens your positioning

Trap 3: Assuming you must create a new market category to grow
- WRONG: Defaulting to creating a new category (inventing a 'fluflommer')
- RIGHT: Consider positioning in an existing market category first
- 90% of tech companies that went public in the past five years positioned in existing markets
- Category creators often lose long-term to companies that gained footholds after the hard work was done (Google vs. Ask Jeeves, Facebook vs. Myspace)
- Salesforce was a niche CRM play until hundreds of millions in revenue
- Gainsight was in survey software until hundreds of millions in revenue
- Most successful startups start in an existing category and expand/redefine later when they have money and momentum

How it works: Look for opportunities to reinforce the value proposition in the physical world. Gojek drivers weaving through traffic in branded gear showed stuck commuters exactly what the service could do for them.

### Positioning Increases WTP Examples (The ultimate guide to willingness-to-pay)
Three examples showing how product descriptions and positioning dramatically change willingness-to-pay

How it works: 1. YELLOW BEAR FIGURE: Someone paid $51 for a seemingly worthless yellow bear figure because the story/description made it feel scarce (Significant Objects project).

2. SFOGLINI PASTA: Pasta described as 'sauceable' commands an 80% higher price. The adjective implies quality. If marketing asked customers 'What's the most you'd pay for pasta?' sauceability wouldn't factor in.

3. MIRO: 'How much would you pay for a virtual whiteboard?' Probably not $960/year. Yet Miro charges $80/month for a team of 10 by positioning as 'the visual workspace for innovation.'

Key insight: Customers don't have predetermined WTP - they decide in real time based on available information. You shape their POV through positioning.

### Retool Outbound Email Pivot (Nabeel S. Qureshi)
An example of changing product framing to find product-market fit.

How it works: Retool initially framed their product as 'supercharged Excel' and got no traction. They changed their outbound email subject line to 'build internal tools easily' and immediately got replies from CTOs, proving the value of pivoting messaging to match customer pain points.

### Segway Launch Failure (April Dunford)
An example of how refusing to define a specific market category and target customer leads to product failure.

How it works: Segway hyped as a 'revolution in human transport' without defining if it replaced a car or a bike. Resulted in user confusion (honked at on roads, yelled at on sidewalks) because the context and target use case were never established.

### Segway Positioning Failure Case Study (Summary: April Dunford on product positioning, segmentation, and optimizing your sales process)
Example of how vague positioning ('Revolution in human transport') created unrealistic expectations and customer disappointment

How it works: Company: Segway
Positioning claim: 'Revolution in human transport'
Problem: People got excited because they imagined a flying car. When they saw the actual product, they were disappointed because they saw something like a moped and didn't know what to compare it to.
Lesson: Overly ambitious or vague positioning without clear competitive context leads to mismatched expectations and customer confusion.

### Snickers vs. Milky Way (Bob Moesta)
A case study demonstrating how two functionally identical products have completely different competitive sets based on the customer's context.

How it works: Snickers is hired for energy/meal replacement and competes with coffee or Red Bull. Milky Way is hired for emotional comfort/regrouping and competes with wine or brownies.

### The 'Double Down' Positioning Case Study (Jason Cohen)
An example of how changing product positioning from 'saving money' to 'driving growth' allowed a company to charge 8x more.

How it works: Instead of pitching 'We cut your $40k AdWords cost in half' (which caps pricing at ~$5k to ensure the client saves money), pitch 'We double your leads for the same $40k spend'. The client is already willing to spend $40k for X leads, so they will pay $40k to get 2X leads, increasing the SaaS price from $5k to $40k.

### Typeform Cautionary Tale: Broad vs. Narrow Targeting (How to kickstart and scale a consumer business—Step 2: Identify your super-specific who)
How Typeform's initially broad positioning led to hypergrowth then stagnation, and how narrowing to a specific persona reignited growth.

How it works: Phase 1 (Broad): Positioned as 'democratizing data collection for everyone.' Cast a wide net, huge TAM, attracted SMBs. Market perception: some value to everyone but not deep enough in any single use case.

Result: Hypergrowth for 3-4 years, then growth slowed.

Phase 2 (Narrow): Dug into data, focused GTM on specific personas with specific JTBD. Discovered most valuable persona in terms of usage and LTV was 'a marketer using Typeform to grow their business.'

Lesson: What got them through the first phase wasn't going to get them to the next level. Narrowing the focus was required to reignite growth.

### Waze Value Proposition Discovery — Certainty Over Time Savings (Uri Levine)
How talking to U.S. users revealed that certainty of arrival time was more valuable than avoiding traffic.

How it works: Original vision: Help people avoid traffic jams. Reality: More traffic jams exist today than in 2007. User insight (from U.S. market): 'I don't want to change my route. I just want to know how long it'll take.' Conclusion: Certainty creates way more value than saving time. Users in Cupertino said they'd stay on 101 — just tell them how long. This reframed the core value proposition.

### Zip's Weekly Pitch Refinement Process (Prioritizing at startups)
How Zip iterated on their value proposition and mapped feature requests to core problems

How it works: Process: (1) Revisit customer pitch and investor pitch (in a simple Google Doc) on a weekly basis. (2) Modify it as understanding of the problem/solution evolved. (3) Write out what they believed to be the core problems Zip solves. (4) Log every single request that any prospect or customer had. (5) Map each request to the list of core problems. (6) Decide whether to build or skip based on that mapping.
