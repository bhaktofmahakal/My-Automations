# Evaluating Startup Ideas - Frameworks, Templates & Checklists

*107 artifacts extracted from Lenny's Podcast and Newsletter*

## Frameworks

### 'Here Be Dragons' Effect (Why now)
Alexander Taussig's framing for why investors ask 'why now'—if the idea has been tried before and failed, what's different this time?

How it works: Concept: For every good idea, there are probably dozens of founders over the years who have attempted to build a company around a similar premise. Most of them are like ships that sailed into uncharted waters and never returned. We don't really know what happened to them. It follows that if you're working on an idea, someone probably tried it before and failed, so what's different now? The 'why now' question is an attempt to figure out why this time the ship won't sink.

### 'Why Now' Time Horizon Strength Model (Why now)
A mental model for evaluating how strong a 'why now' is based on how recent the underlying change is—the more recent, the stronger the current

How it works: Concept: Think of 'why now' in terms of time horizons. The more recent the change, the stronger the current.
- Strongest: Very recent shifts (e.g. COVID-19 drove Zoom, Peloton, Clubhouse to new highs)
- Medium: Ongoing multi-year trends (e.g. move to the cloud continues to spur new businesses but has less impact on whether any individual business succeeds)
- Weakest: Long-standing macro trends (e.g. SaaS broadly)

Implication: If you can catch an existing large current and execute like crazy, you'll still have a shot at building a big business, even if the current isn't brand new.

### 4 Pillars of Early-Stage Startups (Julia Schottenstein)
A mental model for evaluating whether to invest in or join a startup.

How it works: 1. People (Do you trust the CEO to lead? Can they paint a vision while knowing the details?), 2. Market (Is it growing? Is there space for a new entrant?), 3. Product (Is there a spark of enthusiasm from users?), 4. Distribution (Do they have an unfair advantage in ecosystem or sales?).

### AI Investment Thesis: Use AI vs. Be AI (Sam Lessin)
Framework for distinguishing between companies that use AI as infrastructure (investable) versus companies branded as AI companies (avoid)

How it works: Key distinctions: 1) Not using AI = like not using the cloud in 2010 (insane), 2) But being 'the AI [blank]' company = going to zero, 3) AI companies are too capital-intensive for seed math to work, 4) Even OpenAI only returned ~25x at seed — mediocre for seed, 5) AI is fundamentally commoditizing, 6) Look instead for: cultural implications of AI, businesses disrupted by AI, the 'Voight-Kampff test' problem (real vs. bot), 7) Invest in businesses where AI is propellant, not the core value proposition

### AI Market Segmentation Framework (Bret Taylor)
A mental model for evaluating startup opportunities in the AI space.

How it works: 1. Frontier/Foundation Models: Avoid as a startup; requires massive CapEx and scale. 2. Tooling/Pickaxes (Data labeling, evals): Viable but risky; highly susceptible to being obviated by hyperscaler updates. 3. Applied AI/Agents: The best opportunity; functions like modern SaaS, focuses on workflows, and drives measurable business outcomes.

### Bill Trenchard's 'Why Now' Spectrum (Why now?)
A spectrum for evaluating the strength of a 'why now' from literal impossibility without it to trend-riding

How it works: Two levels of 'why now' strength:

**Level 1 — Tightest definition (strongest):** The company literally couldn't have existed without the 'why now.' Platform, technology, or regulatory shifts are examples. E.g., Uber would not have been possible without smartphones + GPS. Salesforce wouldn't have been possible without maturity of web browsers + widespread broadband.

**Level 2 — Trend riding (still valuable):** The company clearly could've existed without these trends but wouldn't have been nearly as successful. Great companies can ride one or more trends to success even without a literal dependency.

### Brett Berson's 18-Month Evaluation Framework (Lessons from 140+ angel investments)
Evaluate startups based on their ability to reach near-term milestones rather than speculating about long-term TAM

How it works: Instead of asking 'Can this be a $10B company?' (fuzzy, speculative), focus on the next 18 months:
- Strength of the founding team
- How many customers already love the product
- Early signs of market pull
- Can they successfully pass through 'basecamp' (near-term milestone)?

This transforms the decision from speculating about moat and TAM to assessing concrete, evaluable factors.

### Coda's Two Litmus Tests for Starting a Company (How to validate your B2B startup idea)
Shishir Mehrotra's framework for deciding whether to start a company, requiring both a compelling idea AND a compelling co-founder

How it works: Two litmus tests for starting a company:

1. **Do you have an idea you can't imagine not working on?**
2. **Do you have a person you can't imagine not working with?**

You need BOTH. Common failure modes:
- 'Me and my buddy are going to start a company' but don't know what it'll be about → Works sometimes, but not often
- 'I've got this great idea' but haven't convinced anyone to join → Probably not that great an idea

Coda's additional commitment test: They made a list of the first 4 people they wanted to hire. 'If all of them say yes, we're starting the company.' All said yes.

### Core Startup Success Formula (What to do if your product isn’t taking off)
Lenny's distillation of what it takes to build a successful business

How it works: To build a successful business, you need to solve a big enough problem for a big enough group of people to make a profit. Everything in this post is a way to help you find a bigger problem or a bigger group of people.

### Decade Litmus Test (Ryan Hoover)
A personal commitment test for founders: can you see yourself working on this idea for a decade?

How it works: Before committing to a startup, ask: 'Do I see myself working on this for a decade?' You probably won't work on it that long (it will likely fail or evolve), but if you can't even imagine 10 years, you probably don't care enough. It's a litmus test for passion and commitment, not a prediction.

### Delta 4 Framework (Kunal Shah, How the most successful B2B startups came up with their original idea)
A mental model to evaluate if a new product will succeed by comparing its efficiency score to the existing solution.

How it works: Steps:
1. Ask: What would a customer rate the *existing* solution on a scale of 1 to 10?
2. Ask: What would they rate *your* solution on a scale of 1 to 10?
3. Calculate the delta between the two ratings.

Rules:
- Your solution needs to be +4 better (delta of at least 4) for anyone to care about your product.
- If the existing solution gets a 6 or higher, it will be very hard to replace (e.g., Excel).
- The sweet spot is when existing solutions rate low (1-5) and yours can rate 4+ points higher.

### Earned Secret (Team Evaluation) (Evaluating a (marketplace) business idea - Issue 31)
A concept for evaluating whether a founding team has unique, hard-won knowledge that gives them an unfair advantage in building a specific product

How it works: The 'Earned Secret' framework: Evaluate whether the founding team has done something in their past — tried to solve some hard problem — and learned something about the world that not a lot of people know. This earned insight gives them a unique advantage in building this particular business. Ask: What did this team learn from their past experience that others don't know? Why are they uniquely positioned to solve this problem?

### Experiment Framing for Side Projects (Ryan Hoover)
Framing new ideas as 'experiments' rather than 'side projects' or 'startups' to remove success pressure and focus on learning.

How it works: Instead of calling it a startup or even a side project, call it an experiment. The goal of an experiment is not success—it's to learn. This framing reduces pressure and makes it easier to try things, adapt, and iterate. Product Hunt started as an experiment (newsletter, then website) and wasn't incorporated for 4-5 months.

### Fall in Love with the Problem (Uri Levine)
Start every startup by identifying a problem you're personally passionate about, validate it with 20+ strangers (target 100), and use the problem as your North Star throughout the journey to reduce deviation and increase likelihood of success.

How it works: Steps: 1) Identify a problem from personal frustration. 2) Ask 'who has this problem?' — if only you, get a therapist, not a startup. 3) Speak with 100 people (at least 20 you don't know) to validate. 4) Validation signal: they describe the problem back to you in their own words. Non-validation: they say 'I know someone who has this problem.' 5) Understand their perception of the problem BEFORE building a solution. 6) Use the problem as North Star for all decisions.

### Fish where the fish are (Andrew Wilkinson)
A mental model for choosing a business idea by looking for uncrowded, profitable niches.

How it works: Instead of competing in a large pond where everyone has the best gear and strategy (high competition, low margins), walk into the forest to find a small fishing hole with lots of fish and no competition.

### Five Conclusions About 'Why Now' (Why now?)
Lenny's framework for understanding the role of 'why now' in startup success, derived from research with investors and analysis of dozens of companies

How it works: Five key conclusions about 'why now':
1. You can build a massive business without an immediate 'why now'
2. A 'why now' is helpful because it creates two distinct advantages (10x better product OR new untapped market need)
3. There are five sources of 'why now' (details behind paywall)
4. Your 'why now' can emerge later in the life of the company
5. You can still absolutely fail with an amazing 'why now'

Bottom line: If there isn't a great reason why your startup is only possible now, don't despair — you still have a shot at building a big business, but know that you'll need to work even harder.

### Five Sources of 'Why Now' (Why now)
A taxonomy of five categories that create timing advantages for startups, divided into two groups based on the type of advantage they create

How it works: Group A - Unlocks the ability to provide a 10x better product:
1. Ubiquity of new technology (e.g. Figma and WebGL, Netflix and broadband, Tesla and batteries)
2. Newly available data or APIs (e.g. MapQuest and navigation databases, Superhuman and Gmail APIs)
3. Changes in regulation (e.g. Eaze and marijuana regulation, Cerebral and loosening telemedicine restrictions)

Group B - Creates a growing untapped market need:
4. Change in people's behavior (e.g. Shopify and the rise of e-commerce, Plaid and the rise of fintech)
5. Change in people's beliefs (e.g. Coinbase and belief in crypto, Robinhood and the lack of trust in big banks)

Application: When thinking through your own 'why now', figure out which of these five buckets timing most benefits you. Even better if there's more than one.

### Five Strategies for Coming Up with a Consumer Startup Idea (How to kickstart and scale a consumer business)
A framework of five distinct strategies successful consumer founders used to generate their startup ideas, all rooted in 'paying attention'

How it works: Strategy 1: Pay attention to your own problems, and solve them (~30% of companies)
- Build self-awareness: notice challenges day to day
- Be motivated: pay attention to problems that drive you to action
- Don't take your ideas for granted: if it's valuable to you, likely valuable to others
- Examples: Dropbox, Etsy, Cameo, Airbnb, Apple, Substack, Uber, Hipcamp, Snapchat, GOAT, WhatsApp, Warby Parker, Calm

Strategy 2: Pay attention to your curiosity, and tinker (~20%)
- Give yourself time to tinker: if excited, make space to explore
- Build: don't just think, build a prototype
- Keep it simple: test as cheaply and quickly as possible
- Examples: Coinbase, Google, OpenSea, Musical.ly/TikTok, Twitter, Tinder, Duolingo, Rent the Runway, The RealReal, Headspace

Strategy 3: Pay attention to what's already working, and double down / pivot (~18%)
- Kill your darlings: if not enough people love it, it won't work
- Don't take what's working for granted: if any part proves valuable, embrace it
- Try stuff: the more ideas you put out, the more likely something sticks
- Examples: Instagram, YouTube, Lyft, Pinterest, Glossier, PayPal, Discord, Twitch

Strategy 4: Pay attention to paradigm shifts, and work backward (~15%)
- Spend time thinking: what will be very different in 5, 10, 20 years?
- What hasn't software eaten? Find a big market with bad customer experience
- What would be amazing if possible? Start with ideal experience, figure out what it takes
- Examples: Spotify, Amazon, 23andme, Instacart, Stitch Fix

Strategy 5: Brainstorm with friends, applying the four strategies above (~15%)
- Examples: Thumbtack, Netflix, Yelp, Reddit, Nextdoor, DoorDash, Bumble, Eventbrite

### Founder Evaluation Framework (Angel Investing) (Scott Belsky)
Scott's criteria for evaluating founders: listen, learn, long to disrupt, mission over money; allergic to promoters who sugarcoat; value founders who are optimistic about the future but pessimistic about the present

How it works: Team criteria: 1) Founders who listen and really learn. 2) Long to shake things up. 3) Value mission more than money (especially pre-revenue). 4) NOT promoters who sugarcoat, gloss over hard parts, name-drop. 5) Optimistic about future + pragmatic/pessimistic about present. 6) Willing to share what's not working, obstacles, what keeps them up at night. Product criteria: 1) Clear object model (how did I get here, what do I do now, what next). 2) Clean breadcrumbs. 3) Believe in the problem they're solving.

### Founder Evaluation Spectrum (Gustaf Alstromer)
A mental model for evaluating founder traits and where they should index for early-stage success.

How it works: 1. Speed vs Quality: Not a spectrum; moving fast with customers creates quality. 2. Confidence vs Humility: Need extreme confidence to project to investors/team, but humility to learn. 3. Execution vs Strategy: Early stage is 100% execution and single-priority focus; strategy is for later. 4. Autocratic vs Consensus: Need a clear, agreed-upon decision-making process rather than a specific style.

### Founder Social Radar Evaluation (Jessica Livingston)
A mental model for evaluating the intangible qualities of startup founders during a pitch.

How it works: Key traits to look for: 1) Lack of defensiveness when challenged, 2) Deep domain expertise, 3) Co-founder harmony (no interrupting, shared history), 4) Extreme hustle/scrappiness, 5) Earnestness and authenticity.

### Founder Success Proxy: Series A as Measurement (Which companies produce the best product managers)
A method for distinguishing between PMs who merely start companies and those who build successful venture-scale businesses

How it works: To measure 'successful founder' among PM alumni, use Series A fundraising as the proxy metric. Rationale: Starting a company is one thing, building something that works is another. While imperfect (some founders stay bootstrapped, and raising a Series A doesn't guarantee success), it's a simple way to zero in on founders aiming to build large venture-scale companies. This separates the 'raw founder rate' (% of alumni who start any company) from the 'successful founder rate' (% of alumni whose companies raise a Series A). Example: Palantir has highest raw founder rate (33%+) but Chime has highest successful founder rate (20%+).

### Founder-Idea-Timing Venn Diagram (How to kickstart and scale a consumer business)
A visual framework showing that massive consumer businesses emerge from the intersection of the right founder traits and market timing

How it works: The right founder has three attributes:
1. Can't stop thinking about the idea (obsession/passion)
2. Has unique insight into the opportunity (experience/knowledge)
3. Has the right skills (especially engineering)

Combined with:
- Market timing (paradigm shifts, technology readiness, cultural readiness)

When these overlap, you create the opportunity for massive consumer businesses. This combination is also why you shouldn't worry about idea theft — the odds someone else has this exact combination at the right time are very low.

### Frustration-Based Idea Generation (Chip Huyen)
A simple process for coming up with practical AI micro-tool ideas.

How it works: Step 1: For one week, pay close attention to your daily tasks. Step 2: Document specific moments of frustration or friction. Step 3: Ask 'Can this be done a different way?' Step 4: Build a targeted AI micro-tool to solve that exact bottleneck (e.g., Lenny's tool to extract images from Google Docs).

### Ikigai for Creator Businesses (1,000,000)
A framework for evaluating whether a side project should become a full-time pursuit, based on the intersection of what you love, what you're good at, what the world needs, and what you can be paid for.

How it works: Three validation signals before going full-time: (1) Is it still fun after an extended period (9 months / 36 posts)? (2) Are people finding it genuinely valuable? (3) Do you still have a deep backlog of ideas you want to explore? If all three are true, you've found your Ikigai and can commit (e.g., add a paid plan, which forces a 1-year commitment).

### Jurassic Park Moments (How to spot a top 1% startup early)
Soleio's mental model for recognizing breakthrough potential in early-stage product demos, even when the product is ugly and small

How it works: Named after the iconic scene in the original Jurassic Park where the tour stops so John Hammond and his guests can witness a raptor hatching. The group crowds around as a dinosaur emerges from its shell. Even in this slimy, harmless form, you sense the world has shifted — an apex predator has entered it.

Applied to startups: Look for early prototypes or demos that leave you breathless and feeling like you are being given a clear glimpse of the future. Even though it's ugly and small, you can sense this product will grow up and one day eat things.

Examples:
- Facebook: The version the team described was light-years ahead of what Soleio saw when he logged in (pre-Photos, pre-News Feed). He was initially disappointed, but the vision was clear.
- Figma: More prototype than product the day Dylan laid out his vision for collaborative design in the browser.

### LOCKS Algorithm (Brian Halligan)
A mental model for evaluating startup founders and CEOs.

How it works: L = Lovable (inspires followership), O = Obsession (deep founder-market fit), C = Chip on the shoulder, K = Knowledgeable (deep domain expertise), S = Student (constantly learning).

### LUV Framework (Your startup idea probably isn’t venture-scale)
Hunter Walk's framework for evaluating problem size as an alternative to TAM-focused analysis, prioritizing problem characteristics over market size

How it works: The LUV Framework evaluates three dimensions of the problem you're solving:

**L - Large**: Is the problem you're aiming to solve large enough — in terms of customers, users, and spend?

**U - Urgent**: Is the problem urgent to your users/customers? Will they be interested in a new offering and change their current way of solving this problem?

**V - Valuable**: Are people willing to spend money to solve this problem? Is there financial value associated with this problem?

Key insight: You can have large TAMs that are bad markets for startups, and smaller markets that can be expanded by a talented startup with the right product. Problem size > market size.

### Latent Demand Framework (Nikita Bier)
A mental model for finding startup ideas by observing where users go through distortive or difficult processes to obtain a specific value.

How it works: Look for strong signals of unmet needs, such as users downloading an app entirely in a foreign language (e.g., Sarahah in Arabic) or hacking existing tools (e.g., TBH polls on Snapchat stories) just to get the core value.

### Li Jin's Passion Economy Startup Evaluation Criteria (Li Jin launches Atelier Ventures, her debut fund to invest in the passion economy)
Three key characteristics Li Jin looks for when evaluating early-stage passion economy investments

How it works: 1. **People bet / Founder quality**: Key traits are resilience and grit (will they keep going when facing obstacles?), propensity for action, and depth of market thinking (Did they study history of prior startups? Do they understand competitive landscape and market dynamics?). In the creator space, founder-market fit is critical — first users come from relationships, so founders who have faced challenges first-hand or have deep customer empathy have an unfair advantage in building trust.
2. **Broadens access to work**: The company broadens access to a new type of work OR makes it significantly easier to do an existing job that was previously gatekept. Look for differentiated skills/knowledge that can be productized and monetized in new ways.
3. **Expandable markets that appear small**: Markets that may appear small at first glance but can actually be expanded. TAM analysis can belie actual market size for disruptive innovations — the potential market among non-consumers usually exceeds what is already being consumed. New models reconfigure existing value chains and cost structures.

### Love-It/Hate-It Bifurcation Signal (Sam Schillace)
A signal for evaluating whether a product or idea is truly disruptive: if reactions are polarized (some people love it, some hate it) rather than forming a bell curve of indifference, it's likely genuinely disruptive.

How it works: Disruptive product: Strong love + strong hate, little in between. Incremental product: Bell curve of modern indifference with mild like and mild dislike. Key signals to watch for: People calling it 'a toy', saying 'I don't get it', or wanting it to 'die in a fire'. Note: Haters can outnumber lovers early on — that's fine. As long as you don't run out of people who love it, keep going. Examples: Google Docs (executives said kill it while millions used it), ChatGPT (amazing vs. terrible with little in between).

### Markets as Currents (Sarah Tavel) (Why now?)
Mental model for evaluating market timing by thinking about markets as currents rather than static bodies of water

How it works: Sarah Tavel's mental model: Think of markets like currents, not whether or not they are big bodies of water. The stronger the current, the higher your chance of success. 'Why now' is often a description of a current — a macro one. Examples of strong currents: the move to cloud, the employment/cultural trends driving the creator economy. These are tremendous 'why nows' because they represent powerful, directional forces that carry companies forward.

### Megatrend vs. Hype Cycle Heuristic (Jeetu Patel)
A simple test to determine if a new technology is a foundational megatrend or a passing hype cycle.

How it works: If you need a PhD to understand what the technology does in its ultimate form, it's a hype cycle (e.g., Web3). If it's easy for most people to understand the use case and get an answer (e.g., ChatGPT), it's a megatrend.

### Passion Economy Definition and Thesis (Li Jin launches Atelier Ventures, her debut fund to invest in the passion economy)
Li Jin's original thesis defining the passion economy as distinct from the gig economy, where people monetize individuality and non-commoditized skills at scale via digital platforms

How it works: The passion economy is NOT an industry vertical — it's an entirely new economic movement that empowers individuals over institutions. Key characteristics:
- People build audiences at scale and turn passions into livelihoods
- Digital platforms enable people to connect to consumers and monetize skills/interests
- People go direct-to-consumer in offering services and products
- Monetization of individuality at scale, supported by digital platforms
- Applies across ALL industries: chefs, teachers, fitness instructors, musicians, marketers, grocery delivery solopreneurs, etc.
- Distinct from 'creator economy' (which is a subset) — the passion economy encompasses broader economic empowerment
- Fund mission: Rebuild the middle class and create more opportunities for earning money in ways that are non-exploitative and give people a path to upward mobility

### Problem Journal (Ryan Hoover)
A simple practice of writing down problems, annoyances, and inefficiencies as you encounter them without trying to solve them, as a way to generate startup ideas over time.

How it works: Keep a running note/journal of things that annoy you or are inefficient in daily life. Key rule: Don't try to solve the problem when you write it down—just capture the problem. Example: 'In San Francisco, a lot of people don't have laundry in their unit. That's annoying.' Over time, patterns emerge and ideas surface.

### Rachleff's Law of Markets (Your startup idea probably isn’t venture-scale)
Andy Rachleff's classic framework showing that market quality trumps team quality in determining startup outcomes

How it works: Three scenarios:
1. When a great team meets a lousy market → market wins
2. When a lousy team meets a great market → market wins
3. When a great team meets a great market → something special happens

Key takeaway: Market selection is the single most important decision. No amount of team quality can overcome a bad market.

### Schlep Blindness (Essential reading for product builders—part 2)
A mental model for recognizing that your unconscious mind filters out the most valuable startup ideas because they involve intimidating operational complexity.

How it works: Key insights:
1. Schleps (tedious, unpleasant tasks) are not merely inevitable—they're pretty much what business consists of.
2. A company is defined by the schleps it will undertake.
3. Your unconscious mind shrinks from complications (dealing with banks, fraud, regulations, etc.), preventing you from even considering the best ideas.
4. Example: Stripe. For over a decade, every hacker knew online payments were painful. Thousands of people knew about this problem. But they chose to build recipe sites instead because their unconscious mind shrank from the complications.
5. Scariness makes ambitious ideas doubly valuable: (a) intrinsic value of solving a hard problem, (b) less competition because everyone else is frightened off.
6. Ambitious ideas are like undervalued stocks—there's less demand for them among founders.

### Second and Third Order Effects of the One-Person Billion Dollar Startup (Sherwin Wu V2)
Cascading impact analysis showing how AI-enabled solo founders create an explosion of micro-companies and reshape the startup/VC ecosystem

How it works: First order: One person can build a billion dollar company with AI leverage. Second order: If one person can build a billion dollar company, it's way easier for anyone to create a startup — startup boom / SMB boom, vertical software explosion. Third order: The billion dollar startup is enabled by 100 micro-startups building bespoke supporting tools. Distribution: 1 billion dollar startup → 100 $100M startups → 10,000+ $10M startups. Fourth order (speculative): VC ecosystem changes — fewer venture-scale return companies, more lifestyle-scale businesses. Distribution becomes king as the number of products explodes.

### Seven Patterns for Knowing When to Go All-In (How to validate your B2B startup idea)
Seven distinct patterns that convinced successful B2B founders to fully commit to their startup idea

How it works: Seven patterns for committing to your startup:

1. **The right people agreeing to join** — Coda's Shishir Mehrotra's litmus test: (a) Do you have an idea you can't imagine not working on? AND (b) Do you have a person you can't imagine not working with? You need both. They made a list of the first 4 people they wanted to hire — when all said yes, they started the company.

2. **An impactful conversation** — A single conversation that crystallizes conviction. Gusto: An accountant spontaneously ranted for 30 minutes about broken payroll systems before the founders even pitched. Census: Two conversations revealed how underleveraged operators were and how even Amazon's infrastructure was terrible.

3. **Momentum** — A snowball effect where commitment feels inevitable rather than a conscious decision. Stytch: 'By the time we consciously talked about it, it felt like it wasn't a decision we were making.'

4. **Companies giving you time** — When potential customers invest their own time in working with you. Vanta: Segment allowed them to act as 'SOC 2 consultants' and spend time with their engineering team.

5. **Desperation** — Running out of money or options. Segment: 'We were running out of money and would have to find real jobs in the next few months.'

6. **Cosmic coincidence** — Life circumstances forcing a decision. Hex: Both co-founders had to relocate for partners' jobs, creating a natural jumping-off point.

7. **Already committed to starting something** — The decision to start a company preceded the specific idea. Figma: Thiel Fellowship gave runway to 'just try stuff.' Amplitude: Spenser committed to doing a startup for minimum 2 years no matter what. Snyk: Guy left Akamai specifically to start another company.

### Six-Part Great Company Framework (Jeetu Patel)
A stack-ranked checklist of the six essential elements required for a company or new business unit to win.

How it works: In descending order of importance: 1) Timing (most important, least controllable), 2) Market (large enough to prosecute a chunk at a time), 3) Team (well-rounded, complementing weaknesses), 4) Product (the soul of the company), 5) Brand (trust, hard to resurrect if lost), 6) Distribution (scaled mechanism to reach users).

### Startup Evaluation Criteria (Priority-Ordered) (Lessons from 140+ angel investments)
Lenny's seven criteria for evaluating startups for angel investment, listed in priority order

How it works: 1. Pain: How significant is the pain this product solves? Is it a small pain or a 'can't live without this product' pain? If there are budget cuts or a new hot app, will people stick with this?
2. Big and growing market: Are there enough people with this pain? Could it get to a $1B/year revenue business someday?
3. Evidence of PMF: Is there strong organic growth, killer testimonials, or high retention? Enough reason to believe this team can solve this big pain?
4. A+ founders: Is the team executing frighteningly fast, with high intensity? Do they have unique insights into the problem? If another team emerges doing the same thing, will you feel good about who you're backing?
5. Unfair advantage: What gives this team an edge over future competitors or the status quo? Unique insight, clever distribution strategy, proprietary data, or incredible tech?
6. Business model: Is it software-based, ideally with recurring revenue and high margins, selling to companies that move quickly and embrace new technology?
7. Rocket-ship-ness: Is this on track for $1B/year+ business? If so, get in regardless of price.

### Startup Idea Generation Methods (Ryan Hoover)
Multiple approaches to coming up with startup ideas: problem journals, community immersion, consumer behavior shifts, and technology shifts.

How it works: 1) Problem Journal - write down annoyances without solving them, 2) Community Immersion - immerse in niche/weird communities (subreddits, hobbyist groups) to find patterns and unmet needs, 3) Observe Consumer Behavior Shifts - e.g., distributed/remote working trend, 4) Observe Technology Shifts - what can you build today that couldn't be built yesterday (Web3, AI, mobile in 2010s), 5) Answer the 'Why Now?' question - what has changed that makes this possible/necessary now?

### Tarpit Ideas (Dalton Caldwell)
A mental model for identifying deceptive startup ideas that attract many founders but rarely succeed.

How it works: Criteria of a tarpit idea: 1) A lot of people independently come up with it. 2) It seems like an unsolved problem. 3) You get lots of positive feedback from friends/users initially. 4) People have been trying and failing to build it since the 90s (e.g., an app to coordinate where friends go out at night).

### Team vs. Product vs. Market (Market Wins) (Essential reading for product builders—part 1)
Marc Andreessen's hierarchy for startup success: market is the most important factor, above team and product. A great market pulls product out of the startup.

How it works: The three factors and the common positions:
1. Entrepreneurs/VCs often say 'team' is most important (you know more about team early on; pro-people sentiment).
2. Engineers often say 'product' is most important (this is a product business; Apple and Google win because of product).
3. Andreessen's position: Market is the most important factor.

Why market wins:
- In a great market with lots of real potential customers, the market PULLS product out of the startup.
- The market needs to be fulfilled and WILL be fulfilled by the first viable product.
- The product doesn't need to be great; it just has to basically work.
- The market doesn't care how good the team is, as long as the team can produce that viable product.

### Three Ingredients of a Great B2B Startup Idea (How the most successful B2B startups came up with their original idea)
A framework for evaluating whether a B2B startup idea has the three essential ingredients to succeed

How it works: A great B2B startup idea requires all three ingredients:

1. **The problem is important to people** — There need to be a lot of people willing to spend a lot of money to solve the problem. Rule of thumb: clear path to $100M/year revenue, eventually $1B/year. Most startups fail because the market is too small.

2. **The market is underserved** — Existing solutions need to be doing a subpar job. Use the Delta-4 test: your solution needs to be rated at least 4 points higher than existing solutions on a 1-10 scale. If existing solutions rate 6+, it's very hard to replace them.

### Three Paths to Finding a B2B Startup Idea (How the most successful B2B startups came up with their original idea)
Three reliable paths for discovering a great B2B startup idea, derived from studying 24+ successful B2B companies

How it works: Path 1: **Past Pain** (~40% of startups)
- Identify a large pain you experienced at a previous company, then build a solution.
- Examples: Gong (couldn't understand sales process), Retool (built same internal tools repeatedly), Linear (frustration with Jira), Persona (inadequate identity tooling at Square), Hex (couldn't find the tool he needed)

Path 2: **Ponder and Probe** (~35% of startups)
- Pick a space you're interested in, then whiteboard and tinker while talking to dozens of potential customers, looking for pain and pull.
- Key insight: There's a wrong way (ideating in a silo) and a right way (quickly talking to potential users).
- Examples: Figma (narrowed to two areas, picked design), Vanta (narrowed to security and collaboration, went deep on security), Notion (no-code builder, noticed pull for docs/wiki after 4 years), Zip (6th or 7th idea, looked for large + entrenched + not great markets), Databricks (noticed underutilized technology)

Path 3: **Present Pull** (~25% of startups)
- Identify something you've built that is showing signs of pull, then pivot fully to that.
- Examples: Amplitude (other founders wanted their analytics tool), Sprig (SDK got more pull than main product), Loom (client used product to record summary video), Segment (Analytics.js side project hit #1 on HN), Slack (IRC features during game development)

No matter which path: look for two things — **pain** (tells you there's an opportunity) and **pull** (tells you you're actually solving the problem).

### Value Denial Exercise (Richard Rumelt)
An ideation technique for finding startup/product opportunities: identify things people should be able to buy or do but can't, and envision the 'perfect' version of existing products.

How it works: Two exercises: A) Value Denial: Ask 'What should I be able to buy/do but can't?' ### Origin Stories of 24+ Successful B2B Companies (How the most successful B2B startups came up with their original idea)
A categorized collection of how today's most successful B2B startups found their original idea, organized by discovery path

How it works: **Path 1: Past Pain (~40%)**
- Gong: Co-founder at Sisense couldn't understand why sales weren't working; CRM data was meaningless
- Retool: Founder built the same internal tools at every company; realized they all share building blocks
- Linear: Founders at Coinbase, Airbnb, Uber all frustrated with Jira; one co-founder built a Chrome extension to re-skin Jira
- Persona: Founder worked on identity at Square; team joked 'in 10 years someone will do this well for us'
- Hex: Founder was a buyer looking for the product; everyone he asked said 'no, but let us know if you find it'
- Snyk: Founder combined AppSec experience (2002-2010) with DevOps insights from his first startup
- Canva: All three founders noticed the gap—teaching design (Mel), building creative tools (Cameron), and running a small business (Cliff)
- Stytch: Co-founders both frustrated with authentication at Plaid and Very Good Security during a coffee chat
- Snowflake: Founders at Oracle saw Hadoop hype but knew it wasn't the answer; built a cloud-native data warehouse
- Ramp: Founders learned at Capital One that customers didn't want rewards—they wanted to be better off financially

**Path 2: Ponder and Probe (~35%)**
- Figma: Thiel Fellowship → narrowed to drones and WebGL → eliminated drones → computational photography → settled on design tools
- Vanta: Started with 'garbage' whiteboard ideas → narrowed to collaboration and security → went deep on security by talking to startups → found everyone guilty about ignoring security
- Notion: Started as no-code website builder → 4 years of wandering → noticed users liked docs/wiki features → launched Notion 1.0 focused on docs/wikis
- Zip: 6th or 7th idea after U.S. equities for India, accounting staffing ($200K revenue) → YC partner advised 'large market + entrenched + not great' → landed on procurement (now $1B+ valuation)
- Databricks: UC Berkeley researchers saw Silicon Valley using Apache Spark for AI/ML → open-sourced it → frustrated by low uptake → started company to drive adoption
- Coda: Idea on Shishir's list for 20+ years → brainstormed with friend Alex → couldn't stop thinking about it → wife knew before he did
- Gusto: Three founders with small-business family backgrounds → massive whiteboard of problems → focused on small businesses → learned a third of companies make payroll mistakes yearly
- Census: Sold previous startup to LogMeIn → noticed sales/marketing lacked product usage data → brainstormed CRM ideas → built marketplace CRM prototype → realized whiteboarding without talking to people was bad

**Path 3: Present Pull (~25%)**
- Amplitude: Built voice recognition app Sonalight → built internal analytics tool → other YC founders wanted access to it
- Sprig: Was exploring a different idea → built an SDK with Airtable backend using outcome-driven innovation framework → other founders offered to pay for it
- Loom: Failed video marketplace → failed SaaS user testing tool → client used product to record a summary video of user tests → launched on Product Hunt, thousands downloaded day one
- Segment: Failed classroom lecture tool → 15 months of analytics pivots → co-founder built Analytics.js as a growth hack → users didn't care about main product but loved Analytics.js → launched on HN, went straight to #1
- Slack: Built video game Glitch for 3.5 years → used IRC internally with custom features (archiving, search, mobile) → everything routed to IRC → noticed the communication tool was more valuable than the game
