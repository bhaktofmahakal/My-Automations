## Frameworks

### 3-Part Career Conversations (Kim Scott)
A structure for managers to understand and develop their direct reports over three 45-minute meetings.

How it works: Meeting 1: Past (life story to understand motivations). Meeting 2: Future (dreams at the height of their career, 3-4 different pictures). Meeting 3: Present (career action plan to build skills for that future).

### 5 Tips for Success as a New PM Manager (Moving from IC product manager to manager of product managers)
Crowdsourced wisdom from experienced PM managers on what they wish they knew when transitioning

How it works: 1. It's all about increasing the leverage of your team
2. It's more about teaching than doing
3. There are many ways to do the job
4. It's essential to bring everyone along
5. You don't have to get into management

### 5 Unexpected Jobs of a PM Manager (Moving from IC product manager to manager of product managers)
A framework defining the five core responsibilities that distinguish PM management from IC PM work

How it works: 1. Stop stupid sh*t from happening — Use higher echelon of influence to push back on bad decisions within your team or anywhere in the company.
2. Unblock, unblock, unblock — Leverage authority to remove blockers at three horizons: short-term (making decisions), medium-term (hiring to fill gaps), long-term (aligning around vision and strategy). NOT doing IC PM work like roadmapping or running meetings.
3. Preserve (and improve) the PM team quality bar — Participate in performance reviews/calibrations, resist letting OK work slide, keep the bar high.
4. Preserve (and improve) the product quality bar — Be torch-bearer for quality, ambition, and innovation without micromanaging.
5. Build a strong and united leadership group — Your new team is peer managers (EM, design manager, DS manager); invest as much or more effort in these relationships as you did with IC peers.

### 5-Step Guide to Building a Custom GPT That Thinks Like You (How to become a supermanager with AI)
A step-by-step framework for managers to create GPTs that replicate their expertise, feedback patterns, and standards for their teams.

How it works: Step 1: Identify what kind of feedback you are best at giving. If unsure, prompt AI: 'I want to create a custom GPT that helps my team upskill in an area where I am uniquely strong. What are some examples of skills or areas where [product] leaders might have unique expertise they could teach an AI?' Step 2: Translate this skill area into a GPT concept. Example: If you love UX design details, create a GPT where team uploads wireframes for first-round feedback or generates usability testing questions. If unsure, upload this post as PDF and prompt: 'I'd like to focus on [x domain]. Read this document and then give me 10 new creative ideas for GPTs I could create to help my team upskill in this area.' Step 3: Teach the model to think like you. Gather 'before' and 'after' work examples where your input improved the final product, or identify best-in-class examples. Use AI to analyze differences: 'Describe in detail the differences between the before and after versions.' Drill in with: 'Be 100x clearer and more specific.' Step 4: Ask the AI to write a prompt for you. Prompt: 'Write a prompt that would make an AI turn a rough draft like the before example into a polished version like the after example. Explain the why behind any recommendations with extreme specificity and detail.' Use as starting point, then refine and test by running 'before' examples. Step 5: Create and share the GPT. Follow instructions at https://zapier.com/blog/custom-chatgpt/. Load your prompt, add helpful documents to knowledge base (blog posts, etc.), publish and share with team. Emphasize you're still available but this makes your insights accessible anytime.

### API Endpoint Thought Experiment (Five principles for successfully managing managers)
A cognitive exercise for new skip leads to develop skills in leading through managers rather than directly through ICs

How it works: Core question: 'If I had zero access to any IC in my organization and could only push or pull information from my managers to make decisions, how would I go about being effective?'

Follow-up questions to work through:
1. How do I help line managers spot problems on their team without talking to their ICs?
2. How do I help line managers deal with IC performance issues on their team instead of dealing with them myself?
3. When do I defer to the line manager's judgment if it runs counter to mine?
4. When do I insist that my point of view carries the day even though the manager has talked to all the ICs and has more firsthand information?
5. How do I review work coming out of a line manager's team in a way that is additive or complementary to what the manager is already doing?
6. How do I give input early enough that a line manager can course correct before it's too late?
7. What are the areas that I fully delegate to my line managers and what are the areas where I retain final sign-off, and why?

Caveats: (1) Answering these at instinctive competence takes years, but asking them accelerates learning. (2) This is a thought experiment—in practice, skip leads should still talk with skip reports regularly.

### Bloom's Taxonomy for PMs (Bangaly Kaba)
An educational framework adapted to assess and develop a product manager's critical thinking levels.

How it works: Levels from bottom to top: Knowledge, Comprehension, Application, Analysis, Synthesis, Evaluation. Used to diagnose if a PM knows the theory but can't apply it, or can apply it but can't synthesize the business impact.

### Dual Persona Model for Product Leaders (Varun Parmar)
A leadership philosophy where product leaders operate with two distinct personas—one focused on accountability with peers, one focused on improvement with their team

How it works: Persona 1 - Product Leadership Team Member: #1 goal = Drive accountability. When meeting with peer leaders (e.g., Monday afternoon leadership meeting), your job is to hold each other accountable for doing the right thing for the company. Includes giving constructive feedback, voicing concerns, and challenging decisions. Persona 2 - Stream Leader: #1 goal = Drive improvement. When working with your team, always ask: 'Today compared to yesterday, tomorrow compared to today, have I improved things?' This is the yardstick for evaluating your own leadership.

### Eight Skill Axes for PM Growth (Vikrama Dhiman)
Eight dimensions on which PMs should be evaluated and developed, each rated on a 0-5 scale from novice to expert

How it works: Hard skills: (1) Data & Metrics - Level 0: can't define basic metrics; Level 5: could start a data company. (2) Design & Research - Level 0: can't identify user problems; Level 5: ties user problems to business goals holistically. (3) Technology - Level 0: doesn't understand HTTP/API/internet; Level 5: can write technology design documents. (4) Strategy - Level 0: relies on others for all direction; Level 5: articulates coherent product strategy at broad level. Soft skills: (5) Communication (6) Collaboration (7) Organizational Skills (8) Community - PM as the 'community enabler' tying the team together toward a common mission. Soft skills are lifelong development areas; hard skills can be more concretely leveled up.

### Five Ingredients of PM Coaching (Petra Wille)
A step-by-step approach for managers to coach and develop their product managers.

How it works: 1. Define what a good PM looks like. 2. Assess where the PM is today and identify the 'next bigger challenge'. 3. Align on a shared vision for their growth. 4. Create a development plan. 5. Follow up consistently.

### Five Principles for Managing Managers (Five principles for successfully managing managers)
A comprehensive framework of five principles for new skip leads (managers of managers) to become effective in their role

How it works: 1. Recognize that you are in a different role — Managing managers is fundamentally different from managing ICs. You lose direct context, face blurred responsibilities, and must work through an abstraction layer. Treat this as a new role with a steep learning curve.
2. Conduct the API endpoint experiment — Thought experiment: 'If I had zero access to any IC and could only push/pull information from my managers, how would I be effective?' Forces you to confront how to create value through managers rather than around them.
3. Never undermine — When talking to skip reports, listen carefully and ask questions but do NOT offer strong opinions, solve problems, or make decisions on the spot. Work through your direct-report managers to set direction. Undermining disempowers managers and confuses skip reports with contradictory direction.
4. Never cover — Don't quietly compensate for a struggling manager's underperformance. Instead: (1) Clearly report the underperformance and a plan to course correct up the chain, (2) Solicit feedback on the course correction from leaders up the chain, (3) Set clear performance expectations with the direct-report manager.
5. Use task-relevant maturity (TRM) to delegate and review work — Evaluate each project's scope, risk, ambiguity, and duration, then match to the manager best suited based on their specific skills and experience for that situation. After delegation, calibrate review cadence using the uncertainty × impact 2x2.

### Five Strategies for Balancing Autonomy vs. Direction (Autonomy vs. direction - Issue 35)
Lenny's five-part framework for managers to balance team autonomy with their own directional authority

How it works: 1. Align on the facts — Most disagreements result from having different information. Assume you're wrong. Share data, user research, past experience. Ask what your team sees that you don't.
2. Know the difference between consulting, collaboration, and consensus — Consulting = everyone sharing perspectives. Collaboration = everyone working together. Consensus = everyone agreeing. Encourage collaboration and consulting, but avoid consensus-based decision making (it's an anti-pattern).
3. It's OKAY to overrule your team — disagree and commit — There's a reason there's a boss: to make decisions. Use this power when you feel strongly. Script: 'Normally I want you to feel great about the direction, but I feel strongly about this path, so can we just try it and see how it goes? If I'm wrong, I'm wrong, and I'll take full responsibility.'
4. Use the 90/10 rule — 90% of the time, feel great about the path. 10% of the time, give the team a chance to prove you wrong, especially for two-way-door decisions. Use as a 'pressure release valve' for morale.
5. Always have a point-of-view — Ask yourself: 'If I take people's feelings out of the equation, what would I do?' Having a POV shifts from 'what should we do?' to 'how do I make it happen?' and gives the team a clear reference point. Plan to change your POV, but always have one.

### GROW Model for Powerful Questions (Rachel Lockett)
Four categories of coaching questions that unlock insight: Goal (what does success look like?), Reality (where are you stuck?), Options (what paths could you take?), Way forward (what will you do next?).

How it works: G - Goal: What does success look like? What's the outcome you want? Define the best case scenario. R - Reality: Where are you stuck? What are your current challenges? What have you tried? O - Options: What are the various paths you could take? Let's expand the opportunities. W - Way forward: What are you going to do next? What's one thing you could do in the next two weeks? Note: Don't have to follow in order. These are just four kinds of questions to draw from based on what's needed.

### High Performer Experimentation (Molly Graham)
A method for developing top talent by testing their capabilities with increasing scope.

How it works: 1. Develop a theory about a high performer's potential. 2. Run incremental experiments (e.g., give them a bigger project, manage them less, give them more visibility). 3. Pair their proven capabilities with larger company needs.

### Hypothesis-Based Coaching (Claire Hughes Johnson)
A management approach that treats coaching as collaborative exploration rather than lecturing.

How it works: Instead of waiting for perfect data, use your intuition to form a hypothesis about a direct report's behavior or performance. Present it to them as an exploration (e.g., 'I noticed X, did you feel that way too?') and allow them to confirm or deny it.

### In-the-Room Experiment (Donna Lichaw)
A method for testing a scary or uncomfortable new behavior in a safe, micro-environment before applying it in the real world.

How it works: Instead of committing to a new behavior for an hour (e.g., deep listening in a meeting), role-play the behavior for just 30 seconds with a coach or peer to gather immediate data on how it feels.

### Manager Effectiveness Impact Model (How tech workers really feel about work right now)
Quantified correlations showing how manager effectiveness impacts every dimension of worker sentiment and retention

How it works: Impact of great managers vs. poor managers:
- 48% more engaged
- 63% more belonging
- 31% less burnout
- 62% more job enjoyment

Retention impact:
- Workers with ineffective leadership are 4.3x as likely to be at risk of leaving (72.6% vs 16.9%)
- Workers with extremely effective leadership are 8.2x as likely to be committed to their role (61.6% vs 7.5%)
- Workers 'actively looking' for new opportunities are 5x as likely to rate their manager poorly

Additional findings:
- Remote leadership rated slightly more effective than in-office leadership
- Leadership effectiveness ratings decline as company size increases
- People in product and design roles have most negative leadership perceptions
- Only 26.6% of workers rate managers as highly effective; 42.3% rate them as ineffective

### Manager-Report Writing Contract (Deb Liu)
An accountability agreement to help employees overcome the fear of sharing their expertise publicly.

How it works: The manager requires the direct report to write and publish one piece of content every month. The guiding rule for content generation is: 'Write what you repeat.'

### Motorcycle Sidecar Management (Nikhyl Singhal)
A mental model for how to approach managing a team member.

How it works: Management is not 'divide and conquer' or 'teaching someone to ride a bike and letting go.' It is riding in the sidecar of a motorcycle they are driving. You are attached and offer counsel, but you must wait to be 'invited in' to help with specific areas rather than forcing your authority.

### Never Undermine Protocol for Skip-Level Conversations (Five principles for successfully managing managers)
Guidelines for how skip leads should behave during conversations with skip reports to avoid undermining direct-report managers

How it works: What NOT to do in skip-level conversations:
- Offer strong opinions on the spot
- Solve problems directly without involving the management chain
- Make decisions without getting full context from the direct-report manager
- Give directions that may contradict the direct manager's guidance

What TO do:
- Listen carefully
- Ask lots of follow-up questions
- Actively work with your direct-report manager afterward to do all follow-up and actual direction-setting
- Coach managers on how to handle situations, share your reasoning and context, so they can apply it themselves in a scalable way

Founder mode done well: Founding CEO stays highly engaged and hands-on while coaching and empowering the management chain.
Founder mode done poorly: A recipe for undermining, disempowering, and creating chaos.

### PM Competency Model (12 Competencies) (Ravi Mehta)
A 12-competency framework across 4 areas for evaluating and developing product managers at all levels, originally developed at Tripadvisor

How it works: 4 areas with 3 competencies each: 1) Product Execution: Functional Specification, Product Delivery, Product Quality. 2) Customer Insight: Data Fluency, Voice of the Customer, User Experience Design. 3) Product Strategy: Business Outcome Ownership, Product Vision & Roadmapping, Strategic Impact. 4) Leadership: Stakeholder Inclusion, Team Leadership, Managing Up. Rate each as: Needs Focus / On Track / Outperforming. Same competencies apply APM through CPO — scope changes (individual contribution → building systems). ### PM Performance Scoring Matrix (How to know if you're doing a good job as a product manager)
A simple rubric that maps combinations of yes/no answers on the three questions to an overall performance rating

How it works: Scoring guide:
- Yes to all 3 questions → You're doing great
- No to any 1 of them → You're doing OK
- No to all 3 → You're failing

The three questions (in priority order):
1. Is your team delivering business impact?
2. Are you personally contributing to the team's success?
3. Do stakeholders regard you highly?

### Peer Feedback Groups (Megan Cook)
Small, mixed-level groups of PMs that meet regularly to review early-stage work.

How it works: Meet every two weeks. PMs bring rough drafts (strategies, PRDs, experiments). Groups contain a mix of seniority levels to model constructive feedback. The explicit cultural goal is to lift the presenter up and make their work stronger.

### Performance = Potential - Interference (Katie Dill)
A mental model for managing and growing teams.

How it works: To increase team performance, a leader must simultaneously increase potential (hiring well, developing talent) and decrease interference (removing bad processes, breaking down silos, fixing org design).

### Pyramid of Leadership Self-Awareness (John Cutler)
A model for understanding a leader's level of self-awareness and worldview.

How it works: Level 1: Know nothing about yourself. Level 2: Self-aware but believe the whole world is wired like you. Level 3: Aware others think differently but believe your way is best. Level 4: Realize diverse views are a huge asset and integrate them without losing your authentic self.

### Reverse Engineering Your Own Feedback for AI (How to become a supermanager with AI)
A technique for articulating intuitive standards by uploading high-quality and low-quality examples to AI and having it identify the specific differences.

How it works: Process: (1) Upload examples of work you consider high-quality and low-quality. (2) Ask AI to identify the specific differences between them. (3) Have AI create a prompt that could transform the 'bad' examples into 'good' ones. (4) Use the prompt 'Be 100x clearer and more specific' to drill into patterns. (5) Test the prompt by running 'before' examples and evaluating the output. This technique helps managers articulate standards that feel intuitive but are hard to put into words.

### Role Stages (Lane Shackleton)
A 5-level career ladder framework that transcends specific job titles, focusing on capability rather than tenure.

How it works: Levels range from Apprentice to Principal. Uses a rope analogy: Apprentice (learns about rope), Practitioner (ties basic knots), Career (calculates rope strength), Principal (invented nylon).

### Selective Micromanagement Matrix (Ravi Mehta)
A 2x2 matrix of leader confidence × team autonomy that defines four leadership modes, with two being effective and two being failure modes

How it works: Two axes: Leader's confidence in team direction (high/low) × Team autonomy (high/low). Four quadrants: 1) Scalable Leadership (high confidence + high autonomy) — ideal state. 2) Selective Micromanagement (low confidence + low autonomy, temporary) — deliberately zoom in, set frameworks, then pull back. 3) Hands-off / Neglect (low confidence + high autonomy) — feels good short-term but team goes off rails. 4) Micro-mismanagement (low confidence + low autonomy, permanent) — classic bad micromanagement where no one is happy. Key insight: seniority = increasing dynamic range (strategy to tactical details), not just going higher level.

### Shadows of Superpowers (Nikhyl Singhal)
A concept explaining that an executive's biggest weaknesses are usually the direct byproducts of their greatest strengths.

How it works: Examples: Great collaborators struggle to be opinionated; world-class storytellers struggle with details; highly decisive people struggle to listen. To fix this, look at the feedback you've discarded because you thought 'that's not who I am.'

### Six Fears of Asking for Help (On asking for help (even when you really don’t want to))
A framework identifying the six core fears that prevent people from asking for help, useful for self-diagnosis and coaching.

How it works: 1. **Fear of being a burden:** 'If I ask for help, I'll be imposing on others who are also busy.'
2. **Fear of appearing incompetent:** 'If I can't handle this on my own, they'll think I'm not qualified for my role.'
3. **Fear of rejection:** 'If I ask for help, they'll just say no.'
4. **Fear of losing control:** 'If I involve others, I might lose autonomy over my projects.'
5. **Fear of uncertainty:** 'If I ask for help now, it might negatively impact my future opportunities or relationships.'
6. **Fear of the cost:** 'If I ask for help and it doesn't solve my problem, I'll have exposed my vulnerability for nothing.'

### Six-Month Autonomy Test (Peter Deng)
A hiring and management principle that sets expectations for both manager and report: within six months, the hire should be telling the manager what to do, not the other way around.

How it works: Three levels of impact: 1. Reminder for the hiring manager to keep the bar high and not settle. 2. Communicates to the new hire what success looks like and what to work toward. 3. Creates a shared meta-goal where mistakes become learning opportunities toward the six-month target, reframing the relationship from 'did you hit this OKR' to 'are we calibrating toward full autonomy.'

### Specialty Coaching Model (Cam Adams)
An alternative to traditional management where employees are paired with a coach in their specific discipline.

How it works: Coaches focus on skill improvement and career pivot points (e.g., a PM coaches a PM). Supplemented by a 360-degree feedback cycle every 6 months from a collegiate circle of peers.

### Supermanager Framework (0-to-80 / 80-to-100) (How to become a supermanager with AI)
A management philosophy where AI handles getting work from 0 to 80% quality, freeing the manager to focus on the high-leverage 80-to-100% coaching moments.

How it works: Core concept: A supermanager harnesses AI to amplify their impact while fully embracing the human side of management. Key principles: (1) AI provides real-time, first-round feedback (0 to 80) so the manager focuses on nuanced, high-impact coaching (80 to 100). (2) By scaling efforts via custom GPTs, supermanagers can oversee larger, flatter teams. (3) Coaching extends beyond direct reports, fostering org-wide learning culture. (4) The skills that make great managers (clear feedback, articulating good vs. excellent) translate remarkably well to creating GPTs. (5) Metric: maximize 'Hilary Per Minute (HPM)' — ensure every minute of manager time is maximally impactful. Results cited: team performance, productivity, and NPS all reached all-time highs.

### Surgeon Model of Engineering Management (Sherwin Wu V2)
Management philosophy inspired by The Mythical Man-Month where the manager treats each engineer like a surgeon — the manager's job is to look around corners, anticipate needs, and have everything ready (like the surgical support team) so the engineer can focus purely on the work

How it works: 1. Spend 50%+ of your time with your top 10% performers. 2. Treat each top performer like a surgeon — your job is support, not direction. 3. Look around corners to anticipate organizational blockers before engineers hit them. 4. Remove process and organizational friction so engineers can focus on shipping. 5. As AI increases shipping velocity, organizational bottlenecks become the primary constraint — so this role becomes even more critical.

### Task-Relevant Maturity (TRM) Delegation Questions (Five principles for successfully managing managers)
A set of questions skip leads should ask when deciding which manager to assign to a project or situation, based on Andy Grove's TRM concept

How it works: When a project or situation arises, the skip leader should ask:
1. Which of my managers has dealt with a very similar project before?
2. Which of my managers has the right level of technical skill required by this situation?
3. Which of my managers has asked me to be stretched into a project like this?
4. Which of my managers is senior enough to deal with a high degree of ambiguity?
5. Which of my managers do I trust the most with this very delicate interpersonal situation?
6. Which of my managers is best suited for a [domain]-heavy project?
7. Which of my managers can do this if I personally lean in a bit more than usual?
8. Who can I hand this off to such that I can have this off my plate altogether?

Key factors to evaluate for each project: scope, size, risk, duration, ambiguity, technical complexity, number of teams involved, and potential company impact.

### Team Empowerment Through Personal Goal Alignment (How a traumatic brain injury made me a better PM—and person)
A delegation framework where you match work to team members' personal growth goals for win-win outcomes

How it works: Process:
1. Work with each engineer and designer to understand their personal goals
2. Identify what types of work excite them most
3. Delegate ownership of work that aligns with their goals

Examples of delegation types:
- Taking on more cross-functional communications → for those wanting to improve leadership presence
- Greater responsibility over data and analytics → for those wanting to develop analytical skills
- Doing the work to help define north-star vision → for those wanting more strategic involvement

Result: Win-win — PM gets space to focus on highest-leverage work (or recover), team members get growth opportunities that increase performance and satisfaction levels.

### The 90/10 Rule for Manager Decision-Making (Autonomy vs. direction - Issue 35)
A rule of thumb for how often a manager should assert their own judgment vs. letting the team pursue a direction they disagree with

How it works: 90% of the time: You should feel great about the path your team is taking (either you agree, or you've successfully steered the team).
10% of the time: Give the team a chance to prove you wrong, especially if:
- It's a two-way-door decision (reversible)
- It's not a large investment
- Team member(s) are super passionate about it

Use this as a 'pressure release valve' — taking on something you're not confident in but that your team cares about deeply.
Best case: You're proven wrong and the team benefits.
Worst case: You waste a bit of time while the team feels heard.
Key: Use sparingly.

### The Fear Bet (Matt Mochary)
A method to prove to a leader that fear gives bad advice by making a low-stakes prediction bet.

How it works: 1. Identify the fear. 2. Have the leader predict the outcome. 3. Coach predicts the exact opposite. 4. Make a bet. 5. Execute the action. 6. Review who won to build trust in ignoring fear.

### The Keeper Test (Elizabeth Stone)
A mental model for managers to evaluate talent retention and performance.

How it works: The core question a manager must ask themselves: 'If this person on my team came to me and said they were leaving today for a different opportunity, would I do everything I could to keep them at Netflix?' If the answer is no, the manager should initiate a conversation about whether the employee is in the right role.

### The Monkey Management Framework (Essential reading for product builders—part 1)
A framework for managers to identify when they've inadvertently taken on tasks ('monkeys') from their reports, and how to return ownership. The monkey transfers when the manager accepts responsibility and promises a progress report.

How it works: Key concept: A 'monkey' is the next move or action item in a work situation. Rules for monkey management:
1. Before a meeting, identify whose back the monkey is on (who has the next action).
2. After a meeting, the monkey should remain on or return to the subordinate's back.
3. Watch for two monkey-transfer signals: (a) Manager says 'Let me think about it and get back to you' — monkey jumps to manager. (b) Manager says 'Send me a memo on that' — monkey temporarily on subordinate but poised to leap back when manager reads memo and must respond.
4. If the manager accepts the monkey, the subordinate begins supervising the manager ('How's it coming?').
5. The fix: Ensure every monkey leaves the meeting on the subordinate's back. The subordinate owns the next move. Manager's role is to provide support, not do the work.
6. Subordinate-imposed time begins when a monkey leaps from subordinate to manager and doesn't end until it's returned.

### Three Horizons of Unblocking (Moving from IC product manager to manager of product managers)
A framework for categorizing the types of unblocking a PM manager should be doing across three time horizons

How it works: Short-term: Making sure decisions are made (e.g., resolving debates, providing clarity, breaking ties)
Medium-term: Hiring quickly to fill gaps (e.g., resourcing, recruiting, backfilling)
Long-term: Aligning everyone around a winning vision and strategy (e.g., strategic clarity, organizational alignment)

### Three Levels of Listening (Heidi Helfand, Rachel Lockett)
A coaching framework to evaluate and improve how you listen to others.

How it works: Level 1 - Internal: Listening while thinking about implications on yourself, distracted by inner dialogue. Level 2 - Focused: Can repeat back what the person is saying, problem-solving together. Level 3 - Global: Hearing what's communicated beyond words—body language, tone, context, emotions. Can reflect back more insight than the person is aware of. Great leaders use Level 3 when influencing, selling, pitching vision, and coaching.

### How to Select the Right Development Areas (The Power of Performance Reviews: Use This System to Become a Better Manager 🤝)
A checklist of criteria for identifying the 1-2 most impactful development areas for a direct report

How it works: When selecting development areas, consider:
- What is MOST holding the person back from the next level? Use your company's leveling system to explain your thinking.
- Do any clear themes emerge from the peer feedback or self-review?
- If your company does calibration, is there a key issue you're hearing from other managers? Even if you disagree, you need to address it.
- Even for the highest performers, identify something they can focus on to reach the next level.

Common mistakes to avoid:
- Including too many development areas (more than 2 makes it very hard to make meaningful progress on any)
- Not making development opportunities concrete enough (reports are left guessing at what you really mean)

Examples of development areas: reliable execution, increasing code quality, more succinct verbal communication.

### Three-Step Process for Addressing Manager Underperformance (Never Cover) (Five principles for successfully managing managers)
A three-step approach for skip leads to transparently handle a struggling direct-report manager instead of covering for them

How it works: When a direct-report manager is underperforming:
1. Clearly report the underperformance AND a plan to course correct up the management chain to your own leadership
2. Solicit feedback on the course correction from leaders up the management chain
3. Set clear performance expectations with the direct-report manager

Anti-pattern to avoid: Quietly solving the issue yourself, pulling double duty by taking on part of the line manager's job, and providing cover for the manager's performance with others. This is driven by shame, embarrassment, fear of confrontation, and insecurity—but leads to projects going sideways.

### When to Coach vs. When to Advise (Rachel Lockett)
Decision criteria for leaders to determine whether to coach (ask questions) or advise (tell the answer) when a team member brings a problem.

How it works: ADVISE when: 1) It's an urgent issue and you actually have the answer. 2) You have a specific outcome you want them to drive toward—don't make them guess. 3) The person doesn't have the skill they need and requires your guidance. 4) Someone asks you to share your specific experience (e.g., 'How did you take your company public?'). COACH when: 1) The person has the right context and can solve their own problem. 2) You want to equip them to handle similar problems independently. 3) You want to build their thinking skills, not just solve this instance. 4) You don't have deep enough context to provide the right answer. TIP: Ask 'Do you want advice or do you want some space to think about it?'

### Airbnb Host Referral Bonus Disagreement (Autonomy vs. direction - Issue 35)
A real example from Lenny's time at Airbnb where his team's data proved him wrong about increasing referral bonuses

How it works: Context: Lenny was overseeing a team optimizing the Airbnb host referral program. He was pushing them to keep increasing bonus amounts because historically each increase led to more growth. The team was not onboard and didn't prioritize it. Lenny was getting frustrated.

Resolution: The PM on the team put together a simple memo with the team's thought process, including a key chart that modeled the diminishing ROI on spending more in this area. With this new information, it was clear the team was right and Lenny was wrong.

Lesson: When you find your team heading in what you think is the wrong direction, come back to the facts. Assume you're wrong. What do you see that they don't? What do they see that you don't? They may know more than you give them credit for.
