## Frameworks

### Audience vs. Community Distinction (A founder’s guide to community)
A mental model for understanding the fundamental difference between building an audience and building a community

How it works: To build an audience, you help people.
To build a community, you help people help each other.

Traditionally, businesses create all the value for the consumer. Community-driven businesses create spaces for consumers to create value for each other.

Example: Lenny's Newsletter is an audience (Lenny creates valuable content). Lenny's community is a community (members support each other and contribute content). The amount of value created is no longer limited by Lenny's time — it's limited only by the motivation and ability of the community to contribute.

### Company values as growth ingredients (Rahul Vohra)
Baking the raw ingredients for word-of-mouth growth directly into company values

How it works: Superhuman's three growth-oriented values: 1) Create Delight - make something so joyful it brings people genuine delight. 2) Deliver Remarkable Quality - so striking and compelling that people can't help but tell others. 3) Build the Extraordinary - a measure of efficacy and innovativeness. These values directly fuel word-of-mouth growth by making the product inherently shareable.

### Dark Broadcasters Concept (Virality is a myth (mostly))
The concept that what looks like viral spread is often driven by invisible broadcasters pushing content to large audiences

How it works: 'Much of what outsiders call virality is really a function of what one might call "dark broadcasters"—people or companies distributing information to many viewers at once, but whose influence isn't always visible to people outside of the network.'

This explains why products appear to go viral organically when they're actually being broadcast by influential people or platforms whose reach isn't visible to outside observers. The guacamole recipe example: Multiple people talk about a NYT recipe, making it seem viral, but they all read the same newspaper—it was a broadcast, not person-to-person spread.

### Five Levers for Referral Program Optimization (Building a referrals program)
Five key dimensions to systematically optimize a referral program's performance.

How it works: 1. Eligibility — Who is allowed to send referrals?
2. Discoverability — Do users know the program exists?
3. Motivation — Do users have a compelling reason to spend time referring friends?
4. Conversion — Do users invite the right people, and enough of them?
5. Efficiency — Incremental revenue divided by incremental spend.

### K-Factor (Viral Coefficient) Definition (Virality is a myth (mostly))
Simple definition of viral growth using k-factor, the metric that determines whether a product is truly growing virally

How it works: Something is viral when the average new user brings more than one additional user. This is the viral coefficient or k-factor being higher than 1.0.

- K-factor > 1.0 = viral (exponential growth)
- K-factor = 1.0 = linear growth (each user brings exactly one new user)
- K-factor < 1.0 = sub-linear growth (growth slows over time without external inputs)

Important nuance: Cycle time between new users and referred users matters significantly. A k-factor of 1.1 with a 1-day cycle time grows much faster than k-factor of 1.1 with a 30-day cycle time.

### K-Factor Virality Formula (How to increase virality)
The standard formula for measuring viral growth, measuring the number of new users each existing user drives

How it works: K-factor formula:

i = number of invites sent by each customer
c = percent conversion of each invite
k = i × c

Implicit additional variable: number of active users (they are the fuel for the viral growth engine — they send the invites).

If k > 1, you have exponential viral growth.

Three levers to increase k:
1. Increase sharing rate (i) — more word-of-mouth, invitations, or experiential serendipity
2. Increase conversion rate (c) — getting new users through the funnel at a higher rate
3. Increase engaged users — getting more users and re-engaging existing users (more fuel on the fire)

### NPS to Viral Coefficient Correlation (Nilan Peiris)
A method of overlaying referral data onto NPS scores to see how advocacy scales with satisfaction.

How it works: Scores 1-6 have low invite rates. At 7-8, the number of people told doubles. At 8-9, it doubles again. At 9-10, it doubles again. Use the qualitative comments from 9-10s to identify the core product pillars to optimize.

### Purple Cow / Remarkability Test (Seth Godin)
A framework for evaluating whether your product has built-in word-of-mouth potential by being worth making a remark about.

How it works: Test questions: 1. Do people who engage with you stick around? (Steve Blank's customer traction) 2. Do they tell their friends? 3. What do they say? (You should know in advance what you want them to say) 4. Does the user's life get better if they talk about you? 5. Is there a built-in reason to share? (Network effect) If the answer to #4 is no, then why would they tell anyone? Remarkable = worth making a remark about. Not gimmickry—genuine utility that raises the sharer's status.

### Seven Types of Virality Taxonomy (How to increase virality)
A classification system of seven distinct types of virality organized into three categories, each with real product examples

How it works: Three categories, seven types:

**1. Word-of-mouth virality: Hearing about it from friends or colleagues**
   1. Offline: While chatting with a friend or colleague (e.g. Airbnb)
   2. Online: Seeing it mentioned on social media (e.g. Clubhouse)

**2. Invitation virality: Being actively invited by friends or colleagues**
   3. Social/Collaboration: To connect with friends or colleagues (e.g. WhatsApp, Figma)
   4. Incentivized: Because one or both sides will benefit (e.g. Pinduoduo)
   5. Utility: To accomplish something one-off with friends or colleagues (e.g. Calendly)

**3. Experiential virality: Seeing the product in action**
   6. Passively: Seeing the product as you go about your day (e.g. Citizen)
   7. Actively: Being sent a piece of content from the product, which leads you to the product (e.g. TikTok)

### Six Strategies to Increase Sharing Rate (How to increase virality)
Six distinct strategies for getting users to share your product more often, each with examples and 'How Might You' brainstorming prompts

How it works: Six strategies for increasing sharing rate:

1. **Nurture organic word-of-mouth** — Examples: Tinder, Airbnb, Zillow, Clubhouse, Levels
2. **Incentivize word-of-mouth and inviting** — Examples: Pinduoduo, Uber, Lyft, Airbnb, Robinhood, Dropbox, PayPal
3. **Make the product better when more friends or colleagues use it** — Examples: Slack, Figma, Snapchat, WhatsApp, Facebook, LinkedIn, Venmo, Reclaim, Clockwise
4. **Make the product *only* useful when using it with friends or colleagues** — Examples: Calendly, Zoom, Google Hangouts, DocuSign, Clerky
5. **Generate content that users want to share with friends** — Examples: TikTok, Instagram, YouTube, Pinterest, Spotify Wrapped, Reface, Airbnb listings
6. **Increase experiential serendipity** — Examples: Citizen, Hotmail/iPhone/Superhuman signatures, Tinder, Netflix, Uber/Lyft, Spotify, Dropbox

### Surprise and Delight for Organic Growth (Scott Belsky)
People don't talk about products doing what they expected — they talk about the unexpected. Optimize for Easter eggs, surprising features, and moments of magic to drive word of mouth.

How it works: Example: Tesla owners don't talk about having a great drive — they talk about Easter eggs on the dashboard or cool seasonal features they discovered. Application: Dedicate product effort to things users wouldn't expect the product to do. Creates surprise → develops relationship with product → drives organic conversation.

### Three Levers for Increasing Virality (How to increase virality)
The three core levers for increasing viral growth, tied directly to the K-factor formula

How it works: Three levers for increasing virality:

1. **Increasing sharing rate (i)**: More word-of-mouth, invitations, or experiential serendipity. This is the most interesting and actionable lever. Contains 6 sub-strategies:
   - Nurture organic word-of-mouth
   - Incentivize word-of-mouth and inviting
   - Make the product better when more friends/colleagues use it
   - Make the product only useful with friends/colleagues
   - Generate content users want to share
   - Increase experiential serendipity

2. **Increasing conversion rate (c)**: Getting new users through the funnel at a higher rate. Focus on the full funnel from mention/invite sent → receiving user sharing their own invite/mention.

3. **Increasing engaged users**: Getting more users and re-engaging existing users. More fuel on the fire. Examples: Facebook's People You May Know, photo tagging, TikTok duets, Venmo social feed, Stories features.

### Three Types of Product Virality Strategies (Product-led marketing)
A framework for systematically accelerating both internal and external product virality

How it works: Two types of virality:
- External virality: Multiplayer use and product exposure (e.g., Zoom, Loom, Typeform). Hard to manufacture if not natural.
- Internal virality: Within-organization adoption. Applies to nearly every bottom-up PLG product.

Three strategies to accelerate virality:

1. Add and promote social features: Build features that require multi-user participation (Calendly's group meetings, collective meetings, round-robin scheduling driving internal virality beyond their classic external loop).

2. Remove friction from being social: Eliminate barriers to inviting/trying. Create bridge tiers (Loom's Creator Lite: 25 free videos with 5-min limit). Don't block invitations — use Figma's approach of free editor invites with pre-billing admin notifications.

3. Add social elements around your product: Shareable milestones and analytics, URL-enabled watermarks for free users, Slack notifications, and community building (Webflow's 75K-member Discourse community). Design community for and with your users.

Measurement proxy: Direct traffic via Google Analytics — the share of people who come directly to your website.

### Three-Phase Referral Program Build (Airbnb Model) (Building a referrals program)
Phased approach to building and scaling a referral program, based on how Airbnb evolved its host referral program from nascent to top growth driver.

How it works: Phase 1: 'It Exists!'
- Simple incentive: flat-fee credit for both referrer and referee (Airbnb used $25 travel credit for guests, $75 for hosts)
- Limited discoverability: placed in nav and footer only
- Limited platforms: web only

Phase 2: 'Optimized' (focus on one side first)
- Increase discoverability: add many more touchpoints throughout the user experience, add to mobile
- Optimize messaging: A/B test and optimize the referral pitch copy
- Dedicate resources: assign a fully dedicated team to this program

Phase 3: 'Expanded' (extend to other side / segments)
- Expand the program: make the referral pitch a first-class citizen with its own landing page
- Improve the incentive: switch to cash award if credit isn't motivating enough (Airbnb went from travel credit to flat $100 cash for referrer, $50 for referee)
- Increase efficiency: create personalized referral amounts based on the LTV of the supply/user the referrer is likely to drive

### Virality Measurement Framework for Bottom-Up SaaS (The most important bottom-up SaaS metrics to track)
A two-tier framework for measuring how a product spreads organically within organizations, from basic to advanced metrics

How it works: **Tier 1 - Core virality metrics (track early):**
- Invite rate: % of new users who sent at least one invite in the first X days
- Invite conversion rate: % of users who receive an invite that sign-up in the next X days
- Virality factor: % of new users who have come from an invite

**Tier 2 - Advanced virality metrics (track later):**
- Invite volume: When an invite was sent, median number of invites sent per user
- Velocity of virality: Median days from 1→N seats at a company
- Traction: Number of total companies with at least 3 users signed-up
- Leads: Top domain names with most users (to target outreach)

The distinction matters because Tier 1 tells you IF virality is working, while Tier 2 tells you HOW WELL and WHERE it's working.

### WHY Build a Referral Program (Three Reasons) (Building a referrals program)
Three strategic reasons to justify investing in a referral program.

How it works: 1. Impact — At companies where it works best, 15%-50% of new user growth comes from referrals. Referrals are tied for second most common early growth lever for marketplace supply.
2. Efficiency — Instead of competing for keywords and placement on Google/FB, you leverage your existing user base. When done right, programs pay for themselves many times over. You can easily test and tune incentives to improve efficiency.
3. Scalability — Takes a very small team to build and operate. Airbnb initially had just 3 people (eng, design, PM), and you can get started with even less.

### Word-of-Mouth Growth Prerequisites (Uri Levine)
Two conditions that enable word-of-mouth growth: high frequency of use OR extreme coolness/uniqueness.

How it works: Path 1: High frequency of use — daily usage creates daily opportunities to tell someone (Waze example: 95-99% heard about it from someone). Path 2: Coolness/uniqueness — compensates for low frequency (Airbnb example: ~70% word-of-mouth despite 1-2x/year usage because it was uniquely cool). If neither condition exists: You are 'sentenced to acquire users/customers all of your lifetime' through paid channels.

### Growth Strategy Action Plan for 'Viral' Products (Virality is a myth (mostly))
Two-part action plan for founders and growth leaders building products that aim to grow through virality

How it works: If you're a founder or growth leader building a product that aims to grow through virality:

1. OPTIMIZE VIRALITY MECHANISMS:
   - Facilitate word of mouth
   - Build invite flows
   - Create referral programs
   - Build a remarkable product worth talking about
   - Goal: Raise your k-factor as high as possible

2. INVEST IN ONE-TO-MANY BROADCASTS (more important):

### Incentivized Virality Optimization Checklist (How to increase virality)
Six 'How Might You' brainstorming prompts for optimizing incentive-driven virality

How it works: To optimize incentive-driven virality:
1. How might you create a selfish incentive for users to bring in new users? (e.g. free money, free storage)
2. How might you create an altruistic incentive for users bringing in new users? (e.g. save your friend money)
3. How might you create FOMO which motivates users to send invites? (e.g. a limited number of invites like Gmail, Clubhouse, Spotify, Superhuman, OnePlus)
4. How might you increase an existing incentive? (e.g. double your existing bonus)
5. How might you surface your existing incentives more prominently? (e.g. a large banner, more entry points)
6. How might you make it easier for your users to know *who* to invite? (e.g. import contact list, connect to Twitter to find friends)

### Network Effect Optimization Checklist (How to increase virality)

Three key criteria for designing an effective referral program

### Referral Program Pro Tips (Building a referrals program)
Five expert-level tips for running and scaling a referral program, drawn from Airbnb's experience.

How it works: 1. Pitch referrals everywhere — Don't expect users to immediately discover, understand, AND be motivated to refer. Repeat the pitch at every touchpoint, highlight the incentive, and don't rely on one-off campaigns.
2. Understand cannibalization — Not all referred users are incremental; many would have signed up anyway. Figure out the right haircut for your numbers and be conservative in your estimates.
3. Invest in data science — You'll be an order of magnitude more successful if you have a data scientist deeply involved in planning and iterations.
4. Incentives plateau — Doubling the bonus doesn't always make a big dent. There's a ceiling beyond which you can't motivate people further while maintaining positive ROI.
5. Watch for fraud — The juicier the incentive, the more likely fraud becomes. Design your program so executing fraud is more expensive than benefiting from it.

### Referral Program Readiness Checklist (Building a referrals program)
Five qualifying criteria to evaluate before investing in building a referral program. Use this to decide go/no-go.

How it works: Evaluate your company against these five criteria:
1. The majority of your current growth comes from word-of-mouth — the referral incentive adds fuel to an existing fire. Referrals don't work without strong existing WOM.
2. You have lots of existing users — Do the math: expect less than 10% of your users to convert a successful referral. If you have only a few thousand users, you won't drive hundreds of new users on an ongoing basis.
3. You're able to offer a meaningful incentive — An extra $10 likely won't cut it. Talk to your users to find what genuinely excites them (may not be cash). Verify your CAC/LTV can support it.
4. Your users know a lot of other potential users — Best for consumer or SMB products. Example: Airbnb hosts know other folks with homes. Counter-example: a head of HR may not know many other heads of HR.
5. Your product requires a lot of trust to use — Referrals and WOM are highest leverage when the product is a little bit scary to use (e.g., staying in a stranger's home, leaving your dog with someone you just met).

### Social Elements to Add Around Your Product (Product-led marketing)
A list of social and viral elements to layer around a PLG product

How it works: Consider adding:
1. Shareable milestones from the product experience (analytics, insights, reports)
2. Easy sharing mechanisms: email, IRL, Slack notifications
3. Watermarks on free/self-service tier output (bonus: make watermark URL-enabled)
4. Reserve watermark removal for paid tiers
5. Community spaces designed for and with your users (not just another Slack group)
6. User referral programs with gamified incentives (e.g., storage bonuses)

Detailed real-world example of how Airbnb built and scaled its host referral program from a half-functional feature to the single biggest driver of attributable supply growth.

Results:

### Dropbox's Referral Program Growth (Generating buzz)

### Duolingo Failed Referral Program (How Duolingo reignited user growth)
A cautionary example of borrowing a referral strategy that doesn't fit the product's monetization model

How it works: What they did: Created a referral program similar to Uber's. Reward was a free month of Super Duolingo (premium subscription).

Why they thought it would work: Uber had reputedly grown largely because of its referral program.

Why it underperformed: Referrals work for Uber because riders pay on a never-ending pay-as-you-go system—a free ride is a constant incentive. Duolingo's best and most active users already had Super Duolingo subscriptions, so they couldn't receive the free month reward. The strategy excluded the exact power users it needed to rely on.

Result: New users increased by only 3%. Positive but not the breakthrough needed. Team iterated but couldn't improve further.

Lesson: Change in context (subscription vs. pay-per-use) fundamentally altered the feature's effectiveness.

### Yahoo Research on Twitter Virality (2012) (Virality is a myth (mostly))
Academic research proving that content on Twitter doesn't actually spread virally

How it works: Key findings from 2012 Yahoo study of millions of Twitter messages:
- 90%+ of messages didn't diffuse at all
- Only ~1% were shared more than 7 times
- Nothing went fully viral—not even the most popular messages
- 95% of news people see on Twitter comes directly from the original source or one degree of separation
- Conclusion: Popularity on the Internet is 'driven by the size of the largest broadcast'
