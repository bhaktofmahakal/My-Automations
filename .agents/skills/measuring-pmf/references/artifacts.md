# Measuring Product-Market Fit - Frameworks, Templates & Checklists

## Frameworks

### 5-Step B2B Product-Market Fit Progression (A guide for finding product-market fit in B2B)
A progressive framework for moving toward product-market fit in B2B, derived from interviews with 20+ successful founders. Each step represents a deeper level of PMF validation.

How it works: Step 1: Get one company to love your product — Obsess over making one customer successful. Do whatever it takes. Forward-deploy engineers if needed. Spend 50% of founding team time talking to customers.

Step 2: Get one company to pay (a meaningful amount of money) for your product — Target 5-6 figure annual deals. Charge more than you think you should. If customers ask to pay you, that's a strong signal.

Step 3: Get more than one company to love and pay for your product — Expand to 3-10 paid customers at increasing price points. Each new customer should increase your confidence.

Step 4: Start noticing a shift from push to pull, and organic growth — Look for cold inbound from strangers, word of mouth, feature request documents, and a visceral feeling of the product taking on a life of its own.

Step 5: Keep growing consistently — Sustain week-over-week or month-over-month growth in users and revenue. With each passing month, confidence in lasting PMF grows.

### 80/10/10 Time Allocation for Pre-PMF Startups (Prioritizing at startups)
A recommended breakdown of how to allocate time across three buckets when working toward PMF

How it works: Allocate your time across three buckets:

1. INCREASE THE VALUE OF YOUR PRODUCT (~80% of your time): Put most of your resources into reducing users' pain, saving them time, and helping them do better work. Sub-categories:
   a. Blockers: What's keeping users from quickly accomplishing the task they want to accomplish?
   b. Retentive features: What features would keep the largest number of people using your product longer? When people churn, identify what's missing.
   c. Acquisition features: What one feature would directly lead to the most new customers? What's getting in the way of potential customers being able to use or wanting to use your product?
   d. Differentiators: Double down on what makes your product different from alternatives. You often win not by being better but by being different.

2. IMPROVE ONBOARDING (~10% of your time): Make it easier for new users to experience your product's value. If a great product exists and no one is able to use it, does it even exist?

3. CREATE DELIGHT (~10% of your time): Delight users with humor, design, personality, surprises, or simply quick turnaround on issues.

### Affirmation Bias vs. Pull Signals (Raaz Herzberg)
A framework for distinguishing genuine product-market fit signals from polite customer interest. Polite signals include 'sounds interesting, keep me updated.' Real pull signals include asking about pricing, requesting POV timelines, and immediately connecting you to team members who would deploy the product.

How it works: Weak signals (affirmation bias): 'Sounds interesting,' 'Love to hear more,' 'Please update me.' Strong pull signals: Asking about pricing, Asking about POV timeline and process, Immediately offering to connect you to the deployment team, Filling out detailed questionnaires quickly, Pushing for next steps proactively. Key insight: Humans have a natural bias to look for affirmation rather than listening for deep enthusiasm. Customers aren't incentivized to tell you they don't understand your pitch.

### Alternative Early-Stage PMF Signals (When ARR Growth Is Slow) (What is a good growth rate)
Leading indicators investors accept as evidence of product-market fit when ARR growth hasn't materialized yet

How it works: If you lack fast ARR growth, demonstrate: 1. Cohorted revenue retention — ideally U-shaped cohorts where net revenue retention increases over a year. 2. Marketing spend/revenue efficiency ratio. 3. Salesperson productivity (for sales-led businesses). 4. Oddly short sales cycles. 5. Customers raving and telling friends (leading to low CAC). 6. High engagement intensity (e.g., people who use the app 10 times per day). 7. Accelerating MoM growth (e.g., going from 10% to 12% to 15%) showing momentum pickup. Key warning from Ellen Chisa: 'It's a huge mistake to focus on the growth number before getting retention.' From Kanyi Maqubela: Speed from $500K to $5M ARR is more predictive than speed from $0 to $1M, as the latter phase still involves optimizing retention and segmenting customers.

### B2B PMF Timeline Benchmarks (A guide for finding product-market fit in B2B)

2. From working product to feeling PMF: 9-18 months
3. Time to get alpha product out the door: 1-3 months (for most companies)

Warning signals:

- Seriously worry if 3+ years without feeling PMF

Key insight: Unless you think you're building the next Figma, get your V1 out quickly.

### Burn Multiple (How to know if you've got product-market fit)
A metric to measure growth efficiency as a proxy for product-market fit. Lower burn multiples indicate the market is pulling product out of the startup; higher multiples indicate pushing product onto the market.

How it works: Formula: Burn Multiple = Net Burn / Net New ARR

Interpretation:
- Lower burn multiple = more efficient growth = stronger PMF signal (market pulling product out of startup)
- Higher burn multiple = less efficient growth = weaker PMF signal (startup pushing product onto market)

Example: A startup generating $1M in ARR by burning $2M (burn multiple = 2x) is more impressive than one generating $1M by burning $5M (burn multiple = 5x)

VCs use this to make inferences about product-market fit.

### CAC < LTV as PMF Definition (How to know if you've got product-market fit)
The unit economics definition of product-market fit: you've found PMF when you can repeatably acquire customers for less than their lifetime value.

How it works: Three complementary framings:
1. Elizabeth Yin: PMF = repeatably acquiring customers for a lower cost than what they are worth to you
2. Nikhyl Singhal: PMF = value of each user > cost of bringing them into the product AND there are enough customers out there AND you can efficiently bring them in
3. Casey Winters: PMF = retention that creates enough $ or content/virality to drive sustainable acquisition

Key nuance from Casey Winters: It's not just CAC < LTV in isolation — the retention must generate enough value (monetary or viral) to fund the acquisition engine sustainably.

### Cohort Retention Curve Analysis for PMF (How to know if you've got product-market fit)
A method for determining product-market fit by plotting cohort retention over time and checking if the curve flattens rather than declining to zero.

How it works: Steps:
1. Define a cohort of users who started using the product in a given time period (e.g., one month)
2. Plot the percentage of those users who remain active over subsequent time periods (e.g., 12 months later)
3. Expect a deep drop-off in month 1 — that's OK
4. Look for whether the curve flattens at some point
5. If it flattens: a group of customers is finding sustained value = PMF for at least those customers
6. Benchmark your flattening retention rate against comparable products in your vertical that have achieved significant growth
7. True PMF = the ability to acquire users at less cost than the revenue/value generated by that retention curve

Key insight (Jeff Chang): Different product types have different PMF retention benchmarks — find comparable products that have scaled successfully to identify the right target for your vertical.

### Cohort-Based User Retention Benchmarks (6-month) (What is good monthly churn)
GOOD and GREAT 6-month user retention benchmarks by business type, used as the best predictor of product-market fit

How it works: Consumer Social: ~25% is GOOD, ~45% is GREAT. Consumer Transactional: ~30% is GOOD, ~50% is GREAT. Consumer SaaS: ~40% is GOOD, ~70% is GREAT. SMB/Mid-Market SaaS: ~60% is GOOD, ~80% is GREAT. Enterprise SaaS: ~70% is GOOD, ~90% is GREAT.

### Consumer Startup Engagement Benchmarks (Pre-Series A) (What is a good growth rate)
Key engagement and retention thresholds that consumer investors look for before caring about growth

How it works: Excellent engagement thresholds: >50% DAU/MAU ratio OR >50% D30 retention. If you have both, then focus on growth. For social apps: Ideally all or almost all acquisition is organic. Look at D1, D7, D30 retention, time on app, frequency of posting. Consumer messaging apps should be growing exponentially by default. For subscription apps: Look at m12 'inflection point' when annual subscribers decide whether to renew. Usage retention in prior months is a leading indicator. For marketplaces: Look at both user and GMV retention on supply and demand sides. Non-transactional consumer (media): Awesome K-factor leading to distribution advantage. Transactional consumer (marketplaces/e-commerce): 20%+ MoM organic growth.

### Drug Discovery Analogy for Product-Market Fit (Matt MacInnis)
A mental model for understanding product launches and market reception.

How it works: The market is immutable. Your product is a drug, and you are testing if the market has the 'binding receptors' for it. You cannot market or launch your way into creating receptors that don't exist.

### Enterprise Trial-Pull Test (How to know if you've got product-market fit)
A qualitative PMF test for enterprise products: pull the free trial and see if the customer screams.

How it works: Method: At the end of a free trial period, pull access to the product.

Interpretation:
- If the customer screams / protests = PMF signal (they are desperate for the product)
- If the customer doesn't scream = No PMF (they aren't going to buy at the end of 30 days)

Key insight: If they aren't desperate, you don't have PMF.

### Ex-Growth Company Assessment (Nikhyl Singhal)
Criteria to identify if a company is trapped in a post-hypergrowth phase without true product-market fit.

How it works: Two questions to ask: 1) Are we scaling a product with a 'sucking sound' of customer pull, or are we still trying to find it? 2) Is our valuation in the hundreds of millions while still searching for that pull? If yes to both, it's an ex-growth company and equity is likely overvalued.

### Five PMF Journey Archetypes (How to kickstart and scale a consumer business—Step 5: RETAIN: Iterate until enough people stick around)
Five patterns (plus one sad one) that describe how consumer companies typically find product-market fit over time

How it works: 1. Lightning in a bottle: Immediately clear, up and to the right (Examples: Instagram, Tinder, Patreon, Robinhood, Dropbox)

2. Delayed lightning: Eventually instantly clear, but after 6-18 months of iterating on product, distribution, or business model (Examples: Netflix after 18 months, Uber after 1 year, YouTube after ~6 months, Snapchat after ~6 months, Discord after 3 months)

3. Foothold: Very clear with just a narrow segment, unclear if it'll work broadly, but with growing confidence as you expand. Usually takes 3-18 months. (Examples: Instacart ~1 year, Lyft ~1 month, Cameo 3 years, Superhuman 4 years, Product Hunt ~3 months)

4. Milestone: Glimmers early on, and with steady and consistent growth you hit a meaningful milestone that finally tells you it's working. Usually takes 6-24 months. (Examples: Substack ~1.5 years, DoorDash 9-12 months, Duolingo ~1 year, Outschool ~6 months, Rover 9-12 months, Airbnb ~2 years)

5. Grind: 2-10 years of iterating, and eventually you get there (Examples: Thumbtack 5 years, Coinbase ~2 years, Udemy 2-3 years, OpenTable 2 years, Hipcamp 2 years)

6. 15 minutes of fame (sad archetype): Initially looks like PMF but then fizzles out (Examples: Clubhouse, Yik Yak, Poparazzi, Path, Vine)

Key takeaways:
- Over 80% found PMF within 2 years
- Less than 20% felt clear PMF immediately
- If not immediate, most took 6-18 months
- Most got there by iterating on product or finding effective distribution
- When they got there, the moment was very clear

### Four Levels of Product-Market Fit (Todd Jackson)
A progression framework defining four stages of PMF (nascent, developing, strong, extreme) with specific benchmarks, focus areas, and yellow flags for each level

How it works: Level 1 (Nascent): 3-5 customers, 0-500K ARR, <10 people, focus on satisfaction. Level 2 (Developing): 5-25 customers, 500K-5M ARR, up to 20 people, focus on demand. Level 3 (Strong): 25-100 customers, 5-25M ARR, 30-100 people, focus on efficiency. Level 4 (Extreme): 100+ customers, 25M+ ARR, 100+ people, focus on TAM expansion. Three dimensions tracked across all levels: satisfaction, demand, efficiency.

### Four PMF Iteration Paths (How to kickstart and scale a consumer business—Step 5: RETAIN: Iterate until enough people stick around)
A diagnostic framework for identifying which of four common problems is preventing product-market fit and what to do about each

How it works: 1. Wrong product → Iterate on the product
   - Add core features (Duolingo added streaks, improved teaching)
   - Add essential missing functionality (Coinbase added buy Bitcoin, Superhuman added mobile app + calendar)
   - Add supply to marketplace (Instacart added retail partners, Lyft/Airbnb/Thumbtack built supply)
   - Change the business model (Netflix: No Due Dates + No Late Fees + Subscription; Udemy: decided to charge for courses)

2. Right product, wrong distribution → Iterate on distribution
   - Find the right channel (YouTube → MySpace embeds; Discord → Reddit; Snapchat → high school word of mouth)
   - Warning: Don't assume your product is right and just needs distribution

3. Right product, wrong onboarding → Iterate on onboarding
   - Try manually onboarding users (Superhuman's 1:1 onboarding)
   - Make sure new users experience value quickly
   - Increase odds new users have a great first experience
   - Get more users through the flow by reducing friction
   - Key insight: Month 1-3 churn can often be attributed to activation/onboarding failure

4. Right product, wrong audience → Change your target audience
   - Pinterest: shifted from tech friends to female bloggers
   - Ask: Is there a different super-specific who that may benefit from this product?

### Four Phases of a Startup (Uri Levine)
Sequential phases that require singular focus — doing multiple at once leads to failure.

How it works: Phase 1: All-over-the-place — thinking about everything but need to quickly focus. Phase 2: Product-market fit — the only thing that matters; need developers and product lead, not sales or marketing. Phase 3a (if high-frequency use): Growth first — word-of-mouth drives growth naturally, figure out business model after. Phase 3b (if low-frequency use): Business model first — you'll be acquiring customers forever, so know the economics before scaling. Phase 4: The remaining one (growth or business model). Key rule: Focus is about what you're NOT doing. Shift gears completely between phases.

### Four Signs of Product-Market Fit (PMF Confidence Spectrum) (How to kickstart and scale a consumer business—Step 5: RETAIN: Iterate until enough people stick around)
A framework presenting four signs of PMF in decreasing order of confidence, treating PMF as a spectrum rather than a binary state

How it works: Sign #1 (Highest confidence): Cohort retention curves flatten - Track the percentage of users who continue to use your product x months/weeks/days later. If the curve flattens above 0%, some part of the market wants your product.

Sign #2: Explosive growth through word of mouth - Exponential organic growth driven by word of mouth, not paid acquisition. Users are buying/downloading as fast as you can build. 'Chasing a boulder' vs 'pushing a boulder.'

Sign #3: Sean Ellis survey - Ask users 'How disappointed would you be if this product no longer existed?' If 40%+ say 'very disappointed,' you likely have PMF.

Sign #4 (Lowest confidence): Visceral excitement - Strong, immediate reactions like pupil dilation, 'where have you been all my life,' users screaming when you pull the trial, or calling when service goes down.

### Growth Rate Benchmarks by ARR Stage (B2B SaaS) (What is a good growth rate)
A comprehensive table of what investors consider 'good' and 'great' growth rates at each ARR milestone, from pre-$1M through $100M+

How it works: Pre-$1M ARR: Good = reach $1M ARR within 12 months of launch; Great = reach $1M ARR within 9 months of launch. MoM growth at this stage: 15%-25% range as a rough benchmark, but investors don't focus on this. $1M-$5M ARR (Series A): Good = 3x YoY; Great = 4-5x YoY. $5M-$10M ARR (Series B): Good = 2.5-3x YoY; Great = 3x+ YoY. $10M-$25M ARR: Good = 2x YoY; Great = 2.5x+ YoY. $25M-$50M ARR: Good = 1.8-2x YoY; Great = 2x+ YoY. $50M-$100M ARR: Good = 1.5-1.8x YoY; Great = 2x+ YoY. Classic trajectory: 'Triple, triple, double, double, double' — i.e. $1M → $3M → $9M → $18M → $36M → $72M.

### Growth Rate Benchmarks by Funding Round (What is a good growth rate)
Expected YoY growth rates mapped to Series A, B, and C rounds

How it works: Series A (~$1-5M revenue): Good = 2-3x YoY; Great = 3x+ YoY. Series B (~$5-10M revenue): Good = 2-2.5x YoY; Great = 3x+ YoY. Series C (~$10M+ revenue): Good = 1.5-2x YoY; Great = 2x+ YoY. These benchmarks apply to B2B, B2C, and GMV businesses. For GMV businesses, both GMV and take rate should be growing at healthy rates.

### J-Curve Retention Analysis (Robby Stein)
Evaluate product-market fit by tracking the percentage of users still using the product at day 7, 30, and 90 to see if retention flattens or decays to zero

How it works: The J-curve shows retention over time (D7, D30, D90). If it flattens: some users find lasting value — you have something. If it decays toward zero: no one will use it on a long enough timeline — you're toast. Gates: 1) Does it flatten? (core value exists) 2) Can you get more people through? (growth) 3) How big can this get? (TAM). Every product starts small — even viral ones started with 100 people internally.

### Latent Demand (Boris Cherny)
A mental model for finding product-market fit by observing unintended product usage.

How it works: Look at how users abuse a product to do something useful (e.g., 40% of Facebook group posts were buying/selling, leading to FB Marketplace). Modern AI framing: Look at what the model is trying to do and make that easier, rather than boxing it into a strict workflow.

### Make 10 Customers Very Happy (Prioritizing at startups)
The single goal for pre-PMF startups that everything should be prioritized against

How it works: Core principle: Your single goal as an early-stage pre-PMF startup should be to make 10 customers VERY happy. Everything you prioritize should be in service of this goal.

Why 10? If you can make 10 customers very happy, you can probably make 100 customers very happy. From there, you can continue to add value, reduce friction, build your growth engine, etc. If you cannot make 10 customers very happy, it's unlikely you have a business.

Why VERY happy? There are endless products fighting for your customers' attention. The bar for people continuing to come back to (and pay for) your product is much higher than you think. When you've solved a real problem, people will beg you to take their money.

How to measure: 'Very happy' is just another way to describe product-market fit. Look at cohort retention numbers once you have data.

### Marginal Customer Test (Todd Jackson)
A concept for measuring PMF progress — the next incremental customer should be getting easier and easier to acquire and serve over time

How it works: If you have progressing PMF, the marginal (next) customer should be: easier to acquire, easier to deliver good service to, and less expensive to support. If the marginal customer is getting harder, you may be stuck or regressing. This is the core diagnostic across all levels.

### Market Signal vs. Support Signal (Lessons learned from a startup that didn’t make it)
A framework for distinguishing true market pull from founder-driven momentum and supporter enthusiasm

How it works: FALSE SIGNALS (people rooting for you, not market signal):
1. Investor checks at seed stage — often a broad bet on the team, not validation of strategy
2. Founder-led deals — early deals done on the backs of founders' personal relationships
3. Supporter enthusiasm — people who like you, trust you, and want you to succeed

THE TEST: Can the car move when founders stop pushing?
- If founding team must be involved in every deal → not true market signal
- If product can't retain/grow without founder involvement → not true market signal
- If customers bought because of relationship, not product → not true market signal

DANGER: Broad value propositions and multiple use cases make this worse — supporters can accelerate you in the wrong direction because they're buying the relationship, not the specific solution.

'HAPPY EARS' WARNING: All founders suffer from chronic confirmation bias in conversations with customers, partners, team members, and investors. Irrational confidence gets compounded when support is mistaken for signal.

### Message-Market Fit vs. Product-Market Fit vs. Product-User Fit vs. Product-Buyer Fit (A guide for finding product-market fit in B2B)
A framework for understanding the different types of 'fit' that precede or comprise full product-market fit.

How it works: Message-Market Fit (Eric Glyman, Ramp): Your positioning and messaging resonates even before the product fully delivers. Tested via:
- Launch press coverage interest
- Cold outbound email response rates
- SDR testing of different messages
- Elevator pitch reactions (do people 'get it' or are they turned off?)

Product-User Fit (Guy Podjarny, Snyk): Users love and actively use the product, but you haven't figured out how to get them to pay. Can be achieved quickly (within a year).

Product-Buyer Fit (Guy Podjarny, Snyk): Aligning with commercial needs and getting people to pay. Can take a full extra year beyond product-user fit.

Full Product-Market Fit: The combination of users loving, paying for, and organically spreading your product, across expanding market segments.

### Opposites Approach to Avoiding Overspecialization (Lessons on building a viral consumer app: The story of Saturn)
A method for testing product-market fit across diverse user segments by deliberately launching in communities that are demographically opposite to your initial ones

How it works: Problem: When developing products for small audiences/communities, there's a risk of overspecializing for a specific area, community, or type of user.

Solution: Use an 'approach around opposites' to test fit across diverse segments:
- If launching urban schools → try suburban schools
- If predominantly affluent schools → try schools with students from more mixed economic backgrounds
- Vary by region, age, income level, etc.

Saturn's example: First schools were all in southwestern Connecticut (very affluent region). Next launched schools in more economically diverse parts of New Jersey, Massachusetts, etc.

Why it matters:
1. Ensures the product has fit with students/users from different backgrounds and different types of communities
2. Critical for raising venture capital—investors will always want to know the 'bounds' of users you have tested with across age, income level, region, etc.
3. The more types of users you've PROVEN your product can win over, the better.

### PMF Assumption Falsification Approach (What to ask your users about Product-Market Fit)
A research philosophy for evaluating PMF by trying to disprove your product's underlying assumptions rather than confirm them

How it works: As you collect data, try to falsify your product's underlying assumptions rather than confirm them. Key questions to investigate:

1. Is it true that people are experiencing the problem your product sets out to solve in the first place?
2. What motivated them to try it in the first place?
3. What was the magical way in which they were hoping it might make their life or some task even a little bit better?

Understanding people's motivations for using what you've built can sometimes be surprising, but can also lead you down unexpected new paths to achieving a strong product-market fit.

Product-market fit happens only when a set of underlying assumptions end up being true. Break down what those assumptions might be for your product, and use questions that measure your progress toward confirming (or disconfirming) them.

### PMF Confidence Progression (This Week #3: Finding product/market fit, interviewing for a Director of PM role, and structuring discussions with senior leaders)
A progression model for building confidence in product/market fit across stages of product development

How it works: PMF is not a binary, big-bang event — you get clearer and clearer about what the market wants. The progression of confidence-building activities:

1. **Conversations** (earliest stage): Talk to potential customers, look for passion, skin in the game, and a clear why
2. **Wireframes & Prototypes**: Show tangible concepts to increase signal fidelity
3. **Actual product usage**: Get people to use the product
4. **Long-term retention measurement**: Enough people continue to use it for an extended period
5. **Superhuman PMF survey**: Measure level of 'disappointment' if your product disappeared (reference: https://firstround.com/review/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/)

Warning: Watch out for 'fools gold' — lots of initial traction followed by quick fade (examples: Yo app, CryptoKitties).

### PMF Intensity Matrix: Fit × Market Size (What it feels like when you've found product-market fit)
A mental model explaining why PMF feels different for different companies, based on product fit quality and initial market size

How it works: The intensity of market pull is a factor of two variables: 1. Fit — How good your product is at solving the user's problem. 2. Initial market size — Is the initial target niche or broad? Combinations: - 10x better product + Huge initial market = Sudden and broad pull (Examples: Dropbox, Netflix, Tinder). - 10x better product + Narrow initial customer set = Steady and compounding pull (Examples: Instacart, Superhuman, Substack). This explains why some founders describe PMF as 'like getting pressed into the back of your seat' while others describe it as 'a calm breeze that becomes a powerful wind.'

### PMF Research Method Selection (What to ask your users about Product-Market Fit)
A simple decision framework for choosing between quantitative survey and qualitative interviews based on user base size

How it works: **Decision rule:**
- If you can easily reach 100+ active users → Use a survey (3 questions covering affection, utility, behavior)
- If you cannot easily reach 100+ active users → Conduct 1:1 interviews with 6-12 users (3 questions covering workflows, competitive preference, motivation)

In both cases, use multiple approaches and triangulate between them for the most reliable signal.

### PMF Signal Hierarchy (Pre-Product and Post-Product) (How to know if you've got product-market fit)
A structured taxonomy of product-market fit signals organized into pre-product (2 signals) and post-product (7 signals), sorted from most concrete/measurable to least concrete. Used as a diagnostic checklist to assess where you stand on PMF.

How it works: PRE-PRODUCT SIGNALS:
1. Visible excitement — Pupils dilate during demos, 'where have you been all my life' reactions, literal love letters (Steve Blank, Elad Gil)
2. People are willing to pay for it now — Send invoices for early access before building; works even for consumer apps (Lenny)

POST-PRODUCT SIGNALS (most to least concrete):
1. Retention: Users stick around — Cohort retention curve flattens over time (Brian Balfour, Casey Winters, Jeff Chang)
2. Surveys: 40%+ 'very disappointed' — Sean Ellis test; for enterprise, pull the trial and see if customer screams (Sean Ellis/Rahul Vohra, Doug Leone/Andy Rachleff)
3. Exponential organic growth — Word of mouth, spontaneous referrals, >50% organic/direct traffic (Andy Rachleff, Michael Seibel, Sam Altman, Merci Victoria Grace, Elad Gil)
4. Cost-efficient growth — Low burn multiple (Net Burn / Net New ARR), sales yield > 1.0 (David Sacks, Andy Rachleff)
5. CAC < LTV — Repeatably acquire customers for less than their value; retention creates enough $/virality for sustainable acquisition (Elizabeth Yin, Nikhyl Singhal, Casey Winters)
6. Customers clamor for your product — Buying as fast as you can make it, usage outpaces servers, reporters calling, grabbing product out of your hands (Marc Andreessen, Steve Blank, Emmett Shear)
7. People are using it even when it's broken — High retention despite poor product quality, growing despite poor execution (Elad Gil, Brett Berson, Don Valentine)

### PMF Timeline Expectations (Todd Jackson)
Rough expected timeline for progressing through the four levels of product-market fit for B2B companies

How it works: Total journey to Level 4: approximately 4-6 years. Level 1 (Nascent): 12-18 months — choosing the right persona and problem. Level 2 (Developing): ~1 year — going from 5 to 25 customers quickly. Level 3 (Strong): ~2 years — scaling from 5M to 25M ARR. Level 4 (Extreme): ~1-2 years — scaling from 25M to 100M+ ARR. If stuck at any level for 12-18 months, consider a significant pivot. If stuck for 4-5 years total without market pull, consider returning money or soft landing.

### PMF is a Spectrum, Not Binary (A guide for finding product-market fit in B2B)
The mental model that product-market fit is never a yes/no state but a continuous process of finding fit with larger market segments.

How it works: Key insight: PMF is always with respect to a particular market segment. You have PMF with one group but not the next. The group you haven't achieved PMF with is always the one you want to target.

Examples:
- YouTube at 100M DAUs: Had teenagers but not adults. Envious of Netflix/Facebook satisfaction.
- Databricks at $100M revenue: Still feared hitting a wall. PMF is segment-specific (small companies, enterprises, US, Asia, etc.)
- Persona: 'Anytime you raise, your product-market fit is gone. You now have to hit the next bar of PMF.'
- Linear: 'PMF has been so gradual... It's more like we're chipping away at obstacles and getting more and more people interested.'
- Gusto: 'I never felt at any point like, ah, now I have product-market fit.'

Implication: Stop thinking of PMF as a destination and start thinking of it as a continuous process of expanding fit across segments.

### Push vs. Pull PMF Indicator (What it feels like when you've found product-market fit)
A simple diagnostic framework from Segment's co-founder: if you're pushing your product on people, you don't have PMF; if you feel pull, you do

How it works: Before PMF: It always felt like you had to PUSH ideas on other people. You had to nag people to use the product. After PMF: You start feeling PULL for the first time. You solve one problem for people, so they start asking you to solve a second, third, fourth, and fifth. Most early product iteration becomes responding to customer requests rather than convincing people to try things. Diagnostic question: Are you pushing your product onto users, or are users pulling you toward solving more problems?

### Pushing vs. Chasing the Boulder (Emmett Shear PMF Metaphor) (How to know if you've got product-market fit)
A mental model for understanding whether you have PMF: founding a startup is Sisyphean, but the feeling shifts from pushing to chasing the boulder.

How it works: Pushing a boulder up a hill = don't have product-market fit
Chasing a boulder downhill = have product-market fit

Both states are very demanding, but they feel totally different. If you're still pushing, you don't have it yet.

### Reference Customer Discovery Method (Christian Idiodi)
A method for achieving product-market fit by co-developing a solution with a set number of target customers until they become vocal advocates.

How it works: Target 6-8 reference customers for B2B, or 15-25 for B2C. Do not launch until all target customers in the cohort are willing to leave a 5-star review or provide a public testimonial. Use their exact feedback as the marketing copy.

### Retention as the Single PMF Metric (Uri Levine)
Product-market fit is measured by one metric: retention. If users come back, you've created value. If they don't, you haven't.

How it works: For high-frequency products: Look at 3-month retention of 30-40-50% as a good indication. Users typically convert (or don't) by the 3rd or 4th use. If they don't reach a 4th use, the product is 'not good enough.' Compare to a benchmark market if available (Waze compared all markets to Israel). Once PMF is achieved, the product essentially stops changing — every major product today works the same as it did on first use.

### SUSS Framework (Segment, Understand, Solve, Stay focused) (Prioritizing at startups)
A four-step prioritization framework for pre-PMF startups to achieve product-market fit by making 10 customers very happy

How it works: Goal: Make 10 customers VERY happy.

1. SEGMENT: Narrowly and concretely define who you're building for. Use an ICP template. Example: Vanta's first segment was specific company size, stage, and compliance need.

2. UNDERSTAND: Talk to many (ideally 100+) people from this segment. Identify a major pain point that you can solve. Don't settle for nice-to-have problems. Re-listen to power-user calls, hand-write clutch quotes, connect dots between happy users.

3. SOLVE: Put all of your energy into building a product that significantly reduces this very specific pain point. Come up with ideas, test them out, and continue to iterate until your customer can't live without your product. The bigger the pain, and the better you solve it, the bigger your business.

4. STAY FOCUSED: Resist solving adjacent problems, or problems for other segments. Continue to come back to your primary segment and the primary problem you're solving for them. Unless you discover that the segment or problem isn't big enough and intentionally pivot.

### Sales Yield PMF Test (Enterprise) (How to know if you've got product-market fit)
For enterprise/B2B businesses, measuring whether a sales team's contribution margin exceeds its cost as a signal of product-market fit.

How it works: Formula: Sales Yield = Contribution margin of a sales team / Total cost to field the sales team

Benchmark: A sales team may cost ~$500K-$600K. When sales yield exceeds 1.0, that's how you know you've hit PMF.

Interpretation: Sales yield > 1.0 means each sales team is generating more in contribution margin than they cost, indicating the market is receptive enough to sustain efficient growth.

### Sean Ellis PMF Survey (40% Test) (How to know if you've got product-market fit)
A simple survey-based method to quantitatively measure product-market fit by asking users how they would feel if they could no longer use the product.

How it works: Survey question: 'How would you feel if you could no longer use [product]?'
Response options: Very disappointed / Somewhat disappointed / Not disappointed
Threshold: If 40%+ of respondents answer 'very disappointed', you have product-market fit.
Reference: Superhuman used this method systematically to build an engine for finding PMF. Full guide at firstround.com/review/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/

### Sean Ellis Product-Market Fit Score (Jag Duggal)
A survey methodology to quantitatively measure if a product has achieved product-market fit.

How it works: Ask customers: 'How disappointed would you be if this product went away?' Options: Not disappointed, somewhat disappointed, very disappointed. Standard PMF threshold is 40% answering 'very disappointed'. Nubank adjusts this to 50% to account for the cultural politeness and optimism of Brazilian consumers.

Survey asking users 'How disappointed would you be if this product disappeared?' — 40%+ responding 'very disappointed' indicates product-market fit

How it works: Sarah prefers this over NPS. Question: 'How disappointed would you be if this product disappeared?' Threshold: At least 40% of respondents say they'd be very disappointed. Has a dedicated blog post on why she prefers this to NPS.

### Seven Milestones of Increasing PMF Confidence (How to kickstart and scale a consumer business—Step 5: RETAIN: Iterate until enough people stick around)
A progressive series of milestones that build confidence in your business, from first user to sustainable profitability

How it works: Milestone #1: One person wants your product
Milestone #2: Many people want your product
Milestone #3: Many people continue to use your product
Milestone #4: You can acquire new users efficiently
Milestone #5: You make money from people using your product
Milestone #6: You make a profit from people using your product
Milestone #7: You can continue to make a profit while acquiring new users efficiently

Note: Somewhere around milestone #2 you hit what most people refer to as 'product-market fit,' and confidence grows with each subsequent milestone. But milestones can regress (e.g., market tastes change, platform policy changes kill acquisition).

### Superhuman Product Market Fit Engine (Rahul Vohra)
An algorithm for measuring, optimizing, and systematically increasing product market fit using the Sean Ellis survey question and segmented user feedback analysis

How it works: Steps: 1) Ask users 'How would you feel if you could no longer use this product?' with options: Very disappointed, Somewhat disappointed, Not disappointed. 2) Target 40%+ 'very disappointed' (the PMF threshold). 3) Ask 'very disappointed' users what they love most to identify your main benefit. 4) Segment 'somewhat disappointed' users into those for whom main benefit resonates vs doesn't. 5) Politely disregard 'somewhat disappointed' users for whom main benefit doesn't resonate. 6) Ask the remaining segment what they don't like. 7) Build roadmap: 50% doubling down on what people love, 50% overcoming objections from the right 'somewhat disappointed' segment. 8) Re-measure and repeat each planning cycle.

### The Four Ps (Persona, Problem, Promise, Product) (Todd Jackson)
A framework for diagnosing and pivoting when stuck at any PMF level — all four must align for PMF to work, and founders should evaluate which to change

How it works: Persona: the specific buyer/user (collection of people with money to spend). Problem: what's important and urgent to them. Promise: how you describe the value you deliver (positioning). Product: the actual solution. Your product must deliver a promise that solves the problem of your persona. When stuck, evaluate which P to change. Examples: Lattice kept persona, changed problem/promise/product. Vanta changed all four. Plaid kept product, changed other three. Start with persona/problem/promise before rebuilding product.

### The PMF Treadmill (Elena Verna 4.0)
A concept describing the continuous need to recapture Product-Market Fit in the AI era.

How it works: PMF is no longer a multi-year scaling phase. Because underlying LLM capabilities and consumer expectations change every ~3 months, companies must constantly reinvent their solution to maintain PMF.

### Three Dimensions of PMF Measurement (What to ask your users about Product-Market Fit)
A framework for triangulating product-market fit signals across three dimensions: affection, relative utility, and behavior

How it works: Product-market fit can be assessed across three complementary dimensions:

1. **Affection** — Do users have emotional attachment to the product? (Measured via Sean Ellis's disappointment question)
2. **Relative Utility** — Do users find the product more useful than alternatives? (Measured via comparative rating scale)
3. **Behavior** — Are users actually choosing your product when the moment arises? (Measured via open-ended recall of last usage)

Key principle: 'It's hard to imagine a scenario where you'll attain PMF if people don't feel some degree of affection for your product, don't find it useful, and don't use it.'

Use multiple approaches and triangulate between them — there is no single magic question.

### Three Dimensions of Product-Market Fit (Todd Jackson)
The three intertwined dimensions that define PMF — demand, satisfaction, and efficiency — which must be sequenced differently at each level

How it works: Demand: customers want and are looking for your product. Satisfaction: product solves a critical need deeply. Efficiency: product can be delivered repeatably and economically (gross margin, burn multiple, unit economics). Key insight: these trade off against each other. Spending on marketing increases demand but decreases efficiency. Automating too early may harm satisfaction. Sequencing: L1 prioritize satisfaction, L2 add demand, L3 add efficiency, L4 maintain all three while expanding TAM.

### Three Signals of Early Product/Market Fit (This Week #3: Finding product/market fit, interviewing for a Director of PM role, and structuring discussions with senior leaders)
A framework for evaluating PMF confidence during early customer conversations, before you have a product to measure retention or the Superhuman PMF survey

How it works: When talking to potential customers early on, look for these three signals to build confidence you are heading towards PMF:

1. **Passion**: How excited do people get when you describe your idea? Look for: visible excitement, asking how soon it will exist, offering you money for it. If you can't find a handful of people that act this way, either you don't have PMF, you aren't pitching it well, or you haven't talked to the right people. Try to find at least five people who are very excited. Per Paul Graham: 'It's better to have 100 people that love you than a million people that just sort of like you.'

2. **Skin in the game**: Are people willing to pay you for this product? Ask them directly. Even try to get them to pay you now (literally send them an invoice) to get early access. Nothing will be a better signal of interest and PMF. Try this even for a free consumer app — it shows how much value you're creating.

3. **A clear why**: Keep asking 'why.' Why are they excited? What specifically will it do for them? Why is this problem a big deal? Why aren't they using something else? This helps you verify understanding and get to the core of the pain point.

### Three Signals of Product-Market Fit (How long it takes to find Product-Market Fit)
A framework for categorizing how PMF manifests, derived from Lenny's analysis of marketplace founder stories.

How it works: Product-market fit generally manifests in one of three ways:

1. **Clear and sudden inflection in growth** — e.g., Uber seeing obvious virality one year in with almost no effort on growth; Udemy going from $10-20k/course launch to $70k in one day.

2. **Steady and compounding growth/retention** — e.g., Caviar seeing super-users order repeatedly with strong retention; DoorDash seeing retention, order frequency, and spend retention metrics holding at 6 months; Outschool measuring cohort retention.

3. **Hitting an important milestone** — e.g., raising a Series A (Cameo's Lightspeed round at 1.5 years); Rover's organic acquisition taking off and raising Series B; OpenTable surviving a price tripling with minimal churn.

For local marketplaces, a fourth signal applies: successfully replicating PMF in a second city (e.g., Lyft in LA after SF, DoorDash in city 2).

### Three Signs of Product-Market Fit (What it feels like when you've found product-market fit)
A categorization framework for how PMF manifests across companies, organized into three distinct patterns that founders can use to recognize their own PMF signals

How it works: Sign 1: 🔥 Sudden and significant pull from the market — Immediate, unmistakable demand (e.g., Dropbox, Uber, Tinder, Stripe, Robinhood). Sign 2: 🎢 Steady and compounding pull from the market — Gradual realization through consistent growth and compounding wins over months/years (e.g., Substack, Superhuman, Instacart, PagerDuty, Gusto). Sign 3: 🥳 Hitting a meaningful milestone that proves the idea is working — A specific moment or event that crystallizes the realization (e.g., Product Hunt, Airbnb, Amplitude, Canva). Roughly half of iconic companies experienced Sign 1 immediately, while the other half experienced Signs 2 or 3 over extended periods.

### True Product-Market Fit Triangle (What it feels like when you've found product-market fit)
A three-part framework defining what must be true for genuine, sustainable product-market fit — not just initial traction

How it works: Three requirements for True Product-Market Fit: 1. Make a product that people want — Build something that solves a real problem people care about (the focus of this post). 2. Make a profit delivering this product to people at scale — Unit economics must work. Cautionary tales: Cherry (car wash), Luxe (valet), Exec (cleaning) all had demand but couldn't deliver profitably. 3. Find and keep these people, sustainably — Quantitative PMF metrics like retention curves, sales yield, and sustainable acquisition. The most likely killer of your business is #1: not building something people want.

### True Product-Market Fit Trifecta (How to kickstart and scale a consumer business—Step 5: RETAIN: Iterate until enough people stick around)
A framework showing that real PMF requires three types of fit, not just building something people want

How it works: True Product-Market Fit = Three fits combined:
1. Product-market fit: Enough people want your product
2. Product-business model fit: You can make a profit selling your product to these people
3. Product-growth engine fit: You can acquire these people sustainably

Cautionary examples:
- Shyp, Cherry, Exec: Great product-market fit but couldn't find a business model
- Clubhouse, Vine, Yik Yak: Great growth engines but couldn't retain users long-term
- Justin.tv: $8M revenue, $1M profit, 30M monthly viewers but NOT PMF because no repeatable growth engine and couldn't reliably monetize (copyrighted content)

### Wave vs. Riptide Framework for PMF Assessment (Startup to exit: Lessons from a first-time founder)
A framework for evaluating whether your startup has product-market fit, no fit, or is stuck in the dangerous middle ground of steady but unaccelerated sales

How it works: Three zones of early-stage traction:
1. **Wave (Clear PMF)**: Overwhelming pull from the market, rapid word-of-mouth growth, organic acceleration. This is the signal to pour gasoline on sales and product.
2. **Riptide (Dangerous Middle)**: Steady sales with no acceleration. Customers willing to try but not desperate for your solution. Early customers churn before you can build their requested features. You are in 'push-to-market' territory. This is the most dangerous zone because it feels like winning but isn't.
3. **No Sales (Clear Signal)**: No demand at all. Painful but clarifying — forces you to pivot or restart quickly.

If in the Riptide:
- Double down on customer research to understand whether your product/execution is limiting word-of-mouth or if your problem/market assumptions are wrong
- Be maniacally focused on customer behavior as you iterate
- Look for signs of market pull vs. push
- Be honest about whether bad-fit customers are masking lack of real traction

### Zone of Benefit (Oji Udezue)
A mental model for determining if a product improvement is significant enough to drive adoption.

How it works: A product must be at least 3x better (faster, more output) than the status quo for a user to notice the difference and be willing to pay or switch.

## Templates

### PMF Interview Guide (6-12 Users) (What to ask your users about Product-Market Fit)
A three-question qualitative interview template for assessing product-market fit when you have fewer than 100 active users

How it works: **Prerequisites:** 6-12 active users for 1:1 conversations.

**Question 1 — Behavioral workflow exploration (does NOT mention your product):**
"Walk me through the last time you did [X — some essential function of your product]. For each key decision you made along the way, tell me a bit about why you made those decisions."

Purpose: Understand how people typically go about the key task your product is meant to enable, without biasing toward your product.

**Question 2 — Competitive landscape and preference:**
"What are the different products you use to do [X]? What are the best and worst parts of using those products? The last time you did [X] using [this product], why did you use it instead of something else?"

Purpose: See how well-suited your product is to replacing or improving existing workflows.

**Question 3 — Initial motivation and expectation delivery:**
"Why did you try this product in the first place? What were you hoping it would do for you? How well has it delivered on that hope?"

Purpose: Understand motivations and whether the product is meeting expectations.

**What to listen for (two key PMF signals):**
1. **Recognition of explicit, superior value relative to competitors or alternatives** — There should be clear qualitative daylight between how people talk about your product vs. alternatives, and people should have no trouble articulating detailed specifics of why your product is preferable.
2. **Genuine enthusiasm** — If users say they *want* to love your product because it has so much potential, you're only halfway there. Trust your intuition: you'll know PMF when you see it, and you'll know it when you don't.

### PMF Survey (100+ Users) (What to ask your users about Product-Market Fit)
A three-question survey template for assessing product-market fit when you have 100+ active users

How it works: **Prerequisites:** 100+ active users you can easily reach.

**Question 1 — Affection (Sean Ellis question):**
"How would you feel if you could no longer use this product?"
- Very Disappointed
- Somewhat Disappointed
- Not Disappointed

Interpretation: 40% answering 'Very Disappointed' is the critical threshold (per Sean Ellis). This directionally makes sense.

**Question 2 — Relative Utility:**
"Compared to other ways you do [X — some essential function of your product], using this product to do [X] is generally:"
- Much Better
- Somewhat Better
- Neither better nor worse
- Somewhat Worse
- Much Worse

Interpretation: Look for non-trivial skews in a positive direction — even if they're not majorities.

**Question 3 — Behavioral (open-ended):**
"The last time you did [X], what product or tool did you use to do it, and why?"

Interpretation: Look for evidence that people are choosing an alternative to do the job you want your product to do. Example: If early Airbnb guests said they booked their last vacation on VRBO because it offered more choices for non-urban travel, that would signal a problem.

### Questions NOT to Ask When Measuring PMF (What to ask your users about Product-Market Fit)
Two commonly used questions that should be avoided when assessing product-market fit

How it works: **Do NOT ask these questions for PMF assessment:**

1. **NPS (Net Promoter Score) — 'How likely are you to recommend this product to a friend?'**
   - Reason: Both the underlying science and the practical utility of this question are debatable at best. Referred to as part of 'the NPS Industrial Complex.'

2. **'What do you want us to build?' (or any variant asking users what they want so you can blindly build it)**
   - Reason: It's obviously true that people sometimes don't know what they want until they see it. Asking people what they want isn't research. This is why the Henry Ford / Steve Jobs tropes about ignoring research are irrelevant — good research doesn't ask what to build.

### Yellow Flags - Stuck at Level 2 (Todd Jackson)
Warning signs that a startup is stuck at developing product-market fit

How it works: 1. Current customers are happy but you can't open the demand floodgates. 2. You're not getting known for a thing in your space. 3. You've been stuck for 12-18 months. 4. Regretted churn is greater than 20%. 5. Sales cycles are taking too long. 6. Losing deals late in the funnel or to competitors. 7. Not feeling urgency from customers. 8. Struggling to hit target price point. 9. Customers saying 'we don't have the budget' or 'not the right time, let's talk next year' (polite nos). 10. Low ACV combined with slow sales cycle (quadrant of death).

### Yellow Flags - Stuck at Level 3 (Todd Jackson)
Warning signs that a startup is struggling at strong product-market fit

How it works: 1. NRR is below 90% (leaky bucket). 2. Regretted churn greater than 10%. 3. Growth is slowing (e.g., grew 3x prior years but struggling to do 2x). 4. Increasing competition from incumbents and new startups. 5. First scalable channel is getting saturated. 6. Trading off growth vs. efficiency (burn multiple above 3 to maintain growth rate).
