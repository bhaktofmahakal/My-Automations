# Naming and Branding - Frameworks, Templates & Checklists

*29 artifacts extracted from Lenny's Podcast and Newsletter*

## Frameworks

### 5 Dimensions of Brand Personality (Arielle Jackson)
A model based on Jennifer Aaker's research to define a brand's core vibe.

How it works: All brands fit into five dimensions: Sincerity, Excitement, Competence, Sophistication, and Ruggedness. Strong brands spike in exactly two of these five.

### Brand Trust Signal Framework (Zoelle Egner)
Every customer touchpoint (word of mouth, ads, landing pages, signup flows, customer service) either adds or removes trust; early-stage companies should audit each touchpoint for signals that say 'we understand you specifically' and 'we operate at a higher level than our size suggests'

How it works: Two key signals to provide: 1) 'We understand you specifically, and our solution is designed for a person like you' (e.g., industry-specific sample content, targeted jokes/references). 2) 'We operate at a higher level than you'd expect for our size/stage' (e.g., polished emails, decent illustrations, remnant billboards). Audit: landing pages, emails, sample content, customer service interactions, public communications.

### Brand as a Promise (Not a Logo) (Seth Godin)
A framework for evaluating brand strength based on whether it represents a kept promise that generates true loyalty.

How it works: Brand test questions: 1. Would people miss you if you were gone? 2. Could you extend into adjacent categories and people would know what to expect? (Nike hotel test vs. Hyatt sneakers test) 3. Would customers pay extra to stay with you? (loyalty vs. bribery—if they only stay for points, you have no brand) 4. Are you making a difficult, remarkable promise? 5. Are you keeping that promise consistently?

### Lexicon 3-Step Naming Process (David Placek)
A three-phase approach to creating brand names combining creativity and discipline.

How it works: 1. Identify: Define behavior, experience, and landscape to build a creative framework. 2. Invent: Use small teams of 2, misdirected briefs to free creativity, and linguistic sound symbolism. 3. Implement: Create prototypes, mockups, and conduct customer research.

### Linguistic Sound Symbolism (David Placek)
Using the inherent sounds and vibrations of letters to evoke specific feelings for a brand.

How it works: 'V' is the most alive/vibrant sound (e.g., Vercel, Corvette). 'B' is one of the most reliable sounds (e.g., Blackberry). 'Z' is a noisy letter that creates a strong signal (e.g., Azure). 'X' is fast, crisp, and semantic for innovation.

### Naming Brainstorm Process (Arielle Jackson)
A structured 1-hour meeting format to generate hundreds of name ideas.

How it works: Involves 5-7 people (including disinterested linguists/writers). Part 1: Take meaningful words from positioning statement and run synonyms, antonyms, free associations, and translations. Part 2: Thematic brainstorm using 7-10 'Jeopardy-style' themes related to the product space.

### Prefix-Suffix Combination Matrix Method (How to find the perfect name)
A systematic method for generating coined brand names by combining linguistic components in a matrix

How it works: Process:
1. Define desired brand attributes from Diamond Framework (e.g., speed, accuracy, flow, reliability, power)
2. Build component categories:
   a. Prefixes communicating key attributes
   b. Suffixes communicating key attributes
   c. Word parts related to domain-specific concepts (e.g., talent/recruiting for a tech company)
   d. Root words from Greek, Latin, and Sanskrit conveying desired meanings
   e. Sound-symbolic letters identified via analysis (e.g., 'fast-sounding' letters)
3. Combine prefixes × suffixes in a matrix (Vercel example: 55 prefixes × 102 suffixes)
4. Review output for interesting ideas (~60 from Vercel's first pass)
5. Repeat the entire process at least 3 times over 2+ weeks
6. The best candidates 'rise to the top' through repeated iteration

Note: First-pass outputs are described as 'ideas, not solutions; not yet' — iteration is essential.

### Six Challenges of Modern Brand Naming (How to find the perfect name)
Six key challenges that make naming harder in the current market environment

How it works: 1. UNPRECEDENTED COMPETITION: More players, more noise, more choice.
2. TRADEMARK CLUTTER: Congestion across all 45 trademark classes. In the U.S., there are over 5,500,000 registered trademarks.
3. ATTENTION SCARCITY: Audiences are distracted and overwhelmed.
4. GLOBAL DIGITAL COMMERCE: Names must work across languages, cultures, and platforms simultaneously.
5. INSTITUTIONAL SKEPTICISM: People have learned not to believe hype.
6. INFORMATION OVERLOAD: A signal must be stronger to stand out.

Additional startup challenge: Your name must attract both investors AND early adopters.

### The 'New Competitor' Feedback Test (David Placek)
A method for getting unbiased feedback on a potential name from friends or colleagues.

How it works: Instead of asking 'What do you think of this name?', tell them 'A new competitor just launched and their name is [Name]. What do you think about that?' This reveals what the name makes them imagine and how it positions the brand in their mind.

### The Diamond Framework (How to find the perfect name)
A four-question strategic alignment framework used at the start of any naming project to force clarity on business, marketing, and naming strategy

How it works: Four questions arranged in a diamond shape:

1. WHAT DOES WINNING LOOK LIKE? (Top of diamond) — Define the ultimate success outcome for the brand.
2. WHAT DO WE HAVE TO WIN? (Left of diamond) — Identify existing assets, strengths, and advantages.
3. WHAT DO WE NEED TO WIN? (Right of diamond) — Identify gaps, requirements, and what must be true.
4. WHAT DO WE NEED TO SAY? (Bottom of diamond) — Define the core message the name must communicate.

This creates alignment on the role of the name versus other brand elements like design and messaging.

Swiffer Example:
- What does winning look like? → 'Be the leading quick-clean brand in the U.S. within three years.'
- What do we have to win? → 'Revolutionary cleaning technology, P&G distribution, consumer dissatisfaction with mopping.'
- What do we need to win? → 'A name that reframes mopping as fun and effortless, not a chore.'
- What do we need to say? → 'This is not a mop. This is something new, fast, and enjoyable.'

Vercel Example:
- Winning defined as being 'the most innovative and fastest-moving company in the category.'
- Based on that definition, they agreed a coined, inventive name was the right direction.

### The Three Pillars of Effective Naming (How to find the perfect name)
A three-dimensional framework for evaluating and creating brand names that are memorable, differentiated, and trusted

How it works: Three pillars:

1. BUILD FOR TRUST: The right name inspires both trust and imagination. Example: Microsoft Azure — familiar as a word, generates interest, the 'z' sound creates a strong signal, 'zure' (like 'sure') creates a smooth experience. Avoid straightforward descriptive names like 'Microsoft Cloud Services' which carry no news value.

2. COMMUNICATE AN ORIGINAL IDEA. NEVER DESCRIBE: Descriptive names are boring and one-dimensional (e.g., Infoseek, TripActions). Original names signal a new idea (e.g., Google, Navan). Test: customers should say 'They're not like the other guys.' Descriptive names also limit growth into new categories.

3. BE ACCESSIBLE: The human brain doesn't like complexity or remember it well. Make sure your name is easy to process and contains familiar components. Example: Subaru's 'Outback' uses a simple, familiar geographic term.

## Templates

### Naming Brief (Arielle Jackson)
A document to align stakeholders before brainstorming names.

How it works: Includes: What are you naming (company/product/both)? What do you want the name to communicate? What do you want to avoid? What are the names of competitors? Are there other considerations (e.g., pronounceable in other languages)?

### Open-Source Brand Kit (Brand.gamma.app) (Grant Lee)
A public repository of a company's brand DNA given to influencers to reduce friction in content creation.

How it works: Includes voice and tone guidelines, art direction, and specific AI prompts (e.g., Midjourney prompts) so creators can easily replicate the brand's style without having to invent it from scratch.

### Tension-Driven Personality Attributes (Arielle Jackson)
A format for defining brand personality traits that prevents generic, boring descriptions.

How it works: Create 5 statements using the format: 'We are [X], but not [Y]', where Y is taking X too far. Example: 'We are playful, but not silly' or 'We are daring, but not stupid'.

### The Naming Strategy Diamond (David Placek)
A brainstorming exercise to define the company's positioning and experience before generating names.

How it works: Draw a diamond. Top point: 'Win' (How do you define winning?). Right point: 'What do you have to win?' Bottom point: 'What do you need to win?' Left point: 'What do you have to say to win?' This focuses the team on behavior and experience rather than descriptive words.

## Checklists

### 7 Criteria for Names (Arielle Jackson)
A checklist to evaluate and filter a shortlist of brainstormed names using a red/yellow/green scoring system.

How it works: 1. Trademark availability. 2. Domain availability. 3. Distinctiveness (memorable, doesn't sound like competitors). 4. Timelessness (avoids naming trends). 5. Reflective of key messaging/emotion. 6. Sound and ease of pronunciation/spelling. 7. Appearance (visual symmetry, length).

### Lexicon Naming Process Steps (How to find the perfect name)
The step-by-step process Lexicon uses to go from brief to final brand name, synthesized from the newsletter's various case studies

How it works: Step 1: SET HIGH STANDARDS, NOT NARROW OBJECTIVES — Aim for fresh, energetic ideas, not just basic objectives like 'support the product's positioning' or 'keep it under seven letters.'

Step 2: RUN THE DIAMOND FRAMEWORK — Answer: What does winning look like? What do we have to win? What do we need to win? What do we need to say? This creates alignment on the role of the name vs. other brand elements.

Step 3: CONDUCT QUALITATIVE RESEARCH — Interview 25+ people about the category experience. Look for emotional insights, not just functional needs. (Swiffer: discovered no one liked mopping; one person said 'It's just moving dirt and grease from one place to another.')

Step 4: DEFINE CREATIVE DIRECTION — Based on research, establish a clear creative brief. (Pentium: 'Develop a name that sounds and looks like an element inside the computer that is fundamental to performance.')

Step 5: IMAGINE THE SOUND FIRST — Before generating names, decide what your brand should sound like. Should it sound fast? Full? Reliable? Use sound-symbolism principles.

Step 6: BUILD NAMING COMPONENT MATRICES — Create matrices of prefixes, suffixes, root words from Greek/Latin/Sanskrit, and sound-symbolic letters that map to desired attributes. (Vercel: 55 prefixes × 102 suffixes)

Step 7: GENERATE 1,000+ IDEAS MINIMUM — Use small teams exploring different creative territories (not one big brainstorm). Explore places where you think there's no chance of finding the name. Repeat exploration cycles multiple times over weeks. (Vercel: repeated combination steps 3 times over 2 weeks.)

Step 8: SHORTLIST 50-100 CANDIDATES — Narrow from 1,000+ to a working shortlist.

Step 9: RESEARCH WITH CONSUMERS AND LINGUISTS — Test shortlisted names with target customers and linguistic experts. Look for the signal: 'They're not like the other guys.' (Swiffer: selected over 80% of the time as the brand people would most want to try.)

Step 10: SELECT AND VALIDATE — Confirm trademark availability. Ensure the name works across languages and cultures for global brands.

### Naming Process Anti-Patterns (What Not to Do) (How to find the perfect name)
Four common mistakes to avoid when naming a product or company, based on 40 years of naming experience

How it works: 1. DON'T THINK YOU'LL 'KNOW IT WHEN YOU SEE IT.' — You won't. You need a structured evaluation process.
2. DON'T LISTEN TO ANYONE WHO DISMISSES BOLD IDEAS AS 'OFF-BRAND.' — Bold often becomes the brand. One Microsoft executive called Azure 'a dumb idea.'
3. DON'T RELY ON LARGE BRAINSTORMING SESSIONS — Sessions with 4+ participants get bogged down by everyone's need to be right and peer pressure to agree on a popular solution. They rarely work. Also avoid distant freelancers. Use small teams with real esprit de corps and grit.
4. DON'T PLAY IT SAFE. — Most approaches favor comfort over competitive edge, producing forgettable names that disappear. There is no power in comfort.

## Examples

### CommandBar AI Branding Experiment (Counterintuitive advice for building AI products)
Evidence that branding chatbot products as 'AI-powered' increases both usage and quality of interactions

How it works: Finding: Branding AI-powered products as 'AI-powered' does more than increase initial engagement—it helps users understand what the feature is capable of and how they should interact with it.
Application: CommandBar encourages customers to brand their chatbot as AI-powered in some way.
Result: When customers brand as AI-powered, more users use it AND they use it better (higher quality interactions).
Key insight: Contrary to the software truism that users don't care how something is built, the 'AI' label actually functions as a UX signal.

### Dasani Sound Symbolism Breakdown (How to find the perfect name)
Detailed phonetic analysis of how each sound in Dasani evokes specific brand attributes

How it works: Brand: Dasani (Coca-Cola water brand)
Type: Invented name

Phonetic breakdown:
- 'Da' sound → delivers a crisp, clean experience
- 'San' → Latin word for health embedded in the middle
- 'i' ending → feels light and slim

Overall effect: The name sounds like water should feel — clean, healthy, and light.

### Jaguar Rebrand Critique (Seth Godin)
Analysis of why Jaguar's rebrand was actually just a re-logo-ing that abandoned valuable brand assets.

How it works: Jaguar had one of the most iconic, beautiful, beloved car brands with almost no cars on the road to undermine future positioning—perfect starting position for an EV transition. Instead of leveraging this, they pleased an art director with a new logo that undermined decades of awareness. Better approach: Find 50 people with authority, get them in a transformative car. Buzz (IHOP's burger name change, Oreo's Super Bowl tweet) does not sell products. Customer traction does.

### Jeni's Ice Cream Logo Refresh (Jessica Hische)
A case study of a utilitarian logo fix to solve legibility and layout issues.

How it works: Jeni's Ice Cream had a long 'J' that created an unusable pocket of white space underneath it. Additionally, the apostrophe was placed over the 'I' in a way that, combined with a loop at the top of the 'J', caused the logo to occasionally be misread as 'penis'. The refresh focused on fixing these specific utilitarian and legibility issues.

### Lenny's Podcast Logo Refresh (Jessica Hische)
A case study of evolving a beloved brand without alienating the existing audience.

How it works: The goal was to refine the 'cut-paper' wonky vibe into something cleaner that scaled well, especially for small podcast avatars. Used the 'Degular' typeface to maintain the funky edge. Ensured the typography and the illustration (campfire and marshmallows) felt like they were drawn by the same hand. Ultimately removed the microphone icon because it cluttered the design and wasn't necessary.

### Navan Rebrand Revenue Impact (How to find the perfect name)
Quantified business impact of renaming TripActions to Navan

How it works: BEFORE: TripActions — descriptive name that stopped working when the company expanded from travel into expense management and financial services. Had 'a thousand similar names swirling around in the competitive landscape.'

AFTER: Navan — a palindrome that links to travel ('navigation') but offers originality and flexibility to grow into new industries.

BUSINESS RESULT: Since the rebrand in 2023, Navan revenues grew from $150 million to over $500 million in 2025.

### Pentium Naming Case Study (How to find the perfect name)
How Intel differentiated from AMD's identical 486 processor by creating a coined name that sounds like a scientific element

How it works: PROBLEM: Intel's 486 processor outperformed AMD's 486, but consumers perceived them as identical due to the model number naming convention. Intel needed to differentiate.

FIRST CRITERION: Develop a name that would differentiate Intel's processor from all others.

STRATEGIC INSIGHT: The new name needed to capitalize on the success of the 'Intel Inside' campaign.

INTERNAL FAVORITE: 'Prochip' — rejected.

CREATIVE DIRECTION (from research): 'Develop a name that sounds and looks like an element inside the computer that is fundamental to performance.'

SOLUTION: 'Pentium' — uses the '-ium' suffix for fluency (like sodium, uranium), sounding like a science-based element. Familiar-seeming but unexpected for the category.

KEY INSIGHT: Invented names take LESS money to build into brands than existing words (contrary to common myth). They signal 'new and innovative' better and generate more attention because they are unexpected.

RESULT: Research in 6 markets validated the name. Within months, consumers were asking for Pentium computers by name — to the frustration of Dell, HP, and others.

### Sonos Naming Case Study (David Placek)
How Lexicon convinced the Sonos founders to adopt a non-descriptive, palindromic name.

How it works: Founders wanted an 'entertainment' name. Lexicon pitched 'Sonos' (sound, palindrome, noisy 'S' letters). It was initially rejected for lacking emotion. Placek flew back to argue for it, convincing them to name it for the marketplace, not for themselves.

### Swiffer Naming Case Study (How to find the perfect name)
How P&G's new cleaning product was named by reframing mopping as fun rather than describing the product functionally

How it works: PROBLEM: P&G invented a 'new mop' but it wasn't really a mop. Needed a name for a new category.

RESEARCH: Interviewed 25 people about cleaning routines. Key findings: Some enjoyed washing windows, others polishing furniture. NO ONE liked mopping. Quote: 'It's just moving dirt and grease from one place to another.' Quote: 'Mopping is just not fun.'

REFRAME: Reframed the problem around FUN. Eliminated descriptive names like 'Promop' or 'EffiClean.'

EXPLORATION: One small team explored toys, exotic vacations, and easy-to-use tools. Linguists focused on the actions and sounds of cleaning — sweeping, wiping, washing.

SOLUTION: When the teams merged their work, 'Swiffer' sat in the middle — combining the sounds of swift action with a playful, invented feel.

PRINCIPLE: 'The best names don't describe reality; they change it.' Swiffer made mopping feel like a quick, joyful action.

RESULT: In research with Lexicon's linguistic network and consumers, Swiffer was selected over 80% of the time as the new brand people would be most interested to try.

### Vercel Naming Case Study (How to find the perfect name)
How Zeit was renamed to Vercel using systematic prefix/suffix combination and sound symbolism

How it works: PROBLEM: Company called Zeit needed a new name. Team and technology were impressive.

DIAMOND FRAMEWORK RESULT: Winning = 'being the most innovative and fastest-moving company in the category.' Decision: a coined, inventive name was the right direction.

COMPONENT MATRICES BUILT:
- Prefixes and suffixes communicating accuracy, speed, and flow
- Word parts speaking to the company's talent and recruiting
- Greek, Latin, and Sanskrit words meaning reliability and power
- Fast-sounding letters identified using proprietary sound-symbolism software

COMBINATORIAL PROCESS: Combined 55 prefixes × 102 suffixes → ~60 interesting ideas (described as 'ideas, not solutions; not yet'). Repeated the steps 3 times over 2 weeks.

RESULT: 'Vercel' rose to the top — a sound that is 'alive and daring,' six letters, familiar parts assembled in a unique way.

### WP Engine Trademark Dispute Case Study (Matt Mullenweg)
A real-world example of an open-source creator taking aggressive action against a private equity-backed company for trademark infringement and brand dilution.

How it works: WP Engine (owned by Silver Lake) used the WordPress trademark confusingly, stopped contributing to core, and disabled core features (like revisions) to save money. Matt responded by publicly calling them out at WordCamp, blocking their access to WordPress.org, and forking their plugin (Advanced Custom Fields) to patch security vulnerabilities.
