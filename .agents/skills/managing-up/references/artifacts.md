# Managing Up for Leaders - Frameworks, Templates & Checklists

*34 artifacts extracted from Lenny's Podcast and Newsletter*

## Frameworks

### Alignment-Confidence Matrix (Managing Up) (Ravi Mehta)
A framework for understanding your relationship currency with your manager based on alignment with their direction and their confidence in you

How it works: Two axes: Alignment with manager × Manager's confidence in you. Key quadrants: High alignment + High confidence = Full support. Low alignment + High confidence = Permission (you have earned the right to take a different path). Low alignment + Low confidence = Recipe for failure (must either align or build confidence first). Confidence is the currency that buys permission to diverge.

### CEO Trust-Building Progression for First PMs (Joining as the first product manager)
A progression model for gradually taking over product ownership from the CEO

How it works: Phase 1 (First weeks): Observe and learn, don't expect autonomy
Phase 2 (Month 1): Run jam sessions with founders — frequent chats/debates about product vision, strategy, competition. These give a window into each other's thought processes, principles, and values.
Phase 3 (~1 month in): Pitch back the role and define it clearly. Help the CEO learn the new lean process you put in place.
Phase 4 (Ongoing): Increasingly take the product reins. Communicate constantly. Don't be afraid to stand your ground if the CEO breaks process.
Goal: Within first 90 days, CEO should feel comfortable with you and be increasingly handing over product control.

### Five Tactics for Managing Up (Managing up)
Lenny's five-part framework for effectively managing up with your manager at any level

How it works: 1. **Over-communicate**: Regularly share what you're doing, what you plan to do, and what you've done. Send a weekly 'State of The Me' email with current priorities, things on your mind, and blockers.

2. **Set realistic expectations**: When taking on work, set realistic expectations around time, resources needed, and quality. Performance is judged on results relative to expectations. Be ambitious but realistic.

3. **Be proactive**: Don't wait for your manager to come to you. Anticipate looming asks, deliverables, and problems. Examples: get started on strategy before yearly planning, gather board meeting data early, develop plans for emerging problems/opportunities.

4. **Prioritize + communicate**: When asked to take on new work (especially last-minute), prioritize it among existing work, share updated priorities with your manager, surface trade-offs (what project suffers, who will be upset), make a recommendation, and ask if they agree.

5. **Do great work**: Better work → more trust → more freedom and influence. If you feel micro-managed and others don't, it's likely a performance issue.

### Five Types of Help Menu (On asking for help (even when you really don’t want to))
A categorization of five types of help you can ask for, each with a metaphor, to clarify what kind of support you actually need.

How it works: 1. **Perspective gathering** (metaphor: 'a simulator of someone else's brain'): Advice, expertise, opinion, alternative viewpoints.
2. **Information/knowledge** (metaphor: 'a database download'): Analysis, data, insider knowledge, facts, domain know-how.
3. **Task or goal progression** (metaphor: 'the conveyor belt moving forward'): Thought partnership, rubber-ducking, taking on a task or reducing the load, giving feedback on work, reducing risk or overhead, helping to clarify/focus/prioritize.
4. **Empathetic support** (metaphor: 'a ship to help you navigate the stormy seas'): Being a shoulder to lean on, providing supportive challenge, helping you access your own strengths or resourcefulness, being a mirror and reflecting back, mitigating interpersonal conflict.
5. **Advocacy** (metaphor: 'a propeller or catalyst'): Opening doors, championing you, holding you accountable, celebrating your wins, believing in you, providing new opportunities.

### Meat and Potatoes Before Soufflé Trust Framework (How to ship like a startup)
A framework for earning leadership trust to pursue your own ambitious product ideas

How it works: The way to get leadership to fund your idea (your soufflé) is by first executing on their ideas (the meat and potatoes).

Four requirements to earn trust:
1. You need reps → Ship things.
2. You need wins → Ship *good* things.
3. You need a line of communication → Leaders need to *know* you.
4. You need excellent communication → Leaders need to *respect* you.

Once you check these boxes, the world is your oyster.

Example: Mihika launched FigJam's earliest features (Sections, Music, Voting) as her meat and potatoes, which paved the way for pitching and building Figma Slides (her soufflé).

### Performance-Trust-Freedom Flywheel (Managing up)
A simple causal model for how doing great work leads to autonomy and influence

How it works: The better you do → the more your manager will trust you → the more freedom and influence you will have.

Corollary: If you feel micro-managed, and others around you don't, it's likely because you aren't performing well. Do great work, and life will get better.

### Prioritize and Communicate Technique (Using your working time well - Issue 22)
A two-step technique for responding to new work requests from managers or stakeholders without overcommitting

How it works: When you are asked to take on more work (and you agree it's a good idea):

Step 1 — PRIORITIZE: Take the new ask and prioritize it amongst the work you already have on your plate.

Step 2 — COMMUNICATE: Tell your manager where you prioritized this ask relative to your existing work.

Script: 'Great idea [Manager Name]! I've added this to my priority list, right after X, Y, Z. Let me know if you disagree with that prioritization.'

Outcomes:
- If your manager is onboard → you're done
- If not → take their feedback and go through the cycle again
- Either way → you've avoided adding more immediate work, you're now on the same page about priorities, and you've set clear expectations

Key insight: The two wrong default answers to new asks are (1) 'Yes!' and (2) 'No!' — neither serves you well.

### Say-Do-Say Operating Principle (Peter Deng)
A three-part communication cadence for managing up and general alignment: say you're going to do the thing, say you're doing the thing, say you did the thing.

How it works: Step 1: Say you're going to do the thing — calibrate with your manager/stakeholders on what you'll do. Craft the words carefully. This creates an opportunity to redirect before work begins. Step 2: Say you're doing the thing — reaffirm in one-on-ones or team all-hands. Invites conversation if priorities have changed. Step 3: Say you did the thing — close the loop. Especially important for introverts who might otherwise get lost in organizations. Origin attributed to Jill at Uber who said 'Repetition doesn't spoil the prayer.'

### The Eye of Sauron (Boz)
A mental model for understanding executive attention and project prioritization.

How it works: When the CEO/exec determines a project is the most important thing, no detail is too small for them to notice. It's a high-visibility, high-stress place to be, but invaluable for career growth.

### The Magic Loop (The Magic Loop)
A five-step repeatable framework for systematically growing your career within a company by building a collaborative relationship with your manager

How it works: Five core steps:
1. Do your current job well — Verify with your manager that your performance is solid before seeking more. Ask in a non-threatening way during a quiet time.
2. Ask your manager how you can help them — Managers always need help. Volunteer when tasks come up in meetings. If they don't have something immediately, offer to check back in a week.
3. Do what they ask — Even if it's not glamorous work (maintenance, operational tasks). Meet the quality bar. If it's beyond your skills, be honest and ask for support.
4. Ask your manager how you can help in a way that also grows your skills toward a particular goal — Leverage reciprocity. For simple goals: 'Is there a way I could help you out while working with [skill/technology]?' For complex goals like promotion: 'One day I'd like to be promoted. Could I help out in a way that you think would develop me toward that goal?'
5. Repeat the loop — Return to step 4 for new growth-aligned work, or step 1 to re-verify performance with new responsibilities.

The loop evolves through three maturity levels:
- Level 1 (Ask): Ask your manager what they need help with
- Level 2 (Suggest): Propose your own ideas for what needs to be done, confirming with manager
- Level 3 (Just Do It): See what's needed, do it autonomously, and report back

The end goal is a partnership rather than a boss/employee relationship.

### Three Levels of Contribution Maturity (The Magic Loop)
A progression model showing how to evolve from reactive helper to autonomous leader

How it works: Level 1 — Ask: Ask your manager what they need help with. Best for: building initial trust, early in the loop.
Level 2 — Suggest: Propose your own ideas based on observed problems. Manager sees you as helpful, innovative, and proactive. Best for: when you understand your group and company well.
Level 3 — Just Do It: See what's needed, do it autonomously, mention it in your next discussion. Manager sees you as helpful, innovative, and autonomous. Best for: when trust is high and you know the manager's style well.

Optimal approach: The strongest performers blend all three styles. Occasionally ask (manager may have needs based on info outside your purview), get input on biggest suggestions (manager feels part of results), and independently handle things you're confident about (fastest execution).

Success indicator: Your relationship with your manager becomes less boss/employee and more of a partnership.

### Three-Step Process for Saying No (Saying no)
A structured approach for responding to manager ideas: Listen → Form your POV → Respond constructively

How it works: Step 1: Listen, ask questions — Avoid reacting with a quick yes or no. Force yourself to ask at least three questions. Use the Socratic method. Try to turn verbal conversations into written ones.

Step 2: Form your own point of view — Take your team's feelings out of the equation. Consider perishability, downside/upside, strategic alignment, and whether the business would be better off. Place the idea on the spectrum: (1) Amazing, do ASAP, (2) Great, but not now, (3) Interesting, explore further, (4) Bad idea, but a variation could work, (5) Just a bad idea.

Step 3: Respond constructively — Use one of five response paths depending on your assessment.

### U-Curve of Founder Involvement (Noah Weiss)
A mental model for when to involve a product-minded founder in a project: high involvement at the beginning (strategy/vision) and high involvement at the end (craft/polish), with low involvement in the middle (exploration).

How it works: X-axis is time, Y-axis is level of involvement. Step 1: Strategic buy-in, goals, anti-goals. Step 2: Team runs and explores independently. Step 3: Founder tastes the soup, collective bug bash to raise the craft bar.

### What Managers Want from Direct Reports (Empathy Flip) (Saying no)
Lenny's five-point empathy framework for how managers want reports to handle their ideas

How it works: As a manager, I'd want my report to:
1. Truly listen
2. Assume I know what I'm talking about
3. Come at it with an attitude of 'How could we do this, if we wanted to?' vs. 'Here are all the reasons we can't'
4. Be direct and honest about where they see gaps in my thinking
5. Be open-minded to big, radical, scary ideas

Basically, treat me like an adult.

## Templates

### Delegation Request Script (The Magic Loop)
A script for requesting to hand off low-value work to create capacity for higher-value growth work

How it works: Template structure:
1. Cite your contributions: 'I've really enjoyed taking on [list of things you've taken on]'
2. Express willingness for more: 'I'd like to do more, but I am at capacity.'
3. Identify low-value work to hand off: '[Specific low-value task] takes a lot of my time and is something anyone can do.'
4. Connect to manager's need: 'You told me you need help with [manager's priority] and I think I could do what you need there.'
5. Suggest the swap: 'Is there someone else who could do [low-value task] to free up my time?'

Example: 'I've really enjoyed taking on vacation scheduling, project reporting, and ownership of the Acme account. I'd like to do more, but I am at capacity. The daily run to the supply office takes a lot of my time and is something anyone can do. You told me you need help with the ABC project and I think I could do what you need there, but is there someone else who could do the supply office run to free up my time?'

Strategic note: Think of your career like a card game—discard a 3 and add a jack to your hand. Let the manager do the shifting of work so coworkers direct any issues to them, not you.

### Manager Communication Template (Autonomy Template) (Becoming a senior Product Manager)
A 3-step template for sharing challenges and decisions with your manager that demonstrates capability, earns trust, and opens the door for feedback without sounding self-promotional or helpless.

How it works: Use this template when sharing progress or asking your manager for advice:

1. 'Hi manager, let me tell you about a challenge I ran into ______'
2. 'Here's how I'm thinking of handling it (or did handle it) ______'
3. 'Any thoughts?'

This format lets you:
- Show off the challenge you faced and how you handled it
- Make an opening for feedback
- If your solution was good, your manager can say 'Great work!'
- If there's room for improvement, proactively asking demonstrates you can be trusted to ask for input when needed
- Avoids sounding too self-promotional
- Avoids making your manager think you're stuck
- Avoids frustrating your manager that you're not asking for advice

### Opening Script for Receiving a Manager's Idea (Saying no)
A conversational opener to signal open-mindedness before diving into questions

How it works: Say: 'Interesting! Let me try to better understand what you're thinking here…'

Alternative openers: 'I'm into it' or 'Interesting!' or 'I'm intrigued.'

Then pivot to questions. The goal is to make your manager feel that you're genuinely open to the idea.

### Performance Check-In Script (The Magic Loop)
A script for asking your manager how you're doing in a non-threatening way, used as part of Step 1 of the Magic Loop

How it works: Timing: Wait until a relatively quiet time when there is no crisis (or less of one than usual).
Setup: Ask your manager if you can talk for a few minutes. Sit down (non-threatening posture).
Script: 'It's been a while since I last checked in with you. I want to do a good job here and be helpful. I was wondering if you could tell me how you feel I am doing.'
After receiving feedback: Say thank you. Do not argue. At most, ask for an example if you receive a complaint.
Next step: If feedback is basically good, proceed to Step 2 of the Magic Loop. If not, address the feedback and re-verify before moving on.

### Proactive Idea Suggestion Script (The Magic Loop)
A script template for suggesting your own ideas to your manager while preserving their authority to redirect

How it works: Template: 'I noticed that [specific problem you've observed]. I've been thinking that I could [your proposed solution]. Would that be helpful, or is there something else you would like me to do?'
Example: 'I noticed that we are often late on projects because we forget to account for vacation schedules and then someone critical is out. I've been thinking that I could implement a step in our planning and update process that captures these. Would that be helpful, or is there something else you would like me to do?'
Key: The ending question both asks if this would be helpful AND implies the offer to do something else the manager prefers. This gives managers choice while removing the burden of generating ideas.

### State of The Me — Weekly Manager Update Email (Managing up)
A simple weekly email template sent to your manager to over-communicate your status, keep them informed, and surface blockers. Lenny recommends trying it for two weeks.

How it works: Weekly email to your manager with three sections:

**Subject: State of The Me — [Your Name] — Week of [Date]**

**1. Current Priorities**
- List your top priorities for the current week/period
- Optionally highlight material changes from the prior week (added, removed, or re-ordered priorities)

**2. Things on My Mind**
- Topics, ideas, concerns, or observations you're thinking about
- Could include strategic questions, team dynamics, product direction, etc.

**3. Blockers I Need Help With**
- Specific items where you're stuck and need your manager's help
- Decisions that need to be made, resources needed, escalations required

Tip from Nishi Patel: Highlight material changes to your priorities from the prior week to make it even easier for your manager to track what shifted.

## Checklists

### 5 Tactics for Managing Up (This Week #0: Advice on growth, product, and leadership)
A checklist of five behaviors and tactics for effectively managing your manager

How it works: 1. Over-communicate: Consistently share what you plan to do, what you're doing, and what you did. Send a weekly 'State of Me' email to your boss with (1) things they can help me with, (2) my current priorities, and (3) things on my mind.
2. Set expectations: When taking on work, set realistic expectations around time, resources needed, and quality. Your performance will be judged based on how it compared to your manager's expectations. Be ambitious, but also be realistic.
3. Share the trade-offs: When asked to make changes to a plan, be transparent about the trade-offs. What will suffer? What will benefit? What do you recommend? Partner with your manager to make the best decision.
4. Prioritize and communicate: When asked to take on additional work, particularly last minute, prioritize it amongst your existing priorities and share your updated priorities with your manager. Ask them if they disagree — adjust if so.
5. Do great work: The better you do → the more your manager will trust you → the more freedom and influence you will have. If you feel micro-managed, and others around you don't, it's likely because you aren't performing well.

### Questions to Ask When First Hearing a Manager's Idea (Saying no)
Five questions to ask before reacting to understand the manager's thinking and demonstrate open-mindedness

How it works: 1. Can you help me visualize what it'll look like in the product, roughly?
2. How high of a priority is this for you, vs. other work we're doing? Do you think we hop on this now or queue it up for our next planning cycle?
3. How perishable do you think this opportunity is—if we tackle it next year, would the opportunity still be there?
4. What gets you most excited about this idea?
5. How do you see this fitting into our broader strategy—or is this a new direction you think we should take?

### Re-prioritization Communication Checklist (Managing up)
Steps to follow when you receive a new work request to transparently re-prioritize and get manager alignment

How it works: When asked to take on new work (particularly last-minute):
1. Prioritize the new work amongst your existing priorities
2. Identify what project will suffer if you take on this new work
3. Identify who will be upset by the trade-off
4. Develop your own recommendation for how to re-prioritize
5. Share your updated set of priorities with your manager
6. Ask your manager if they agree with how you're re-prioritizing
7. If they don't agree, partner with them to update your priorities
8. If they agree, proceed with the updated plan

### Schedule Syncing (Managing Up) (Nir Eyal)
A process to align with your manager on priorities using your timeboxed calendar.

How it works: 1. Ask for 10 minutes with your boss. 2. Show them your timeboxed calendar for the week. 3. Show them a separate list of tasks they asked for that didn't fit. 4. Ask: 'Help me prioritize—what should I swap out to fit these in?'

## Examples

### Amazon Screws / Bezos '?' Email — Steelmanning Example (How to communicate tradeoffs so leaders will listen)
A PM at Amazon received a '?' email from Jeff Bezos about why screws were hard to find. Instead of dismissing it, the PM steelmanned the request and discovered a systemic problem.

How it works: Jeff Bezos forwarded a customer complaint about difficulty finding screws on Amazon with just a '?' to a PM. Instead of explaining why screws were low-priority, the PM investigated deeply for days — digging into data and talking to customers. Discovery: The issue wasn't just screws. Anything on Amazon that was unbranded and 'specification-driven' rather than 'brand-driven' was very hard to find. Bezos's instincts were right due to expertise or well-informed hunch the PM didn't have. Fixing this class of issue required meaningful engineering and product work and likely derailed the existing roadmap, but it was worth it. Lesson: Steelmanning seemingly trivial requests can reveal major systemic problems.

### Lenny's 'Let It Blow Up' Doomed Project Story (Communicating bad news - Issue 26)
A personal anecdote about taking on a project Lenny knew was doomed to fail, being unable to convince his manager, and ultimately learning to let the experiment run its course

How it works: Situation: Lenny took on a project he knew was doomed to fail. He tried to convince his manager but couldn't. They launched it anyway. Lenny worked long hours and got stressed trying to make it succeed. A colleague's advice over a beer: 'Let it blow up. Let it fail.' He let the experiment run its course — it was negative on every metric. Once the experiment ended, both Lenny and his manager could see the results plainly and were finally able to move on. Key takeaway: Sometimes there's no way AROUND something — sometimes you just have to go THROUGH it.

### Product Leader Roadmap Override Story (Why you’re so angry at work (and what to do about it))
An example of how unmet needs manifest as unproductive work behavior when a product leader's roadmap is overturned by an executive.

How it works: Situation: A product leader had her carefully crafted roadmap completely overturned by an executive after they used a competitor's product and decided to pivot the company's strategy. She was furious but instead of naming her need for respect and acknowledgment, she spent two weeks preparing a detailed competitive analysis to prove herself 'right,' stalling her team's work. The unmet need festered, and so did the anger.

### Script: Asking the CEO in a Live 1:1 (On asking for help (even when you really don’t want to))
Example of pushing back and asking for help when a CEO proposes a new project, delivered in a live conversation.

How it works: "I'm glad you want me to own this. I want to say yes, but I don't feel confident I can deliver it and need some support from you [signpost].

My top goal right now is to drive expansion within the Big Bad Customer account by moving it to multiplayer. To do that well and also take this on, I'd need us to either deprioritize the other initiative or staff up the new project for success [rationale].

Is there another possibility you see or other relevant context that you have that I'm missing [opt-in]?"

Note: No need for heavy 'why them' or 'timeline' in a live conversation. The signpost still helps set up your intent.
