# Fixing Underperforming Teams - Frameworks, Templates & Checklists

*14 artifacts extracted from Lenny's Podcast and Newsletter*

## Frameworks

### Enthusiastic Rehire Test (Rachel Lockett)
A binary question used at Stripe to gain clarity on talent decisions: 'Would you enthusiastically rehire this person for the same role?'

How it works: Question: 'Would you enthusiastically rehire this person for the same role?' If no: Take action—doesn't mean immediate firing. Options include: having the difficult conversation, performance plan, role change (e.g., CFO becomes controller), or parting ways. Key insight: The answer produces a physical, honest reaction that cuts through rationalization. Used especially when leaders are avoiding talent conversations about underperformers. Important context: In fast-scaling companies, leadership roles change naturally every 6-12 months, so 'no' doesn't mean the person failed—the job may have outgrown them.

### Execution Problem Diagnostic (Shreyas Doshi)
A diagnostic approach for determining whether a recurring execution problem is actually a strategy problem, culture problem, or interpersonal problem

How it works: Key diagnostic signal: If you put a bandaid on and it falls off (the same problem keeps recurring), it's probably NOT an execution problem. True execution problems: Infrastructure limitations, skills gaps, wrong person in wrong role. Strategy problems masquerading as execution: Teams misaligned because no clear strategy → misaligned OKRs → constant coordination friction. Culture problems masquerading as execution: Incentive structures that punish cross-team collaboration (e.g., 'optimize for your OKRs' culture). Interpersonal problems masquerading as execution: Two managers who can't get along creating friction that manifests as team misalignment.

### Observe, Identify, Share, and Act (OISA) Framework (Turning around an underperforming team - Issue 17)
A four-step framework for making changes on an underperforming team, adapted from the book Nonviolent Communication. It structures how to diagnose problems, build a case, communicate carefully, and then implement changes.

How it works: Step 1 — OBSERVE: Collect facts. Ask questions, listen, and observe the team. Sit in meetings, talk to team members, review team documents and processes. Come in with an open mind. Focus on getting crystal clear on what's working and what isn't.

Step 2 — IDENTIFY: As you observe and listen, collect your ideas on what needs to change. Ask: What is MOST contributing to this team underperforming? What is ONE change that would significantly improve performance? What are the actual ROOT causes (vs. symptoms)? Categories to explore: process issues, communication issues, people issues, team charter issues, expectations issues. Build out a document with: (a) the facts you've observed, (b) the impact the issues are having on the team, and (c) what you recommend change.

Step 3 — SHARE: Share what you've learned along with your recommendations. Manage the communication carefully. First share with peer leaders or higher-ups (depending on severity). Once peers and higher-ups are aligned, share the plan with your team.

Step 4 — ACT: Make the necessary changes. This step comes last, only after spending time observing, identifying, and communicating.

### Performance Diagnosis Framework (Anuj Rathi)
A mental model for diagnosing why a team member or team is underperforming.

How it works: 1. Can't do (capability issue requiring coaching or role change), 2. Won't do (motivation/alignment issue requiring vision alignment), 3. Not set up to do (leadership/org design issue requiring a fix to ways of working).

### Smart + Motivated People Framework (This Week #4: Motivating engineers to hit deadlines, PM career ladders, and aligning with execs)
Lenny's high-level diagnostic for team performance: if delivery is consistently slow, either intelligence or motivation is the issue

How it works: All you really need for your team to excel is: (1) Smart people and (2) Motivated people. If people continue to take way too long to ship, and you're confident this isn't just an incorrect perception, then one or the other is amiss. Figure out which one, and focus on addressing that. Smart → hiring and skill alignment. Motivated → connect to personal goals, mission, incentives, passions.

### The Low Impact PM Death Spiral (Matt LeMay)
A mental model describing how teams get trapped doing low-value work that eventually leads to layoffs.

How it works: Teams take on low-impact cosmetic work -> the product becomes heavy and complicated -> dependencies increase -> program management layers are added -> high-impact work becomes too hard to execute -> teams default to doing more low-impact work.

### The Waterline Model (Molly Graham)
A diagnostic tool for identifying the root cause of team dysfunction by looking below the surface.

How it works: Four levels (top to bottom): 1. Structural (goals, roles, expectations). 2. Dynamics (culture, decision-making, conflict resolution). 3. Interpersonal (relationships between two people). 4. Intrapersonal (individual struggles). Rule: 'Snorkel before you scuba' - always check structural issues before diving into personal ones.

## Templates

### Internal Transparency Survey (Christine Itwaru)
A survey sent to PMs and revenue teams to measure time spent on firefighting vs. quality work, identifying alignment gaps.

How it works: Questions include: 'Where's your time going, PMs?', 'How much time are you giving to the revenue team to firefight?', 'How much time do you spend with customers?', and for revenue teams: 'How many times do you find yourself asking questions?'

### Team Diagnosis Document (Turning around an underperforming team - Issue 17)
A document template for structuring your findings when diagnosing an underperforming team, referenced in the OISA framework's Identify step.

How it works: Structure the document with three sections:

1. FACTS OBSERVED: What you've seen and heard from meetings, conversations with team members, and document/process reviews. Stick to objective observations.

2. IMPACT ON THE TEAM: How these issues are affecting team performance, shipping velocity, morale, career growth, and work-life balance.

3. RECOMMENDED CHANGES: Your proposals for what should change, organized by category (process issues, communication issues, people issues, team charter issues, expectations issues). For each recommendation, highlight: (a) what is MOST contributing to underperformance, (b) the ONE change that would most significantly improve performance, and (c) the actual ROOT causes vs. symptoms.

## Checklists

### Five Tips for Turning Around an Underperforming Team (Turning around an underperforming team - Issue 17)
A five-step playbook for a PM or leader joining a team with a history of underperformance.

How it works: 1. SET CLEAR EXPECTATIONS WITH YOUR MANAGER: Ensure your manager and other influential leaders understand the situation. Expect 6-12 months of less business impact. Prepare them for the possibility that some team members will become upset. This sets you up so that when you succeed, it's all the more impressive.

2. SET A HIGH BAR: Don't make superficial changes and settle for 'good enough.' Work backwards from what the team COULD become (e.g., 'the best growth team in the world'). Get the team excited about this vision. When you see low-quality work, point it out privately. When you see great work, point it out enthusiastically and publicly. Don't settle — insist on great work always.

3. MOTIVATE THE TEAM TO MAKE CHANGE: Figure out what motivates key members. Connect changes to personal motivations (impact, promotion, work-life balance).

4. OBSERVE, IDENTIFY, SHARE, AND ACT: (a) Observe — collect facts with an open mind. (b) Identify — find root causes vs. symptoms; ask what ONE change would most improve performance. (c) Share — communicate recommendations to peer leaders/higher-ups first, then team. (d) Act — make changes only after the prior three steps.

5. FIND YOUR ALLIES: Map supporters, detractors, and persuadable people. Determine if you need buy-in from managers outside the team. Decide if changes should be gradual or require a 'come to Jesus' moment.

### Playbook: Moving or Letting Go of an Underperforming EM (Turning around an underperforming team - Issue 17)
A step-by-step process for handling an underperforming engineering manager on your team.

How it works: 1. Collect input from everyone who raises the issue, particularly those pushing for the change.
2. Put together your own POV on what's not working and what should happen.
3. Talk to your manager and get their perspective and support.
4. [Depending on the situation] Sit down with the EM and share what you've been hearing. See if you can help them turn things around.
5. Sit down with the EM's manager and share your perspective.
6. Work with the EM's manager and your manager to find a way to move forward — either making the change now or waiting to see what happens.

### Playbook: Resetting a Team Goal (Turning around an underperforming team - Issue 17)
A step-by-step process for resetting a misaligned team goal, including navigating organizational approval.

How it works: 1. Collect input from everyone who brings this up, particularly people pushing for the change.
2. Put together your own POV on what's not working and what should happen.
3. Talk to your manager and get their perspective and support.
4. Work with your peer leads to come up with a proposal.
5. Work with your manager and your peer leads to convince the folks up the org chart that change is necessary.

### Playbook: Revamping a Team Process (Turning around an underperforming team - Issue 17)
A step-by-step process for changing a broken team process.

How it works: 1. Collect input from everyone who brings this up, particularly people pushing for the change.
2. Put together your own POV on what's not working and what should happen.
3. Talk to your peer leads and get their perspective and support.
4. Sit down with the team and share what you plan to change, get their feedback and tweak as necessary.
5. Roll it out.

### The Consumer Stack Scorecard (Manik Gupta)
A 5-point checklist to evaluate a team's capability to build successful consumer products.

How it works: Rate the team (A-D) on: 1. Design-led thinking to delight users (craftsmanship, pixel attention). 2. Strong focus and prioritization (few features, critical user journeys). 3. Right metrics and instrumentation (codified, agreed-upon definitions). 4. High ship velocity and experimentation (ability to check in code and learn fast). 5. Strong cross-functional talent.
