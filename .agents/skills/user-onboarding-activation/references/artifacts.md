# User Onboarding and Activation - Frameworks, Templates & Checklists

*118 artifacts extracted from Lenny's Podcast and Newsletter*

## Frameworks

### 5-Step Behavioral Design Process (How behavioral science can boost your conversion rates)
An end-to-end process for applying behavioral science to product development, from research through experimentation

How it works: Step 1: LITERATURE REVIEW
- Spend a day getting up to speed on existing research insights related to your problem
- Helps develop your hypothesis before talking to users
- Avoids the trap of noisy small-sample user interviews leading you astray

Step 2: PICK A KEY BEHAVIOR
- Get 'uncomfortably specific' about the behavior you want to change
- Bad example: 'We want new users to create more workspaces'
- Good example: 'Within one week of joining, users start to fill in one workspace via a pre-existing template and invite two collaborators'
- Specify: How quickly? How many? From scratch or template? What adjacent actions?

Step 3: BEHAVIORAL MAP
- Map every single step someone needs to take to get to your key behavior
- Include both cognitive steps (making decisions) and logistical steps (filling out form fields)
- This is wildly more detailed than a typical journey map
- Usually leads to several 'aha' moments for marketing and product teams

Step 4: IDENTIFY THE PSYCHOLOGIES AT PLAY
- At each step of the map, identify underlying forces driving behavior
- Use the 3Bs framework: identify barriers to reduce and immediate benefits to increase
- Principle: Humans follow path of least resistance and respond to immediate incentives
- These insights drive your roadmap

Step 5: CONDUCT THEORY-BASED EXPERIMENTS
- Change only one thing at a time (vs. launching a 'redesign')
- Isolate what works and why
- Build a long-term roadmap around the insights
- Ground each experiment in a specific behavioral science hypothesis

### Activated User Churn Prioritization (How to make an impact in your first 90 days)
A rule of thumb for where to focus retention efforts in a PLG product based on typical funnel drop-off patterns

How it works: Rule of thumb:
- 50% of signups will NOT activate (meaningfully use your product even once)
- 40% of users who activate for the first time will drop within 7 days

Prioritization: Focus on that 40% churn first. Do NOT go after volume or non-actives.

Rationale:
- These users made the effort to use the product
- You have more information about them
- There will be 2-3 common drop-off reasons
- You can experiment around these reasons quickly

### Activation Escape Velocity Metric (Sri Batchu)
Identifying the specific actions a new user must complete in a defined timeframe to predict long-term engagement and retention

How it works: Examples: Facebook = 10 friends in first 7 days, Instacart = 3 orders in first month, Ramp = 4 specific events in first 30 days. The activation team focuses entirely on driving new users to hit this escape velocity threshold.

Good and great activation rate benchmarks segmented by 8 product types, based on 500+ survey responses.

How it works: Overall: Average activation rate = 34%, Median = 25%.
SaaS only (excluding marketplaces, e-commerce, DTC): Average = 36%, Median = 30%.

Product types surveyed:
1. B2B enterprise SaaS
2. B2B prosumer SaaS
3. B2C freemium/subscription
4. B2C free/ads
5. B2C marketplace
6. B2B marketplace
7. E-commerce

Key insight: Marketplaces and e-commerce have lowest rates (activation = spending money). B2C freemium has highest rates (low-friction milestones like starting a session or logging a meal).

### Activation Rate Formula (What is a good activation rate)
The standard formula for calculating activation rate.

How it works: activation rate = [users who hit your activation milestone] / [users who completed your signup flow]

### Airtable's Multi-Metric Activation Approach (How to determine your activation metric)
Airtable's four-metric activation framework that assesses different vectors of activation (aha, habit forming, collaboration, sophistication)

How it works: Overall activation milestone: W4MUA (week 4 multi-user active)

Four tracked metrics:

User level:
- Activity: W2A (week 2 active) rate, split by builder and collaborator personas
- Activity: W4A (week 4 active) rate, split by builder and collaborator personas

Workspace level:
- Activity: W4MUA (week 4 multi-user active) — at least 2 users working together in week 4
- Sophistication: Build rate — percentage of workspaces reaching intermediate use and setup, indicating they've built something substantial

Benefit: This multi-metric approach enables learning which specific component of activation (aha, habit, collaboration, sophistication) each experiment affects.

### Ben Williams' Aha vs. Habit Distinction (How to determine your activation metric)
Framework distinguishing between the 'aha moment' and the 'habit moment' in B2B PLG SaaS activation, with guidance on identifying user-level actions that feed team-level activation

How it works: Two distinct moments in activation:
1. Aha moment: User recognizes the value of the product
2. Habit moment: User or team forms a habit such that they will significantly more likely continue to realize value long-term

Focusing only on the aha moment can be a trap—you need to find the habit moment.

For B2B PLG, ask:
- What user-level actions are absolutely necessary as part of the activation process?
- What user-level actions may not be strictly necessary but increase the likelihood of reaching the activation milestone or accelerate getting there?
- Different actions in the activation process may be performed by different people (e.g., workspace creator vs. team member joining)

Visualize with an org-state model coupled with a narrative supported by data for wider communication.

### Blurred UI Signup Flow Technique (How to make an impact in your first 90 days)
A conversion rate optimization technique that places the signup form over a blurred version of the product to create curiosity and increase signups

How it works: Instead of the usual white background, place your signup form over a blurred or dimmed version of your product UI. Populate the UI with fake data (it's unclear anyway). This creates a 'peek behind the curtain' effect that makes users feel like they're at the final step. It taps into curiosity bias, making users want to see what lies beneath, and increases conversion rates.

### CAN and WANT Framework (Getting better at product strategy)
A framework for organizing adoption strategy into two tracks: enabling capability and creating motivation

How it works: Two tracks of work:
1. Features (CAN): 'I CAN use [feature] if these existed' — Build the tools and features users need to successfully adopt the new model
2. Incentives (WANT): 'I WANT to use [feature] if I could' — Create incentives and encouragements that motivate users to switch

Used on the Airbnb Instant Book team to organize the strategy into distinct workstreams after initially using a 'minefield' metaphor to convey host challenges.

### Can vs. Want Problem Framework (What Seven Years at Airbnb Taught Me About Building a Business)
A simple two-category framework for breaking down adoption barriers into capability problems and motivation problems.

How it works: Used for Airbnb's Instant Book adoption. Break gaps into two types: 1) 'Can' problems — Am I able to use it? (capability, access, technical barriers) 2) 'Want' problems — Do I want to use it? (motivation, trust, perceived value). Work through them in priority order. This made the problem of converting hosts to Instant Book more tractable.

### Contextual Feature Introduction (How to accelerate growth by focusing on the features you already have)
A principle for timing feature introductions at the moment of user need rather than during onboarding or via What's New pop-ups

How it works: Key principle: The right time to introduce a feature is IN CONTEXT — at a point when the user is most likely to want to use it.

Why traditional approaches fail:
- New-user onboarding: Limited capacity before overwhelming users; users forget by the time they need features
- What's New pop-ups: Users reflexively close them without reading

Why contextual introduction works:
- User who is told about a feature when they need it is much more likely to use it AND remember it
- Applies to ALL key features, not just newly launched ones
- For new users, every feature is new to them

Motivation tactics to pair with awareness:
a. Describe the benefits explicitly — don't assume users understand the value
b. Highlight different use cases — show users additional ways the feature can help them

### Conversion Optimization Framework: Two Focus Areas × Three Levers (Strategy and tactics for increasing conversion)
A structured framework for thinking about where to invest in conversion optimization, combining two macro strategies (keeping users in the flow and bringing users back) with three levers for in-flow optimization

How it works: Two places to focus for conversion:

1. KEEPING USERS IN THE FLOW — Three levers:
   a) Maintain user focus 🧐 — Prevent distractions that pull users away from completing the core action
   b) Maintain user motivation 🤗 — Give users reasons to continue and act now
   c) Reduce user friction 😄 — Remove steps, set defaults, improve performance

2. BRINGING USERS BACK INTO THE FLOW — Two dimensions:
   a) WHAT to tell them (content strategy)
   b) HOW to reach them (channel strategy)

Usage: Generate ideas within each lever, then test your favorites. Experiment with as many ideas as possible rather than betting on a few.

### Day 1 Commitment / Mental Model Principle (How behavioral science can boost your conversion rates)
Embedding behavioral nudges and commitments into the core product onboarding on day one rather than adding them as afterthoughts later

How it works: Principle: Day 1 is the day your users have the most momentum they will ever have. Catching them at this moment with the right features and mental model drives much higher opt-in rates than nudging later.

Example: Latino Community Credit Union loan repayment
- When signing loan documents on day 1, borrowers were asked by the loan officer to round up their payment (opt-out on loan form)
- Result: 36% opted in to round up their loan payment
- Compare to EarnUp's email nudge after the fact: only 14% opted in
- Same 'round up' concept, but 2.5x better results when integrated into day 1 onboarding

Why it works:
- Part of the core product's mental model from day one
- Integrated into onboarding rather than an afterthought email
- Users have maximum momentum and engagement at sign-up
- Behavioral science insights were designed into the product from the ground up

Key insight: With an up-front model of how and why someone will likely engage with your product, you're less likely to need marginal improvements later.

### Defaults vs. Modals Decision Framework (How GiveDirectly increased donations by over $3 million/year through experimentation)
A mental model for choosing between changing defaults and using interstitial modals to nudge user behavior.

How it works: Modals/Interstitials: Can dramatically increase the target behavior (e.g., 7x more monthly signups) BUT add friction that reduces overall conversion (e.g., 10% drop in one-time conversion). Best for: Situations where the target behavior uplift clearly outweighs the conversion drop.

Default changes: Provide a frictionless nudge that shifts behavior without adding steps. May produce smaller individual uplift but preserves conversion rates. Best for: High-traffic flows where even small conversion drops have large absolute impact.

Pre-checked vs. Unchecked checkboxes: Pre-checked can feel manipulative (especially if it changes the visible total/price), potentially hurting conversion. Unchecked respects user agency—lower opt-in rate but no conversion penalty. Rule of thumb: If the checkbox changes the price/amount shown, leave it unchecked by default.

### Endowment Effect / Psychological Ownership (How behavioral science can boost your conversion rates)
A behavioral principle where making users feel they already own something increases their likelihood of taking action to claim or keep it

How it works: When people feel like something is already theirs (the 'endowment effect'), they value it more and are more likely to act to keep it.

Application to product design:
- Frame actions as 'claiming' something that's already the user's, rather than 'joining' or 'signing up'
- Make the ask concrete and small rather than abstract and large
- Position inaction as losing something they already have

Example: Livongo changed email CTA from 'Join the program' to 'Claim your welcome kit'
- Result: 120% increase in registration
- Why: 'Claim your welcome kit' implies something already belongs to the user AND is a more concrete, smaller ask than 'joining a program'

Design principle: Your product is already theirs, and by not taking action, they may lose the opportunity to benefit from it.

### Facebook's 10 Friends in 14 Days Activation Metric (Sriram and Aarthi)
Facebook's discovery that new users who reached 10 friends within 14 days were likely to become retained users, which drove all onboarding product decisions including People You May Know

How it works: If a new user got 10 friends in 14 days, they would likely stick with Facebook. This drove aggressive tactics like People You May Know, which sometimes made existing users' experiences slightly worse to help new users connect faster — a systems-level trade-off that JTBD can't capture.

### Forced Choice Principle (How behavioral science can boost your conversion rates)
A behavioral design principle where users are asked to explicitly 'accept' or 'decline' rather than being given a 'skip' or 'maybe later' option, making inaction more costly

How it works: Instead of allowing users to skip or say 'maybe later' (which has very little cost), ask them to explicitly 'Accept' or 'Decline' a feature or action.

Why it works:
- 'Decline' is definitive and final — there's now a cost to not accepting
- 'Accept' looks much better in comparison to a permanent 'Decline'
- 'Skip' or 'Maybe later' has almost zero psychological cost, making it too easy to avoid engagement

Example: Steady app changed their bank linkage screen from having a 'Skip' option to having 'Accept' and 'Decline' buttons. Result: 63% increase in bank linkage (from 7.1% to 11.6%).

Can be combined with Goal Gradient (showing progress toward completing setup) for even stronger results — Steady saw conversion go from 7.1% to 15.9% when combining forced choice with goal gradient framing.

### Four B2B Aha Moments Framework (Five steps to starting your product-led growth motion)
A multi-persona aha moment framework for B2B PLG products that goes beyond individual user activation to cover team, buyer, and paid-customer moments

How it works: 1. User Aha Moment: Get an individual user to hit the aha moment via product usage quickly. This is the first step (most companies stop here).

2. Team Aha Moment: Help the initial user build confidence and invite team members to join. The team uses the product collaboratively to experience value. Example: GitLab defined this as '2+ users and 2+ features within the first 14 days.'

3. Buyer Aha Moment: When the user/team gets enough value, they advocate to the decision maker/buyer, who needs to clearly see ROI for themselves.

4. Paid-Customer Aha Moment: After purchase, the customer needs to reach the aha moment again on the paid version, answering: 'Is this worth it?'

Key insight: B2B aha moments are much more nuanced than Facebook's '7 friends in 10 days.' You must drive users through all four levels.

### Good Activation Metric Criteria (What is a good activation rate)
Two criteria that define a well-chosen activation metric.

How it works: A good activation metric must be:
1. Highly predictive: Predictive of long-term value delivery (retention, monetization, or both). Users who hit the milestone should retain at a rate at least 2x better than those who do not.
2. Highly actionable: Something growth teams can directly impact. For multi-user SaaS, set activation on user-level actions rather than workspace/account-level actions you can't control.

### Intent/Ease Framework for Conversion Optimization (Prioritizing conversion opportunities)
A framework that splits conversion levers into intent (motivation) and ease (focus + friction) and recommends which to prioritize based on current user intent level

How it works: Split conversion levers into two categories:
- **Intent** = motivation
- **Ease** = focus + friction (combined since they're usually related)

Decision logic:
- **If intent is already high** (e.g. user types your URL directly into browser): Less opportunity in increasing intent. Focus on ease instead. This is why 5+ year old product homepages tend to be bare and simple — the user already knows the product.
- **If intent is low** (e.g. user searches a generic term and lands on your page without knowing what your product is): Opportunities like educating the user to increase intent are bigger.

Every flow has different current intent and ease levels — evaluate each flow individually.

### Jackie Bavaro's Two Uses of Activation Milestones (How to determine your activation metric)
Two distinct purposes for activation milestones: noise reduction and quality user detection

How it works: Purpose 1: Reduce noisiness of sign-up rate
- Filter out users who never intended to use the product (e.g., curiosity-driven sign-ups from press coverage)
- Simple milestone like 'visited twice in a week' can work
- Reduces noise without excluding actual users

Purpose 2: Detect 'good' users
- Filter to users who are experiencing the product's core value proposition
- For collaborative tools, measure collaboration actions
- Run analysis to find the threshold that balances predictive power vs. quantity of activated user base

### Lazy, Vain, and Selfish (First Mile Framework) (Scott Belsky)
In the first 30 seconds of using a new product, users are lazy (want it done quickly), vain (want to look good to peers), and selfish (want to feel successful immediately). Design the first mile — onboarding, orientation, and defaults — around these three truths.

How it works: Three components of the first mile: 1) Onboarding flow — eliminate all friction, no tours or reading required. 2) Orientation — user should know how they got here, where they are, and how to get home/help. 3) Defaults — set presumptuous defaults that work for 90% of users. Key insight: most teams spend the final mile of building considering the first mile of experience.

### Lazy, Vain, and Selfish User Model (Anuj Rathi)
A mental model for empathizing with new users who have no prior loyalty to your product.

How it works: Users are lazy (need their minds blown quickly because they lack time), vain (don't want to change their existing habits), and selfish (need to know exactly what is in it for them).

### Merci Grace's Activation Metric Validation Test (How to determine your activation metric)
Three criteria to validate whether a candidate activation metric actually works for your business

How it works: Once you have a candidate activation metric, verify these three things are true:
1. You can ship experiments: You can build good in-product tests with enough statistical power to make them useful.
2. You can post wins: You are able to increase the rate of activation over months or quarters.
3. Increasing the rate of activation does not destroy the relationship between that activation metric and longer-term retention.

If all three are true, you have a foundation to experiment with adjacent funnel levels (demand gen, monetization).

### Onboarding Friction-Value Framework (Lessons from going freemium: a decision that broke our business)
A mental model for thinking about onboarding friction as a feature, not a bug — the goal of onboarding is activation, not completion

How it works: Key principles:

1. The goal of onboarding is NOT for people to complete onboarding or just get into the product. The goal is for people to get their FIRST MOMENTS OF VALUE (activation). Removing friction is actually detached from this goal.

2. The allure of seeing a new product is the strongest motivator new users have to complete complex setup tasks. If you need users to do something fundamental to seeing value, don't let them skip it — their motivation to do it will only decrease after they're 'in.'

3. Forcing users to invest upfront (credit card + limited-time window) adds inertia and urgency to get set up. The act of putting in a credit card increases their commitment to go through hard steps. A free plan does the opposite.

4. Onboarding is more sales than it is product — it's a game of convincing a new user to keep going. It's about momentum.

Two ways to build and maintain momentum:
- Don't let users skip critical setup steps (use the allure of the product as motivation)
- Require upfront investment (credit card, time-limited trial) to create commitment and urgency

### Onboarding Impact Magnitude Framework (Yuriy Timen)
Expected ranges of improvement from onboarding optimization by company stage

How it works: Early-stage companies (haven't approached local maximum, limited experimentation): 2-4x activation rate improvement through onboarding. Later-stage companies (Series B+): 20-30% lift in activation, depending on remaining low-hanging fruit. Key approach: Gather user intent information upfront, customize landing experience, don't expose full product robustness at once.

### Opinionated Defaults (Adam Fishman)
A product design principle for onboarding that guides users to success without removing their autonomy.

How it works: Make it hard to do the wrong thing and easy to do the right thing, without eliminating choice. Example: Adding friction if a user tries to set up 40 pricing tiers when data shows 3-5 is optimal.

### PLG Activation Multi-Channel Toolbox (Five steps to starting your product-led growth motion)
A prioritized set of channels for driving product-led activation, emphasizing product-led but not product-only

How it works: Product-led activation is product-led, not product-only. Channels (prioritized by ROI and reach):

1. Product onboarding — touches ~100% of new users, scalable and cost-efficient. Spend the most time here.
2. Lifecycle marketing channels — emails, in-apps, SMS
3. Human assistance — sales calls, demos, chats
4. Documents (especially for developer products)
5. Forums

Ideal PLG activation experience: Led by product, powered by data, supported by automated and low-touch channels, with high-touch assistance available to high-value customers.

### Pilling a Hotdog (Laura Schaffer)
A psychological onboarding framework for hiding intimidating steps inside familiar, comfortable environments.

How it works: Identify the 'bogeyman' step (e.g., telecom configuration for developers). Instead of making it Step 1, embed it later in the flow inside a 'safe' environment (e.g., a docs page with familiar code snippets).

### Present Bias / Immediate Benefit Evaluation (How behavioral science can boost your conversion rates)
A principle for evaluating whether the immediate benefit to the user exceeds the immediate cost at every step in a product flow

How it works: Core principle: Always evaluate whether the immediate benefit to your user is more compelling than the cost to them at each step.

Key insight: Due to 'present bias,' people disproportionately weight immediate costs and benefits over future ones.

Rules:
- Any step is friction, and friction presents a cost
- More cost means you need more immediate benefits
- There are exceptions (sometimes making sign-up harder can help), but generally reducing friction and increasing immediate reward drives conversion

Two ways to shift the benefit-cost ratio:
1. Increase the benefit of saying 'yes' (e.g., goal gradient — 'complete your setup')
2. Increase the cost of saying 'no' (e.g., forced choice — making 'decline' feel definitive and final)

Example: Steady app combined both:
- Made 'no' more costly via forced choice (Accept/Decline instead of Skip): 7.1% → 11.6%
- Made 'yes' more beneficial via goal gradient (complete your setup): 7.1% → 15.9%

### Prosumer SaaS Conversion Rate Benchmarks (Yuriy Timen)
Reference benchmarks for prosumer premium SaaS conversion rates at different funnel stages

How it works: Website visit to free signup: 20-35% at scale (can be 40-50% early stage with strong PMF in narrow segment, asymptotes at 25-30% as you go broader). Free to paid conversion: Under 5% won't work long term regardless of top-of-funnel size. North of 5% minimum, ideally 7%+. Consumer subscription LTVs: Most $5-7/month products cap at $50-60 LTV. Prosumer products with work use cases can reach hundreds of dollars in LTV.

### Proving Activation Causality Through Experimentation (What is a good activation rate)
A method for moving from correlation to causation in your activation metric.

How it works: Step 1: Start with a hypothesis-driven activation event that correlates with long-term retention/monetization. It's OK that it's only correlative initially.
Step 2: Run structured experiments to improve the activation metric.
Step 3: As you accumulate experiment winners, monitor later-stage metrics (retention, monetization) to see if they uptick as well.
Step 4a: If later-stage metrics improve → causality is proven. Keep iterating.
Step 4b: If later-stage metrics don't improve → go back to the drawing board and iterate on your activation metric definition.

Key advice: Don't overthink it. Pick a milestone that's relatively early in the user lifecycle and strongly correlates with retention, then just go.

### Qualitative Activation Discovery for Pre-Data Products (How to determine your activation metric)
A three-step qualitative process for determining activation milestones when you have little or no usage data

How it works: Step 1: Conduct in-depth interviews with target segment. Ask: 'What are the signals that this product is solving your problem?'
Step 2: Brainstorm a list of user actions that reflect those signals.
Step 3: Run a quantitative survey targeting the same segment. Show the list of actions and ask: 'Which of the following is the first moment that you feel that this product solves your problem?' Pick the milestone selected by the majority.

### Reality vs. Perception Framework (In-Product Marketing) (Nilan Peiris)
A framework for closing the gap between the actual value a product delivers and the user's perception of that value.

How it works: Identify what the product actually does (e.g., instant transfer, saved $10). Identify if the user realizes it. Build UI (like a wizzy animation or a comparison graph) at the success moment to prove the value, which drives referrals.

### Right for Wrong (Kristen Berman)
A design principle for motivating users by giving them an immediate, superficial reason to do a long-term beneficial action.

How it works: Examples include clearing an error message to sync a bank account, getting a shoutout on Peloton to exercise, or getting pizza at the polls to vote.

### Round-Up / Lean Into Existing Behavior Principle (How behavioral science can boost your conversion rates)
A behavioral design approach that identifies what users are already doing mentally or behaviorally and then makes it easier to do more of it

How it works: Principle: Instead of asking users to do something new, identify what they're already doing (even mentally) and design features that make it easier to do more of it.

Example: EarnUp fintech app
- Users already mentally round up their loan payment amounts
- EarnUp created a 'round up' feature asking users to round their payment to an even number
- Result: 40% increase in people choosing to overpay vs. simply being asked to increase payments
- Impact: Users shave an additional 2 years off their mortgage

Supporting data:
- Digit: 48% of users set goals that are multiples of $500; 87% set goals as multiples of $100
- Qapital: 93% of savings goals are multiples of $50; 88.5% are multiples of $100; only 2.6% of goal amounts don't end in 0 or 5

Peloton's Director of Product David Packles is vocal about using this strategy in Peloton's product design process.

### Six Ways to Build Trust with Users (This Week #6: Cultivating good relationships with distributed co-workers, building trust to accelerate growth, and leveling up as a PM when you have extra time)
A framework of six trust-building strategies for products where users need to share sensitive information or take a leap of faith, particularly relevant to fintech

How it works: 1. Social Proof: Leverage data and testimonials from existing users to show that people like the prospect are already using and loving the product. Show how many people are using it, what kind of people, from where, and for what purpose.

2. Authority: Surface certifications, approvals, badges, awards, seals, or endorsements from authority figures. Actively work to acquire more of these. Make sure users see them early and often.

3. Guarantees: Make users feel safe and protected no matter what. Address the question 'If something goes wrong, who's got my back?' Example: Airbnb Host Guarantee helped get new hosts over the hump of opening their home to strangers.

4. Reputation: Reviews from other users (e.g. Yelp reviews, Uber reviews, Airbnb reviews) are one of the most powerful ways to communicate trust. If many other people have had a good experience, it's likely you will too.

5. Great UX: A badly designed site/app immediately introduces suspicion, especially when dealing with money. Pay attention to details — resolution of images, padding, grammar, performance, and general visual polish. Little things make a big psychological impact. Listen for these issues in user research.

6. Deliver: Consistently provide a great experience and deliver on your promise. Word-of-mouth spreads quickly with our increasing interconnectedness — nothing destroys trust more quickly than failing to deliver. Example: The Amazon Flywheel.

### The 'One Egg' Onboarding Concept (Grant Lee)
A mental model borrowed from consumer advertising applied to user onboarding.

How it works: If you throw a consumer one egg (one core value prop), they can catch it. If you throw them five eggs (multiple features), they will drop all of them. Focus the first 30 seconds of onboarding on delivering just one magical concept.

### The 3 Bs Framework (Kristen Berman)
A mental model for behavior change focusing on Behavior, Barriers, and Benefits.

How it works: 1. Behavior: Define an uncomfortably specific action (e.g., 'Within 7 days, do two 10-min workouts with two different instructors'). 2. Barriers: Remove logistical (clicks/forms) and cognitive (uncertainty/status quo) friction. 3. Benefits: Add immediate, present-bias-friendly rewards.

### The 3 Bs of Behavior Change (EOY Review)
A behavioral science model for designing product features that drive user action.

How it works: 1. Behavior: Define an uncomfortably specific action (e.g., 'do two 10-min workouts in 7 days'). 2. Barriers: Reduce logistical friction and cognitive friction (uncertainty aversion, status quo bias). 3. Benefits: Add immediate benefits (completion bias, social desirability) to overcome present bias.

### The 3Bs Framework (Barriers, Benefits, Behavior) (How behavioral science can boost your conversion rates)
A simplified behavioral science framework for analyzing and designing product interventions. At each step of a user flow, identify barriers to reduce and immediate benefits to increase in order to drive the key behavior.

How it works: The 3Bs Framework simplifies the universe of behavioral biases into three actionable categories:

1. **Behavior**: Define the specific key behavior you want to drive
2. **Barriers**: At each step of the user flow, identify what barriers (friction, cognitive load, uncertainty, effort) are preventing the behavior
3. **Benefits**: At each step, identify what immediate benefits can be increased to motivate action

Key principle: Humans tend to follow the path of least resistance and respond to immediate incentives. To drive a key behavior, reduce barriers and increase immediate benefits at every step.

Application: For each step in your behavioral map, list the barriers and benefits. These insights then drive your product roadmap and experiment design.

### The Marginal User Approach (Adriel Frederick)
A method for identifying what to fix in a growth funnel by looking at the user who is on the cusp of converting but failing.

How it works: Find a segment with high intent but terrible conversion (e.g., users in a specific country). Look at the absolute worst-case scenario (e.g., feature phone on Edge network far from a data center) to expose all product flaws. Then, identify which of those flaws are solvable (e.g., language, latency) to prioritize fixes.

### Three Conversion Levers Framework (Motivation, Focus, Friction) (Prioritizing conversion opportunities)
A framework for categorizing conversion optimization opportunities into three primary levers: maintaining user motivation, maintaining user focus, and reducing user friction

How it works: Three levers for optimizing any conversion funnel:

1. **Motivation** 🤗 — Reminding the user why they should finish (e.g. changing copy, reinforcing value proposition). Highest upside potential but fewer opportunities.
2. **Focus** 🧐 — Reducing distractions so users stay on track (e.g. removing links, simplifying pages). Moderate opportunity.
3. **Friction** 😄 — Making it easier for users to complete the flow (e.g. fewer steps, faster load times, clearer CTAs). Most time spent here because there are more opportunities.

Prioritization order for ideation: Motivation → Focus → Friction
Practical time allocation: Friction gets the most time because it has the most surface area for improvement.

### Three Factors That Most Influence Waitlist Conversion (What is good waitlist conversion)
The three primary variables that determine waitlist conversion rates

How it works: 1. TENURE ON WAITLIST (biggest factor): People quickly lose interest and forget what they signed up for. Example from Linear: 30-50% conversion in first few months, dropping to 10% at 12 months.

### Behavioral Map Creation Checklist (How behavioral science can boost your conversion rates)
Steps for creating a detailed behavioral map of the user's decision-making environment

How it works: A behavioral map documents every single step someone needs to take to reach your key behavior. It differs from a journey map in that it's wildly more detailed.

Steps to create:
1. Define your key behavior (uncomfortably specific)
2. List every logistical step the user must take (e.g., filling out a form field, clicking a button, navigating to a page)
3. List every cognitive step the user must take (e.g., making a decision, evaluating options, understanding a concept)
4. For each step, identify BARRIERS: What makes this step hard? What friction exists? What uncertainty does the user face?
5. For each step, identify BENEFITS: What immediate reward does the user get from completing this step?
6. Evaluate: Is the immediate benefit greater than the cost at every step?
7. Identify the biggest drop-off points and the psychologies driving them
8. Generate intervention ideas that reduce barriers or increase benefits at each step

Usually leads to several 'aha' moments for marketing and product teams.

### Steady App: Bank Linkage Conversion Optimization (How behavioral science can boost your conversion rates)

- Focused user's attention on 'completing setup' with progress framing
- Result: 124% increase from baseline, from 7.1% to 15.9% linkage rate
- Principle: People want to complete things; making them feel closer to completion delivers a bigger immediate benefit
