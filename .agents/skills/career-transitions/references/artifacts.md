# Navigating Career Transitions - Frameworks, Templates & Checklists

*87 artifacts extracted from Lenny's Podcast and Newsletter*

## Frameworks

### 5 Tips for Transitioning from Founder to PM (This Week #14: Transitioning from startup founder to product manager)
A five-point framework for startup founders who are becoming product managers at an acquiring company, based on Lenny's personal experience at Airbnb

How it works: 1. Being a PM is very different from being a founder: As a founder, your job is to win at all costs—chaos, changing course, and pissing people off get swept under the rug if you succeed. As a PM, your job is to AVOID chaos, AVOID changing course, and AVOID pissing people off. Focus on softer skills: teamwork, collaboration, execution, buy-in, and communication. Hitting goals alone won't make your PM career.

2. Don't lose your entrepreneurial spirit: Companies acquire you because they want to move faster. Be the change you want to see. Take risks. Move fast. Push folks to think differently. Share thoughts on what the company should change. This is why they brought you in.

3. Find a PM buddy (or two): Build a strong relationship with a successful PM at the company. Ask them for the inside scoop on how things get done, who you should know, and what it takes to be successful. If you're the first PM, find a peer in a different function.

4. Be humble, ask questions: Don't enter with a huge ego assuming you know more than everyone. Assume everyone else is smarter than you. Spend most of your time asking questions, hearing people out, and studying the business. Then share your informed perspective.

5. Listen to your boss: You are no longer in charge of your destiny. Your boss's opinion matters for your career. In exchange for loss of control, you get steady salary, benefits, and not having to worry about everything that can go wrong.

### Act 1, 2, 3 Career Phases (Nikhyl Singhal)
A model for viewing a 60-year modern career trajectory.

How it works: Act 1: Learning and building the car. Act 2: Building the factory (management/scale). Act 3: Finding a new North Star (often focused on giving back or massive scale) to avoid becoming lost after achieving initial career goals.

### Ask-Learn-Do Framework for Career Transition (Breaking into growth)
A three-step framework for transitioning into a growth PM role, applicable to any PM career transition

How it works: Step 1: ASK - Tell your manager you want the role. Use a script like: 'Hey, manager, I just want to let you know that I'm really interested in product growth, and I want to get into a growth-focused role as soon as I can. What do I need to do to get there?' Don't just ask once — keep reinforcing. Ask to work together on a plan. Identify the skills you need and build them in your current role. Leverage the Baader-Meinhof phenomenon (frequency illusion) to your advantage.

Step 2: LEARN - Get smart about the domain. Follow a structured lesson plan covering key topics. Find a buddy and explore one topic a week.

Step 3: DO - Find time to do actual work in the target role. 'Do the job you want.' Demonstrate to yourself and others that you can do the work successfully.

### Asymmetric Career Risk Assessment (How to spot a top 1% startup early)
Bob's framework for evaluating the risk of joining an ambitious but unproven startup

How it works: When evaluating whether to join a risky-seeming startup:
- Downside: 'If the company failed, at worst, I'd wasted a year of my life and would have to go back to my PhD.'
- Upside: 'But if the company succeeded, it would be life-changing.'

The key insight: Joining Palantir 'seemed like a very risky proposition. But actually it wasn't risky at all.' The asymmetry between limited downside (one year) and potentially unlimited upside (life-changing outcome) makes ambitious startups less risky than they appear.

### Better Waves Make Better Surfers (Seth Godin)
A metaphor for opportunity selection—great performers succeed partly because they choose the right opportunities to pursue.

How it works: Great surfers are almost always on good waves. Experienced surfers pass up waves others would take, waiting for the right one. Applied to product/career: Which company you join, which roles you fill, which projects you take on matter as much as your skills. Being willing to wait for the superstar hire or the right opportunity beats rushing to meet imaginary deadlines with mediocre (average) work.

### Career Bingo Card (Shaun Clowes)
Approach career decisions by intentionally filling in experience gaps—choosing adjacent but stretching roles that build versatility and pattern-matching ability across different domains.

How it works: Principles: 1) Each role should fill boxes you don't have filled, 2) Choose adjacent but stretching—don't jump out of a plane without a parachute, 3) Different sales models, marketing approaches, product types, stack layers, operating models all count, 4) The reward is becoming 'scribble-shaped' not just T-shaped—deep in many things, 5) Going deep in something outside your job rarely has negative ROI—worst case you learned something, best case it becomes the critical tool when you least expect it. Shaun's examples: pre-sales engineering → PLG at Atlassian → consumer at Metromile → enterprise sales at Salesforce → infrastructure at Confluent.

### Career Decision Framework — Optimize for Learning (Peter Deng)
Peter's approach to career decisions: optimize for learning, seek companies with unique human behavior insights, and look for strong founder conviction.

How it works: Three criteria: 1. Will I learn more here than where I am? (Changed roles every ~2.5 years when learning plateaued) 2. Does the company have a unique insight about human behavior / impedance match between product and fundamental human needs? 3. Does the team have strong conviction? ('We may not be right, but we're not confused' — Kevin Systrom/Mike Krieger)

### Career Optionality Framework (500,000)
A mental model for thinking about career progression where leadership titles are stepping stones toward solopreneurship as the ultimate career goal

How it works: Key insight: Leadership titles should not be the end goal—only a step toward the ultimate unlock: career optionality.

Career optionality = solopreneurship where your brain is your product.

Monetization streams for solopreneurship:
- Advising
- Interim roles
- Public speaking
- Workshops
- Course creation

VP titles validate abilities and impact, aiding the journey toward solopreneurship.

Solopreneurship benefits: fulfilling, flexible, challenging, and impactful work.

Advice: Set out to get leadership titles, but not as an end destination. Work towards unlocking career optionality as the end goal.

### Career Sweet Spot Venn Diagram (Navigating your early career)
A three-circle Venn diagram for evaluating career opportunities. The ideal career sits at the intersection of all three circles. Used as a north star when considering career moves.

How it works: Three circles:
1. What you enjoy doing — activities that give you energy
2. What you're good at — your skills and strengths
3. What creates value for others — someone is willing to pay for it or it solves a real problem

The sweet spot is the intersection of all three. Key insight: 'What people often miss when following their passions/interests is creating value for others. Without that, it's just a hobby or a dead-end.'

Practical heuristic: 'Strive to do more of the things that give you energy, and less of the things that sap it. Over time, you'll gravitate towards the right work.'

### Come Alive vs. Get Ahead Decision Framework (Inspiration for the year ahead)
A mental model for making career and life decisions by prioritizing personal energy and aliveness over extrinsic outcomes and prestige.

How it works: The framework: When facing a decision (job offer, book deal, project), ask: 'Does this make me come alive, or does this just help me get ahead?' If the answer is only 'get ahead,' be skeptical. Personal energy and feeling connected > extrinsic outcomes like money, prestige, or status (beyond meeting basic financial needs). Example: Paul Millerd rejected a book deal because the publishers talked down to him and told him he probably wouldn't succeed. The prestige was there but the feeling of aliveness was not. He used the mantra 'come alive over get ahead' to make the decision.

### Coming Alive Over Getting Ahead (Paul Millerd)
Paul's personal life motto that prioritizes feeling energized and connected over chasing extrinsic achievements and status.

How it works: Used as a decision-making mantra. Paul repeated it to himself before rejecting the Penguin book deal: the publisher talked down to him, told him he probably wouldn't succeed. The mantra helped him choose creative control over prestige. Core belief: Extrinsic outcomes won't deliver lasting satisfaction; extended states of feeling alive and connected to your work are the actual goal.

### Conviction-Driven Career Navigation (Tomer Cohen)
A personal framework for career decisions: shift from externally-driven choices (prestige, demand) to internally-driven ones (conviction, excitement, potential for impact)

How it works: Old model (externally-driven): What's most in demand? What's most challenging? What's the best company? New model (internally-driven): What do I have strong conviction about? What am I passionate about? Where can I make a dent and learn? Evaluate product people by: What did you build? What did you learn? How well did it do? Not by companies, logos, or titles. Venn diagram: What's important (high impact/core) + What am I excited about = Best career bet.

### DS-to-PM Strengths and Gaps Assessment (This Week #10: Keeping designers and engineers excited about metrics + Transitioning from DS to PM 🕺)
Framework for evaluating what a data scientist brings to PM and what they need to develop

How it works: Superpowers (hard skills from DS background):
- Driving impact through data
- Prioritization based on evidence
- Execution rigor
- Strategy informed by analysis

Liabilities (soft skills gaps):
- Influence without authority
- Product taste and intuition
- Keeping a team you aren't managing happy and productive

Approach: Leverage your strengths, and actively fill in your gaps.

### Energy Tracking for Career Discovery (Paul Millerd)
A method for discovering your ideal work by systematically tracking what activities energize vs. drain you, then doing more of the former and less of the latter.

How it works: After every activity (calls, hobbies, writing, meetings with friends), ask: Did that give me energy or sap me of energy? Do I feel more energized or tired? Strategy: Do more of what energizes you, do less of what de-energizes you. Lenny's example: Advising/consulting calls → de-energizing → stopped. Writing and publishing → energizing → did more. Startup ideas → draining → stopped pursuing. Key insight: Even hard activities (like writing) can be broadly energizing despite difficult moments.

### Explore and Exploit (Ada Chen Rekhi)
A career strategy model adapted from growth marketing to balance trying new things vs. doubling down on what works.

How it works: Explore mode: Testing hypotheses about what you like (e.g., big company vs. startup, marketing vs. product) when you have little to lose. Exploit mode: Going deep into a specific industry or skill set once you've found a rich vein of opportunity.

### Fear Setting (adapted from Tim Ferriss) (Paul Millerd)
A structured exercise for evaluating fears around career change by writing down fears, mitigation strategies, and — critically — the cost of inaction.

How it works: Steps: (1) Write down your fears (framed around action you want to take). (2) How could you mitigate each fear? (3) What is the cost of INACTION? Paul's example: Fear of hiring someone for community = fear of creating a job for himself. Cost of inaction = draining all creative energy, which is an existential risk to his path. This reframe enabled quick action. Ongoing practice: Have conversations with your fears. 'We're worrying about money today. Okay, I see you. That's real. Six and a half years in, let's keep going.'

### Follow People, Not Domains career heuristic (Tamar Yehoshua)
Career decision-making framework: make a list of the people you most respect who are the best at what they do, see where they work, and use that as your list of companies to pursue. Prioritize learning and skill-building over financial returns or brand names.

How it works: Steps: 1) Identify people who are the best at what they do (not just people you like). 2) See where they work or where they're going. 3) Evaluate: will joining make you learn a durable skill? 4) For leaders: take jobs where hiring people will make their careers. 5) Avoid optimizing primarily for financial return. Key principle: 'Skills can't be taken away. A company can fail, but if you learn a skill, you will always have that skill.'

### Founder vs. PM Mindset Contrast (This Week #14: Transitioning from startup founder to product manager)
A mental model contrasting the key behavioral differences between being a startup founder and being a PM at a larger company

How it works: Founder mindset: Win at all costs. Success covers all sins. Chaos is tolerated. Changing course is expected. Pissing people off is acceptable if the company succeeds. Full autonomy.

PM mindset: Avoid chaos. Avoid changing course. Avoid pissing people off. Keep things running smoothly, productively, and happily. Success = smooth execution + team satisfaction + goal hitting. Soft skills (teamwork, collaboration, buy-in, communication) are as important as outcomes. You report to a boss whose opinion matters.

Key tension to balance: Don't lose your entrepreneurial speed and risk-taking, but channel it through collaboration and buy-in rather than unilateral action.

### Four Role Architecture Tests (How tech’s most resilient workers handle burnout)
Four evaluation tests for assessing whether your current role and company are the right fit, to prevent structural burnout from job misalignment.

How it works: 1. The Relationship Test: Who you work with matters more than almost anything else. Prioritize who you work for and with above all else. Ask: 'Am I surrounded by people I want to be surrounded by?'

2. The Sunday Test: How do you feel on Sunday? If you're excited for the challenge of the next week and looking forward to working with colleagues, it's a great sign. If you have Sunday scaries, take it seriously.

3. The Energy Test: Optimize for energy production. Seek meaning, challenge, and balance. Intentionally pursue work that gives you energy. Exit situations that drain your energy.

4. The Values Alignment Test: Reflect on what you truly want using the Five Whys framework. Don't get enamored by shiny objects. Evaluate: Is anyone around me burned out? Do people respect my boundaries? Does the culture give me ownership and trust?

### Four Steps to Break Into a Growth Role (This Week #9: Breaking into growth, leading with influence, and (not) stepping on toes 🦶)
Brian Balfour's framework for positioning yourself as a strong growth role candidate, from understanding the company's growth definition to building a portfolio

How it works: Step 1: Understand what growth means to the company — Every company defines growth teams differently (cross-functional product team, marketing subsection, market expansion team). Cultures vary from aggressive to reserved. Not understanding this is the top mistake candidates make.

Step 2: Form a point of view on how they grow — Best done using growth loops (not funnels). Come into interviews with a hypothesis on how the product grows, validate and refine it. Shows big-picture thinking.

Step 3: Focus on foundational tools, not tactics — Don't list tactical ideas (you're at a massive information disadvantage). Instead demonstrate: (a) data analysis (qual and quant), (b) user psychology, (c) experiment-driven methodology. Show how you approach solving problems using these three tools.

Step 4: Build a meaningful body of work — Take a portfolio approach (blog, personal site, private collection). If you don't have growth work yet, take products you use, pretend you're the growth PM, and show how you'd break down a problem. 'Application, not credentials' — portfolios stand out 10X more than LinkedIn badges.

### IC PM Team vs. PM Manager Team Shift (Moving from IC product manager to manager of product managers)
A mental model for understanding how your primary team changes when you move from IC to manager

How it works: As an IC PM, your team = your cross-functional IC peers (engineers, designers, DS, researchers, etc.)
As a PM manager, your team = your peer managers (EM, design manager, DS manager, etc.)

Tactical recommendations for the new team:
- Sit together physically
- Have a weekly leads meeting
- Constantly check in on priorities, blockers, and personnel issues

### Ikigai Career Framework (Tom Conrad)
Japanese concept represented as a Venn diagram of four spheres—what you love, what you're good at, what the world needs, and what you can be paid for—where the intersection is optimal career satisfaction

How it works: Four spheres: 1) What you love, 2) What you're good at, 3) What the world needs, 4) What you can be paid for. The intersection of all four = Ikigai. The three-but-not-four intersections are particularly dangerous because they feel close to satisfying but leave something important missing. Use this to evaluate career moves and identify what's missing.

### Layoff Runway Calculator Mental Model (How to be prepared for layoffs)
A simple mental model for assessing financial preparedness for job loss

How it works: Ask yourself: 'Imagine no more paychecks starting next month.'
1. What are you spending per month? (Use budgeting apps to find out)
2. How much runway do you have? (Total savings ÷ monthly spending = months of runway)
3. Based on the gap, take action: cut expenses, increase savings rate, build emergency fund

### MBA Decision Framework for PMs (This Week #1: Must reads on growth, what PMs should focus on when joining a new team, and the value of an MBA)
A simple decision framework for whether a PM should pursue an MBA based on their current career situation

How it works: Decision logic:
- If you ALREADY HAVE a PM job: In most cases, an MBA is NOT worth it. Experience doing the actual job beats business school. Pursue the alternative acceleration paths instead.
- If you are TRYING TO BREAK INTO PM or are STUCK in your career: An MBA is not the worst idea. Benefits include: learning valuable skills, it's an increasingly common path into PM, and some of the strongest PMs have had MBAs (though correlation, not causation).
- When evaluating PM candidates: Nothing beats experience doing the actual job. Two years of PM experience is generally valued more than two years of business school, all else equal.

### PM Career Acceleration Scorecard (6 Dimensions) (Which companies accelerate PM careers most)
A framework for evaluating which companies best accelerate PM careers, using six data-driven dimensions that can be triangulated into an overall ranking

How it works: Six dimensions to evaluate a company's PM career acceleration:
1. Most Promotions — How many promotions do PM alumni receive after leaving?
2. Fastest Immediate Promotion — How quickly do PM alumni get their next promotion after leaving?
3. Fastest Rise to Leadership Roles — How quickly do alumni reach VP, CPO, or Head of Product?
4. Highest Rate of Becoming CPOs and Heads of Product — What % of alumni reach these titles?
5. Highest Rate of Becoming the First PM at Another Company — What % become inaugural PM hires?
6. Highest Rate of Becoming Founders — What % go on to start their own companies?

Triangulate across all six dimensions to produce an overall ranking. Companies that appear in the top 10 across multiple dimensions rank highest overall.

### Resume Checkbox Trap (Navigating your early career)
A mental model illustrating how early-career professionals get stuck endlessly chasing resume credentials instead of doing what they actually want

How it works: Common resume checkboxes people chase:
✔️ Go to a top school
✔️ Become a manager
✔️ Work at a unicorn startup
✔️ Get a fancy title
✔️ Start a company

The trap: You keep telling yourself 'one day I'll finally do what I want, just after I get that one last checkbox.' But the pressure to keep building your resume never ends.

Key insight: 'You can often do what you actually want to do much sooner, without that perfect resume. Don't lose sight of what you're ultimately after.'

Visual: Drawing of a hamster running on a wheel — endlessly chasing the next credential.

### Startup PM vs. Big Company PM Comparison (Startup PM vs. big company PM, What is product management)
A visual comparison (table/image) contrasting the differences between being a PM at a startup versus a big company, accompanied by a list of similarities

How it works: **Differences** (referenced via image — implied contrasts based on context):
- Startup: You're the only PM; Big Company: One of many (potentially hundreds)
- Startup: Broader scope, more hats; Big Company: More specialized, more stakeholders
- Startup: Fewer processes; Big Company: More structure, more communication overhead
- Startup: Direct access to decisions; Big Company: Need to navigate hierarchy and influence

**Similarities (explicitly listed):**
- You're still building a product with a team of cross-functional resources
- You still need to get buy-in
- You still need to keep people aligned and excited
- You're still focused on solving customers/users' problems
- It's still hectic and stressful

### The 'One or Two Strengths' Career Stretch Model (Noam Lovinsky)
A mental model for evaluating if a new role is the right amount of challenging.

How it works: Criteria for taking a new role: The role must have significant stretch/pain for growth, but you must have 1-2 core things you are already really strong at (e.g., design sense, analytical thinking) to act as a foundation while you learn the rest.

### The 10-Year Technical Mastery Heuristic (Camille Fournier)
A heuristic for determining if an engineer has reached sufficient mastery to transition into management.

How it works: Mastery roughly equates to an undergraduate degree, a graduate degree, and 4-5 years of full-time work (or roughly 10 years of total coding experience) before moving to management.

### The Boiling Frog (Ada Chen Rekhi)
A mental model for evaluating whether you are staying in a bad job due to inertia.

How it works: Evaluate your environment by asking: 'Which way is the temperature trending?' and 'Am I still learning?' If the environment is slowly getting worse and learning has stopped, it's time to proactively change your situation.

### The Default Path (Paul Millerd)
A concept describing the unconscious script people follow about work and life (college → job → salary → house → marriage → family → continuous work until retirement). Used to evaluate whether you're consciously choosing your path or just following the default.

How it works: The default path is the story in your head about what you should do. Key questions to ask: (1) Are you conscious about what you're actually doing? (2) What are the costs of your game? (3) What are the trade-offs? (4) What implicit contracts have you created with yourself? (5) Have you 'fired the manager in your head'? Signs you're on the default path: you can't take an afternoon off without guilt, you equate being a good person with working every day, you've never asked 'why do I work?' beyond money.

### The Genie Framework (Graham Weaver)
A mental exercise to identify what you truly want to do with your life by removing the fear of failure.

How it works: Imagine a genie offers you one wish: whatever career or life path you throw yourself into will turn out great (though it will be hard and take longer than expected). What do you wish for? The answer reveals the path you should pursue.

### The Lindy Effect for Career Confidence (Paul Millerd)
Applying the Lindy Effect (the expected remaining lifespan of something equals its current age) to calm fears about the sustainability of an unconventional career path.

How it works: If your unconventional path has worked for X years, it's likely to work for at least X more years. Paul's examples: 6.5 years of self-employment → probably 6.5+ more years ahead. 4 years of newsletter writing → probably 4+ more years of topics to write about. Used as a daily practice to counter anxiety about sustainability.

### The Nine Lives Exercise (Graham Weaver)
A brainstorming tool to reduce the pressure of finding one single life purpose by imagining multiple fulfilling paths.

How it works: Write down 9 different lives you could live. Rule 1: All must start from today (no time travel). Rule 2: You must be excited about all of them. Identify which gives the most energy and pull elements of it into your current life as a side project or hobby.

### The Pathless Path (Paul Millerd)
A mindset shift away from the default path — embracing uncertainty and discomfort rather than treating not knowing what to do as a problem to be solved, and shifting from scarcity to abundance thinking.

How it works: Core definition: A shift away from not knowing what you're doing as a problem to be solved, toward an embrace of discomfort and uncertainty. A personal journey from operating around scarcity toward operating from abundance and having faith that things might be okay. Goal: 'Not to find a job, make money, build a business, or achieve any other metric. It's to actively and consciously search for the work that you want to keep doing.'

### The River Metaphor (Mummy Mode) (Andy Johns)
A mental model for navigating life transitions by surrendering to the current rather than fighting it.

How it works: Instead of treating life like a mountain to climb (fighting for the top), treat it like falling into a river rapid: go into 'mummy mode' (lay back, cross arms, feet out), stop fighting the current, and let it carry you to where you are meant to go.

### The Sabbatical as Life MBA Reframe (Paul Millerd)
A mental reframe that compares self-directed exploration time to the cost of business school to make it feel socially and financially legitimate.

How it works: Business school: 2 years not working + $150-200K tuition + no salary = socially praised. Self-directed sabbatical: Similar time and cost = socially judged. Reframe: Name your spending as investing in a 'life MBA.' Related reframe: 'Gift from your former self' — your past self earned the money specifically so your present self could have this freedom. Thank your former self.

### The Skip (Nikhyl Singhal)
A mental model for career planning where you focus on the job after your next job (the skip level) and work backwards to determine your immediate next steps.

How it works: Instead of focusing on your boss's job or your next promotion, identify your boss's boss's job or your ultimate goal (e.g., founder). Evaluate your current and next role strictly in service of reaching that 'skip' state.

### Three Skills for Startup-to-Big-Company PM Transition (Startup PM vs. big company PM)
A framework identifying the three PM skills to double-down on when moving from a startup PM role to a big company PM role: Communication, Influence, and Data

How it works: 1. **Communication** — Everything you do as a PM is done through writing, speaking, and meetings. 'Communication is the job.' Sub-skills: Emails, Docs, Meetings, Presentations, Storytelling.
2. **Influence** — The best PMs become de-facto leaders of cross-functional teams not through authority but by helping everyone do the best work of their lives. Tactics: observe successful PMs, foster team culture with rituals/outings, create lasting team identity.
3. **Data** — Your best friend in making decisions at large companies is a clear set of principles and hard data. The fewer opinions and the more facts, the easier your life. Tactics: study experimentation platforms, observe how leaders make decisions, always have a POV but be ready to change your mind.

### Three Types of Learners (Starter, Zero-to-One, Scaler) (Sanchan Saxena)
Framework for understanding what type of product work energizes you and choosing companies accordingly.

How it works: Three types: 1) Starter learning — you see an opportunity and can get started (best learned at startups), 2) Zero-to-one learning — taking what's started and finding product-market fit (growth-stage companies), 3) Scaler learning — scaling systems that have PMF (best learned at companies like Amazon, Google, Facebook). Choose companies based on which type of learning you need.

### 30-60-90 Day Onboarding Plan (Deb Liu)
A phased approach for joining a new company or taking over a new team to ensure trust is built before execution begins.

How it works: Days 1-30: Listening and learning focus (conduct a listening tour with 60+ people, summarize a 'state of the union', ask 'what is one thing I can do to help you this week?'). Days 31-60: Aligning on a vision for the future based on the listening tour. Days 61-90: Executing and setting up operational processes.

### Manager Conversation Script for Career Transition (Breaking into growth)
A script template for telling your manager about your desired career direction

How it works: "Hey, manager, I just want to let you know that I'm really interested in product growth, and I want to get into a growth-focused role as soon as I can. What do I need to do to get there?"

Key principles:
- Don't just ask once — keep telling them
- Ask them to work together on a plan to get you there
- Start by identifying the skills you'll need to be successful in the role
- Put together a plan to build those skills while in your current role

### 5 Tactical Steps to Level Up During a Reorg (Surviving reorgs, tactically and emotionally - Issue 32)
A five-step checklist for positioning yourself to advance your career when a reorganization happens

How it works: 1. Deliver impact: Spend the majority of your energy doing great work and delivering results. You'll only be considered for bigger roles if leaders believe you will succeed in those roles. Everything else is secondary.
2. Make known what you want: When new teams and initiatives are being planned, there will be a discussion about who the leaders should be. Unless these people know you are interested, you won't be considered. Bring it up in your next 1:1. Early and often.
3. Anticipate the change: Always try to predict where things are heading. Think 'if I were in charge, what would I change?' Use that to inform your planning — develop a new strategy just in case, adjust your current roadmap, or engage with someone who is likely to be your new boss. If you do this well enough and solve the problem the reorg would have tried to solve, it may result in no change having to happen at all.
4. Embrace the change: Don't fight the change. If you feel strongly the new direction is a mistake, make your thoughts known and express concerns clearly, but be ready to disagree and commit. Accept the new reality and do your darnedest to make it successful. Be the person making the shift successful, not the person constantly complaining.
5. Create the change: Be the person who recognizes that change should happen, figures out a plan to make it happen, and then makes it happen. Instead of just complaining about how badly things are working, be the person with a concrete proposal for how to fix it. Help your team and company become better proactively, before you are asked to.

A comprehensive checklist of five areas to focus on with specific actionable sub-items for each

How it works: 1. Demonstrate that you can lead people:

4. Demonstrate that you can develop a winning vision and strategy:
   a. Craft a winning strategy and get buy-in from leaders
   b. Align around ambitious and clear goals, and hit them
   c. Inspire those around you with your clear and ambitious vision

5. Ask for it:

### 7-Step Layoff Preparedness Checklist (How to be prepared for layoffs)
A comprehensive 7-step checklist for preparing for potential layoffs, crowdsourced from people who were recently laid off

How it works: 1. Save your accomplishments — Open a new doc and dump a list of times you've made an impact. Include metrics, screenshots, timelines, stories.
2. Build (and freshen up) your network — Ping your 5 most talented friends/colleagues at other companies. Join communities. Participate in hackathons. Go to local meetups. Reply to recruiters.
3. Update your resume — Capture accomplishments, create V1 resume using templates, get feedback from friends, share in communities, explore resume review services. Update LinkedIn too.
4. Add some cushion to your savings account — Cancel unnecessary subscriptions, track spending/create budget, use automatic savings apps, put money in high-yield savings, learn about FIRE.
5. Explore additional sources of income — Join Indie Hackers, get inspiration from content creators, explore side hustle ideas (but be realistic about income potential).
6. Save your favorite people's contact info — Save personal contact details for colleagues you care about before losing access to company systems.
7. Don't let work define you — Create distance between work and identity. Think about what you'd want your next job to be. Explore 'roads not taken.'

### Engineer-to-PM Career Switch Self-Assessment (Should I become a product manager)
A two-part checklist to help engineers evaluate whether they should pursue product management or stay in engineering. Designed as a 'which bucket do you fall into more' exercise.

How it works: PURSUE PM IF:
1. You're more excited about solving business challenges (e.g. strategy, growth, positioning), and customer challenges, than technical challenges
2. You often have strong opinions about the user experience
3. Teammates often agree with your product feedback
4. You enjoy being at the center of the action, vs. on your own, heads-down working
5. You could see yourself being happy never coding professionally again
6. You look forward to collaborating with non-engineer stakeholders
7. You feel like you're a mediocre engineer, and aren't motivated to put in the time to become significantly better

DON'T PURSUE PM IF:
1. You expect to finally run the show — most of your time will be spent aligning engineers like yourself, along with designers, researchers, execs, etc.
2. You think it'll be an easier career path — there are far fewer PM roles than engineering roles
3. You can't imagine not coding — once you're a PM you'll only have time to code on nights and weekends
4. You'd fail the marshmallow test — as a PM, you need to be good at finding your joy in long-term outcomes
5. You expect to make more money — PMs and engineers make about the same amount of money

### Rebecca — COO promotion paralysis resolved through parts-work (Why no productivity hack will solve your overwhelm)
A real coaching client example showing how IFS helped resolve career decision paralysis for a new mom offered a COO promotion

Key reframe: Shifted from 'Should I take this promotion?' to 'What do I need in order to feel more confident and clear about saying yes?'
