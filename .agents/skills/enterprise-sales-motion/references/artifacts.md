# Mastering Enterprise Sales Motion - Frameworks, Templates & Checklists

*41 artifacts extracted from Lenny's Podcast and Newsletter*

## Frameworks

### 5-Step B2B Sales Cycle (Jen Abel)
A structured progression of calls for turning non-consumers into consumers.

How it works: Call 1: Intro call. Call 2: Demo. Call 3: Proposal/Scope of work. Call 4: Co-authoring scope/feedback. Call 5: Procurement introduction. Post-procurement: Signature.

### Architecture Whiteboarding Discovery (Jeanne Grosser)
Replacing standard Q&A discovery calls with a collaborative session where the prospect maps out their technical architecture.

How it works: Instead of quizzing the prospect, the rep and prospect draw the current stack together. This uncovers the tech stack, competitors, and pain points while leaving the prospect with a valuable asset (their own architecture diagram).

### Engineers-as-Sales Model (The unconventional Palantir principles that catalyzed a generation of startups)
A sales approach for complex, mission-driven products where engineers and domain experts replace professional salespeople in customer-facing roles.

How it works: Core philosophy: For a complex mission-driven sale, put former operators and engineers in the room instead of professional relationship brokers.

Key behaviors that replace traditional sales tactics:
- Speak intelligently about the mission and product from firsthand experience
- Don't hide skeletons or answer questions 'the right way'
- Speak to clients like normal people
- Find ways to create value in sales settings and not waste time
- Ask authentic questions like: 'How will you make YOUR money on this?' and 'How fast can you get us integrated into your mission so we can have an impact?'

What to eliminate: golf, steak dinners, boxes at sporting events, commission-driven closing questions, opportunistic pitches

When this works: If what you are building is incredibly compelling and someone truly wants to buy it, the customer doesn't require a person with a sales background.

Lessons:
1. Evaluate who the customer needs to hear from to believe, and put those people in the right places at the right times
2. Delay hiring professional salespeople for as long as possible

### Give-to-Get Discount Framework (How to make an impact in your first 90 days)
A structured approach to ensuring your company gets something in return every time it offers a sales discount

How it works: When offering a discount, always get something back. Possible concessions to request:
- Customer references/testimonials
- Product feedback
- Longer commitment term
- Annual price escalator (price automatically increases at renewal)

At the very least, set up tracking for the most common 'gets' for every sales discount. The key insight: many sellers are giving discounts and getting nothing back.

### Go-To-Market Motion Matrix (Bret Taylor)
A framework for selecting the right sales motion based on the product and buyer.

How it works: 1. Developer-led: Use for platform products sold to startups where engineers have buying latitude. 2. Product-Led Growth (PLG): Use when the user and the buyer are the exact same person (e.g., SMB sole proprietors). 3. Direct Sales: Use when the user (employee) and the buyer (finance/department head) are different people.

### Hungry Senior AE Profile — First Sales Hire Archetype (Hiring your early team)
The ideal candidate profile for your first dedicated salesperson, based on patterns across successful B2B startups

How it works: Core archetype: 'Hungry Senior AE' — Somewhat experienced, early-career salespeople who can break through the snow and figure out new things, not just run repeatable playbooks.

Key traits to look for:
- Evidence in resume/interviews of breaking new ground (not just executing playbooks)
- Entrepreneurial mindset (former founder, opened new offices, cross-functional experience)
- Willing to be boots on the ground working deals themselves
- Can navigate ambiguity of early-stage startup
- Thinks of themselves as marketer and product person too
- Generates insights to inform product roadmap
- Creative, raw intelligence, ability to collaborate with engineers/PMs

Real examples:
- Vanta: Senior AE who had been a founder, thought of himself as a marketer, loved recruiting
- Stytch: AE with mature sales org experience at Plaid + had been first AE opening Plaid's Europe office
- Zip: AE with large company experience plus cross-functional roles (programs, enablement)
- Sprig: Top enterprise AE at Box (caveat: star AE doesn't always grow into head of sales)
- Census: Two senior AEs hired in quick succession for comparison
- Gusto: Former investment banker — hired for creativity, raw intelligence, collaboration
- Databricks: Three young AEs (former SDRs) focusing on SMB + one senior enterprise AE

Anti-pattern: Later-stage salespeople who are great at repeatable processes and playbooks but can't break new ground.

### Lego Bricks vs. Lego Boxes (Ivan Zhao)
A framework for segmenting and selling horizontal software to different types of users.

How it works: 'Bricks' are the underlying flexible primitives for power users and builders. 'Boxes' are the pre-packaged, ready-to-use solutions sold to enterprise customers who want to solve a specific business problem without building it themselves.

### MEDDIC Sales Methodology (SEO keywords, career ladders, backlog tools, copywriting, OnlyFans, AMA with Pete Kazanjy and much more)
A deal qualification framework for large enterprise sales with complex, multi-stakeholder deals.

How it works: MEDDIC stands for: M — Metrics: Quantifiable measures of success the customer cares about. E — Economic Buyer: The person with the authority to approve the deal. D — Decision Criteria: The formal criteria the organization uses to evaluate solutions. D — Decision Process: The steps and timeline for making the purchase decision. I — Identify Pain: The specific business pain the solution addresses. C — Champion: An internal advocate who sells on your behalf inside the organization. Used for: Large enterprise deals with complex stakeholders, high ACV, and long sales cycles. Helps with 'being crisper about deal modeling.' Reference: lucidchart.com/blog/close-more-deals-with-meddic-sales-process.

### Mission-First Value Chain (The unconventional Palantir principles that catalyzed a generation of startups)
A sequencing framework for enterprise product companies showing the correct order of operations: access → value → contracts → growth.

How it works: Correct sequence:
1. Mission Access — Get access to the real problems through customer engagement
2. Product Value — Create demonstrable value for the customer's mission
3. Contracts — Once value is proven, contracts are easy to close
4. Growth — Revenue and expansion follow naturally

Anti-pattern (reversed chain):
1. Push sales and your way of thinking onto customers
2. Result: Customers react like someone who wants to leave a blind date as early as possible

Key quote from Alex Karp: 'Society doesn't have to make a choice between doing good and doing well.'

Implication: Skip SaaS-style upsell mechanisms, psychological tricks, and months obsessing over customer funnel. Focus on mission value and hiring people who care more about solving missions than money.

### Modern Salesperson as Consultant Reframe (SEO keywords, career ladders, backlog tools, copywriting, OnlyFans, AMA with Pete Kazanjy and much more)
A mental model for introverts approaching sales — reframing sales as consultative work rather than extroverted persuasion.

How it works: Core reframe: Modern salespeople are 'consultants who happen to have a predisposition to one particular solution (theirs).' Key skills that matter more than extroversion: analysis, writing, math, logic, argumentation, pre-call planning, proposal creation. These are where a 'huge amount of the work is done.' For introverts: Lean into preparation and homework (pre-call planning, proposal creation). Build selling muscles over time through repetition — comfort with selling behaviors comes from practice, not personality. Reference: Chapter 1 of Founding Sales on Mindset Changes (foundingsales.com/1-mindset-changes) and Founder Led Selling Maturity Stages slide deck.

### New Rep Ramp Timeline with Leading Indicators (Pete Kazanjy)
A month-by-month framework for evaluating whether a new sales hire is on track, using stage-appropriate leading indicators rather than waiting for closed revenue.

How it works: Month 1: Onboarding — teach sales motion, mock discovery, mock demos, ride-alongs with founder. Leading indicator: can they demo and run discovery competently? Month 2: 10-20 first meetings. Leading indicator: 50%+ converting to second meetings. Month 3: Subset of pipeline reaching proposal/commercial conversation stage. Some deals may close. Month 4: Meaningful closed revenue expected. Red flags at each stage: Month 1 — can't absorb material. Month 2 — not getting first meetings on calendar (fatal, act immediately). Month 3 — getting meetings but no progression (coaching issue). Month 4 — proposals but no closes (may need intervention).

### Pings and Echoes (Matt Dixon)
A conversational technique to test hypotheses about a buyer's hidden fears without directly asking them.

How it works: State a common concern other buyers face at this stage (the 'ping') and listen to whether the buyer confirms or refutes it (the 'echo'). Example: 'A lot of customers get overwhelmed with options at this point... are you clear on what's in and out?'

### Sales Channel Design Principle (Essential reading for product builders—part 2)
Horowitz's principle that sales channels must be deliberately designed based on your product and market, not copied from other companies.

How it works: A sales channel is a route to market for a product or set of products. Key points:
1. It can range from your website to a sophisticated sales force.
2. The sale must be supported with the right marketing, process, and optimization strategy.
3. Selecting the right channel is critical for any business.
4. Products often fail because the company chose the wrong route to market.
5. Common mistake: Founders reject enterprise sales by default (stereotyping salespeople) and try to copy consumer distribution models like Dropbox without understanding why those models worked.
6. Channel selection should be driven by your product, market, and customer—not by which model seems cooler.

### Sales Comp Plan Aligned to Retention (Sahil Mansuri)
A modern sales compensation structure that rewards customer quality and retention rather than just new business closed, including kickers for renewals, tracking rep-level churn rates, and rewarding customer referencability

How it works: Traditional model: 50-50 base/variable split, OTE = 5:1 quota ratio, accelerators for exceeding quota, no connection to retention. Proposed changes: 1) Include renewal rate in rep comp—if a customer renews, rep gets a kicker. 2) Compare individual rep renewal rates to company average. 3) Reward reps who generate referenceable customers and case studies. 4) Track and reward upsell/expansion from rep's original deals. 5) Consider shifting to 60-40 or higher base ratio to reduce mercenary behavior. 6) Apply same logic to VP of Sales/CRO comp (currently also 50-50 in most companies). 7) Stop weighting comp plans with short-term product push incentives.

### Sales Economics Calculation Model (The Transition: Layering sales onto a bottom-up self-serve product)
A formula for determining whether adding a salesperson is economically viable, with a worked example

How it works: Formula components:
- Average time per opportunity (both wins and losses): e.g., 2 x 30-minute meetings
- Rep meeting capacity per week: e.g., 20 meetings/week = 80/month
- Monthly opportunity budget per rep: e.g., 40 opportunities/month
- Monthly rep cost (salary + commission): e.g., $10K
- Cost per opportunity managed: Monthly cost / opportunities = e.g., $250
- Average deal value: e.g., $5K
- Win rate: e.g., 25%
- Monthly wins: Opportunities x Win rate = e.g., 10
- Monthly revenue per rep: Wins x Deal value = e.g., $50K
- ROI ratio: Revenue / Rep cost = e.g., 5x

Rule of thumb: Target 4x+ fully loaded cost in incremental revenue.

Alternative calculation for conversion assist:
- Annual value per user: e.g., $5,000
- Baseline conversion rate: e.g., 10%
- Assisted conversion rate: e.g., 20%
- Time cost of engagement: e.g., 2 hours
- Hourly staff cost: e.g., $50/hour
- Cost to engage: e.g., $100
- Expected conversion uplift value: (20%-10%) x $5,000 = $500
- If $100 cost captures $500 uplift, it's worth it.

If the math shows only ~2x return, sales assist may not work unless you can: raise prices, lower rep costs (e.g., offshore), raise win rates, or increase opportunity volume per rep.

### Sales Motion as Source Code (Pete Kazanjy)
A mental model for treating your sales process like software — run small cohorts, observe what breaks, update the 'code' (slides, scripts, objection handling), and deploy the updated version in the next cohort.

How it works: 1. Run a cohort of ~10 prospect meetings. 2. Observe how discovery questions, slides, and demos land. 3. Identify gaps (unanswered objections, missing slides, weak talk tracks). 4. Update collateral — add slides for new objections, refine discovery questions, update demo script. 5. Deploy updated sales motion on next cohort. 6. Repeat continuously. Analogy: prospect asks a question you can't answer → create a slide for it → next time that objection comes up, show the slide → get better conversion to next stage.

### Sales Rep A vs B Thought Experiment (Sahil Mansuri)
A comparison framework to evaluate whether your comp plan rewards the right behaviors by contrasting a high-volume closer with a high-quality closer

How it works: Setup: Same quota ($1M), same OTE ($200K), same product. Rep A: 15 deals, $1.5M closed (150% quota), earns $400K with accelerators, wins President's Club and CEO award. But 10 of 15 customers churn. Impact on comp: zero. Rep B: 12 deals, $1.2M closed (120% quota), earns $250K, no trip, no award. But all 12 renew, 3 become case studies, 6 become references, 4 upsell and sign multi-year contracts. Impact on comp: zero. Question for founders: Which rep actually created more business value? Your comp plan should reflect the answer.

### Second Date Metric / Stage Conversion Leading Indicators (Pete Kazanjy)
A dating metaphor for measuring sales pipeline health: track whether prospects are progressing to second meetings, third meetings, proposals, and closes rather than only looking at closed revenue.

How it works: Target conversion benchmarks: 10 first meetings → 7 second meetings → 4.5 third meetings → 2-3 wins. If not getting second meetings: messaging/targeting problem. If getting second but not third meetings: demo/value prop problem. If getting to proposal but not closing: pricing/competition/urgency problem. Monitor these at every stage rather than waiting for lagging revenue data.

### The JOLT Method (Matt Dixon)
A four-step framework to overcome customer indecision and close deals.

How it works: J - Judge the level of indecision; O - Offer a recommendation; L - Limit the exploration; T - Take risk off the table.

### The Setup Framework (Insight → Alternatives → Perfect World) (How to build a killer sales pitch)
The three-component breakdown of the Setup portion of the sales pitch, designed to help customers confidently navigate their purchase decision.

How it works: Component 1 — INSIGHT:
- Definition: What does a customer need to know to understand why your differentiated value is important?
- It is your unique point of view on the market.
- It is NOT: a generic problem statement, a trend, or 'the change in the world' — those are not differentiating because any competitor can claim them.
- Test: Could a competitor also say this? If yes, it's not a real insight.

Component 2 — PROS AND CONS OF ALTERNATIVE APPROACHES:
- Group competitors into approaches/categories, not individual products.
- Teach customers honestly about what works and doesn't work with each approach.
- Include the status quo as one of the approaches.
- This is the 'cure for customer indecision' — you're teaching about the entire market, not just your product.
- Use this step for sales discovery: ask about what they're using, what they've considered.
- This builds credibility with the prospect.

Component 3 — THE PERFECT WORLD:
- Synthesize the discussion: 'Knowing what works and doesn't work with the alternative approaches for a customer like you, what would a perfect solution look like?'
- Present your list of requirements that the perfect solution should have.
- Ask explicitly: 'Would you agree with this?'
- If YES → proceed to follow-through (prove you deliver it).
- If NO → disqualify the prospect ('we literally have nothing to sell them').
- Magic moment: If the prospect agrees, the sale is yours — all you have to do is prove delivery.

### Why Companies Move Upmarket (Not Downmarket) (GTM motions of 30 B2B SaaS companies)
Brian Balfour's explanation for why it's easier to add complexity than to simplify, making upmarket movement the natural direction

How it works: Starting downmarket requires focus on simplicity and creating a self-serve, frictionless motion. It is easier to add things to adapt to enterprise than it is to start complex and then simplify over time. Removing features, flows, permissions, data fields, salespeople, etc. goes against the natural motion of organizations—which is to ADD.

Key implication: Your initial market is just your initial market. Eventually, you'll be pulled upmarket. Plan for this from the start.

## Templates

### Early Sales Comp Plan (Jason M Lemkin)
A compensation structure for the first sales hires at a startup.

How it works: 50/50 split between base and bonus. For the first 3 months (one quarter max), let the rep keep 100% of what they close to help them eat and build momentum. Long-term goal is for the rep to bring in 3x to 5x their OTE (On-Target Earnings).

### The Bowling Alley Sales Script (Geoffrey Moore)
How to open a sales conversation with a pragmatic buyer without pitching.

How it works: Script: 'We're here because we've been working with some people in your industry and we understand there's this really serious problem around [specific issue], and we believe that your company might have it, is that true?'

## Checklists

### Enterprise Feature Readiness Checklist (Varun Parmar)
A checklist of enterprise requirements that every feature team references early in development to ensure enterprise needs are baked in from the start

How it works: Purpose: Democratize deep enterprise requirements across all feature teams so they don't get bolted on downstream. Usage: When building a new feature, PMs reference the checklist to incorporate enterprise considerations (security, compliance, admin controls, etc.) early in architecture and definition. Contains ~10 items covering enterprise needs. Led by a dedicated PM who owns this charter. Still being refined—described as a work in progress.

### Leave No Deal Behind Campaign Checklist (How to make an impact in your first 90 days)
A set of campaigns to improve win rate by re-engaging lost opportunities

How it works: Campaigns to ensure no deal is left behind:
1. Re-engage deals previously lost to competition
2. Reach out to prospects with high product-led growth scores (high product usage) that didn't convert
3. Send trial cancellation emails asking what could have been done better

Context: Improving win rate as a company key result. Product builds features to support this while sales and marketing work the re-engagement motions.

### PM Litmus Test for Sales Hires (Jeanne Grosser)
A standard to ensure sales reps have sufficient technical and product depth.

How it works: Criteria: If an Account Executive is put in front of 10 internal engineers, it should take the engineers at least 10 minutes to realize the AE is not a Product Manager.

## Examples

### Airtable Remnant Billboard Strategy (Zoelle Egner)
Using cheap remnant billboard inventory near target company offices to signal credibility and help close enterprise deals

How it works: Goal: Signal to large companies (fashion, media in NYC) that Airtable was a legitimate, large company they could trust for six-figure purchases. Tactic: Purchased remnant inventory (end of buying cycle, unsold placements) near target company offices. Cost: Low thousands of dollars per billboard. Outcome: When IT received budget requests for Airtable, decision-makers had seen the brand on their commute, reducing perceived risk. Key insight: Everyone assumes only huge companies buy billboards, so it's an asymmetric credibility signal.

### Autobooks Sales Process Optimization (Bob Moesta)
A case study on matching sales demos to the buyer's journey stage to increase conversion.

How it works: Instead of one demo trying to close, they created three: 1. Storytelling/background for passive lookers, 2. Showing alternatives for active lookers, 3. Giving choices for deciders. Resulted in 4x conversion.

### Bravado Quota Attainment Benchmarks (Q1-Q3 2022) (Sahil Mansuri)
Real-time data on sales rep and company-level quota attainment across B2B SaaS, showing accelerating deterioration through 2022

How it works: Individual rep miss rates: Q1 46%, Q2 54%, Q3 63%. Company miss rates: Q1 51%, Q2 59%, Q3 76%. October monthly: 85% of reps missed quota. Prediction: 80%+ companies will miss Q4. ### Dentsply XP-9000 Sales Pitch (Matt Dixon)
A case study of transitioning from a feature-led pitch to an insight-led 'Challenger' pitch.

How it works: Instead of pitching a lightweight cordless drill and getting rejected on price, the reps started by discussing the high costs of hygienist absenteeism and workers' comp due to repetitive motion injuries, then positioned the drill as the only solution.

### Enterprise vs. Startup CRM Risk Profile (Matt Dixon)
An example illustrating the three drivers of fear of failure in software buying.

How it works: Three drivers of fear: 1) Did I choose the right configuration? 2) Will I learn something later that makes me look dumb (e.g., Gartner Magic Quadrant drop)? 3) Will we actually see the promised ROI? Big enterprise brands actually trigger more fear because they offer too many choices, cost more, and have more public scrutiny.

### Joey the Buyer — B2B Purchase Journey Scenario (How to build a killer sales pitch)
A detailed narrative scenario illustrating the typical stressful B2B software buying process and why 'no decision' is the most common outcome.

How it works: Scenario: Janet (VP of Finance) struggling through an audit decides the company needs new accounting software. She delegates to Joey (controller) to find a recommendation.

Joey's journey:
1. Feels stressed and pissed — doesn't know the accounting software market
2. Researches online — finds hundreds of products with similar-sounding features, all claiming to be 'the world's best'
3. Comparison sites (G2) list a dozen 'leading' packages with hundreds of features
4. Signs up for trials — but trials can't answer key questions (audit support, IT integration, security, international offices)
5. Books demos — reps ask requirements questions Joey can't confidently answer
6. Reps do feature walkthroughs — Joey can't assess importance or differentiation
7. Stress peaks — fears: boss hates recommendation, team blames him, product fails during audit, gets passed up for promotion or fired
8. RESULT: Joey tells boss 'now's not a good time, what we have is fine, let's delay until next year'

Key insight: Delaying the decision gets Joey off the hook. The easiest decision is 'no decision.'
Statistic: 40-60% of B2B purchase processes result in 'no decision' (from The JOLT Effect analysis of 2M+ sales calls).

Benefits:
1. Could show prospects/customers real value on their own data

### Postman Sales Pitch — Before and After (How to build a killer sales pitch)
A complete before/after comparison showing how Postman's sales pitch transforms from a feature walkthrough to a positioning-oriented pitch using the two-part structure.

How it works: BACKGROUND:
- Postman has free version for individual devs (API development/testing)
- Full product = complete API lifecycle platform (development, testing, documentation, distribution)
- Sales team sells the full platform to executives for org-wide deployment
- Competes with a wide range of individual tools serving specific API lifecycle purposes
- Point of view: APIs are critical; siloed tools lead to buggy, poorly documented, hard-to-consume APIs. Need 'API-first' mindset.

BEFORE (Feature Walkthrough):
1. Log in
2. Show development and testing features
3. Show documentation features
4. Show collaboration features
5. Show how to organize APIs
6. Show governance features
7. Show integrations
8. Continue — there are a LOT of features
PROBLEM: Executive pushback = 'We already produce APIs and have tools. Why rip all that out?' Plus confusion: Is Postman like an API gateway? What gets replaced? How to measure ROI?

AFTER (Positioning-Oriented Pitch):
1. INSIGHT: 'APIs are a critical way applications interact. Low-quality and difficult-to-consume APIs put customer experience and revenue at risk. Companies must quickly deliver high-quality APIs that are easy to consume.'
2. ALTERNATIVES: 'Today APIs are built, managed, and distributed using a siloed set of tools, each used by separate teams across the API lifecycle. This allows teams to choose task-specific tools. However, this siloed approach makes it impossible to govern API quality across the lifecycle — leading to badly designed, inconsistent, poorly documented APIs that are difficult to use and understand.'
3. PERFECT WORLD: 'If we believe APIs are important to our business, we need an API-first approach that would:
   a. Provide an integrated set of tools across the entire lifecycle to improve API quality
   b. Allow teams to efficiently collaborate to solve problems quickly
   c. Provide a repository for APIs with documentation, test cases, and everything consumers need
   Would you agree with this?'
4. FOLLOW-THROUGH:
   a. Show the integrated toolset — including what gets replaced and what Postman integrates with. Show governance for higher-quality APIs.
   b. Show collaboration and how it lets teams ship faster.
   c. Show collections and the experience for API consumers.
   d. Case study examples calling out ROI achieved and business impact.
