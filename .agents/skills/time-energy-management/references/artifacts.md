## Frameworks

### $10 Game for Individual Priorities (Megan Cook)
An exercise to visualize and align on how an individual is spending their time across priorities.

How it works: List all individual priorities for the week/month. Manager and report divide a hypothetical $10 across the list based on actual time/effort spent. Helps identify if critical tasks are only getting '10 cents' (e.g., 20 minutes) of attention.

### 20-Minute Focus Timer (Gergely)
A tactic to overcome the friction of starting a task by committing to just 20 minutes of zero-distraction work.

How it works: Set a timer for 20 minutes. Block all distractions. The first 5 minutes will be uncomfortable, but it forces a flow state that usually carries through the rest of the session.

### 5 Life Principles (Joe Hudson)
A method for creating a personal operating system to automate difficult decisions.

How it works: 1. Keep the list to exactly 5 principles. 2. Define what each principle is AND what it isn't. 3. Test each principle for 5 days to see if it works for you. 4. Refine until you are 100% confident they will create the life you want. Example: 'Embrace intensity'.

### 5-Step IFS Parts-Work Process for Workplace Overwhelm (Why no productivity hack will solve your overwhelm)
A structured 5-step process adapted from Internal Family Systems therapy to identify and resolve inner conflicts that block productivity and decision-making at work

How it works: Step 1: SENSE AND NAME THE COMPETING PARTS
- Bring a challenge to mind and inquire how you're feeling about it
- Notice what's happening in your body — parts reveal themselves as thoughts, images, words, visuals, sensations, and emotions
- Slow down so you can track what's happening (fast thinkers especially tend to go quickly over their own experience)
- Imagine giving voice or a microphone to different sensations. If this sensation had words, what would it say?
- Ask yourself how you feel about a part that comes up. If you feel frustrated, annoyed, or judgmental, you've found a different part in tension with the first
- Acknowledging and naming the conflict alone can be transformative

Step 2: VALIDATE THAT EACH PART IS VALUABLE AND WISE
- Recognize each part as equally valuable, wise, and worthy of listening to
- Don't favor one part and shove others aside (parts we ignore get louder and more extreme)
- Bring the conflict to life using names or identities, e.g.:
  • 'The ambitious part of me wants X. The balance part of me wants Y.'
  • 'The warrior in me thinks I should give feedback now. The janitor in me doesn't want to cause drama.'
- Notice which voice is louder or which usually 'wins'
- Imagine each part sitting in your hand while you assess their relative weight
- Validation alone can create a shift without having to change anything

Step 3: PULL THE BUS OVER AND LISTEN
- 'Unblend' — ask each part to step away from the steering wheel so you can understand where it wants to go and why
- Interview (not interrogate) each part one at a time with an imaginary microphone
- Starter questions:
  • 'How are you trying to help me?'
  • 'What's so important about that?'
  • 'If you didn't do that role, what are you worried about happening?'
- Repeat back what you're hearing and check:
  • 'It sounds like your role is [x], and you feel like you have to do that in order for [y bad thing] to not happen. Is that right?'
  • 'Is there more you want me to know?'
- If another part interrupts, pull the bus over again — listen one part at a time

Step 4: FACILITATE INTERNAL DIALOGUE AND PROBLEM-SOLVING
- Have the parts speak to each other (imagine them sitting on the same bus seat or in a circle)
- Rules: no disrespecting each other, no debating right/wrong, no persuading
- Facilitation tips:
  • Help parts recognize shared goals or values
  • Encourage non-zero-sum problem-solving
  • Practice self-compassion — don't beat yourself up about the conflict
- Goal: create an internal environment where all parts feel heard and can work together

Step 5: DECIDE ON AND COMMIT TO THE DESTINATION AND ROUTE
- Imagine different parts sitting around a table
- Ask each part: 'What do you need from me or any of these other parts in order to make decision [x]?'
- Gather input, then make a call (same as PM decision-making: gather input, synthesize, decide)
- Follow up with parts on the decision — some may feel betrayed, anxious, or angry
- Clarify how and why you made the decision
- Don't burn your internal team by asking for input and never following up

### 80/20 Toxin Mitigation Approach (A builder’s guide to living a long and healthy life)
A prioritization framework for reducing environmental toxin exposure without trying to eliminate 100% of toxins.

How it works: Cut out toxins in the things you're regularly exposed to (the 80%), and use detox tools (like sauna, or blood cleaning approaches like Inuspheresis/Proxima) to attack the remaining 20%. Focus areas in order:
1. Home environment
2. Office environment
3. Kitchen
Prioritize: air (most microplastics are inhaled), water (PFAS/glyphosate contamination), food sourcing (organic/local/seasonal), and removing plastics from your kitchen.

### 80/20 of Sleep Health (A builder’s guide to living a long and healthy life)
Justin Mares' three-part framework for the most impactful sleep improvements.

How it works: The 80/20 of sleep health boils down to three things:
1. Exercise during the day
2. Get morning sunlight
3. Optimize your sleep setup (no external light, quiet, low CO2, cold) as much as possible

### Agency = Autonomy + Efficacy (Essential reading for product builders—part 1)
Henrik Karlsson's framework for understanding personal agency as the combination of two dispositions: autonomy (formulating your own goals regardless of social expectations) and efficacy (the drive and ability to pursue those goals and solve problems).

How it works: Two components of agency:
1. Autonomy: The capacity to formulate autonomous goals—to dig inside and figure out what wants to happen through you, no matter how strange or wrong it seems to others.
2. Efficacy: The ability and willingness to pursue those goals—the 'will to know,' the drive to see reality as it is so you can manipulate it deftly, instead of fooling yourself that certain problems are unsolvable.

The opposite of agency (two failure modes):
1. Doing what you're 'supposed to do'—playing social games that don't align with what seems valuable to you on reflection.
2. Being passive or ineffective—assuming problems can't be solved, that someone else should solve them, or working on things that don't meaningfully address the problem.

Key insight: Agency is often gentle, not hard-edged—an attunement to the world and self, finding the path of least resistance. Force may be involved, but it's like the force of a river being pulled toward the sea.

### Always vs. Sometimes Rules (Alex Komoroske)
A productivity and self-control heuristic.

How it works: Create black-and-white 'always' rules (e.g., 'I will not have dessert unless socially awkward') rather than 'sometimes' rules, which require constant decision-making and are easily broken.

### Anti-Inflammatory Diet Framework (How a traumatic brain injury made me a better PM—and person)
A dietary framework for reducing chronic inflammation, applicable to sustained professional performance

How it works: Favored foods: Fruits, vegetables, whole grains, lean protein, healthy fats
Discouraged foods: Processed foods, added sugar, red meats, alcohol

Rationale: Chronic inflammation (common byproduct of modern diet/lifestyle) is associated with heart disease, diabetes, cancer, arthritis, and bowel diseases.

Approach:
- Strict adherence during high-stress or recovery periods
- Sustainable 80/20 approach for long-term maintenance (occasional treats are fine)
- Key principle: Follow a diet that's sustainable over the long run

### Arbitrary deadline technique (Part 2: Time management techniques that actually work)
A technique for creating urgency on important-but-not-urgent work by collaboratively setting a made-up deadline

How it works: Steps:
1. Identify a waiting-for item that's been lagging.
2. Approach the person and explicitly say: 'Let's come up with an arbitrary deadline!'
3. Have the person doing the work pick the deadline (not you).
4. Both parties acknowledge it's made up, but commit to it.

Why it works: There's a 'weird gravitational pull' once you've put a date out there. People are surprisingly reasonable with self-imposed deadlines and surprisingly likely to hit them. The transparency about it being arbitrary removes pressure while the commitment creates accountability.

### Autonomic Nervous System (ANS) Framework for Stress Management (How a traumatic brain injury made me a better PM—and person)
A primer on the sympathetic (fight-or-flight) vs. parasympathetic (rest-and-digest) nervous system, and how chronic stress leads to sympathetic dominance. The core principle: stimulate the parasympathetic response to reduce baseline stress.

How it works: Two parts of the ANS:
1. Sympathetic nervous system: 'Fight or flight' — ramps up body's response to perceived threat, keeps you on edge
2. Parasympathetic nervous system: 'Rest and digest' — relaxes body, conserves energy, promotes digestion

In a healthy ANS, these systems are in flux and balance each other. Chronic stress causes sympathetic dominance (fight-or-flight stuck in overdrive), leading to: excessive anxiety, insomnia, digestive issues, higher cortisol.

Solution: Stimulate the parasympathetic rest-and-digest system through meditation, deep breathing, calming music, and dedicated relaxation spaces. This reduces baseline stress and helps you remain calm in high-stakes situations.

### Barbell Media Diet (Marc Andreessen)
A strategy for consuming information that avoids the noise of traditional media.

How it works: Consume only two extremes: 1) Up-to-the-minute, direct-from-practitioner content (podcasts, Substack) and 2) Timeless books written 50+ years ago. Avoid daily/weekly news and magazines which are often inaccurate and quickly outdated.

### Barbell Reading Strategy (Essential reading for product builders—part 1)
Marc Andreessen's approach to filtering information inputs by only reading things that are either completely current or completely timeless, skipping everything in between.

How it works: The strategy: 'Barbell' your inputs.
- Read things that are up to this minute (breaking, current).
- Read things that are timeless (decades-old essays, enduring wisdom).
- Do NOT read anything from 'yesterday through to like 10 years ago'—this is the noise zone.
- Purpose: Separate 'this sounds so smart!' from 'this is genuinely correct, helpful, and timeless.'

### Bottom-Up vs. Top-Down Coverage Approach (How to create an exceptional coverage plan for your parental leave)
Two distinct approaches to structuring a coverage plan depending on whether you're an individual contributor or a people manager

How it works: **Option 1: Bottom-Up Approach (best for ICs)**
- Projects: Create a prioritized list of every project. Include projects that might come up (annual/quarterly planning, feature releases). Add major outcomes needed, upcoming milestones, key documents, and success criteria. Assign one DRI per project (consider XFN partners like EMs and designers). Align with each DRI on specific responsibilities.
- Upcoming Meetings: Document all recurring meetings (excluding 1:1s). For meetings you lead: find someone already in the meeting to cover, share how you run it (outcomes, agenda owner). For meetings you attend: decide if a delegate is needed or if you can skip it.
- Other: Budget approvals, recurring newsletters, any other responsibilities.

**Option 2: Top-Down Approach (best for managers)**
- Area DRIs: Create 3-5 major focus areas. Assign one DRI per area from direct reports or peers. Provide context on specific projects, meetings, workstreams. Give each DRI full autonomy for new decisions in their area.
- De facto proxy (optional): Designate one person as 'Interim Product Lead' — primary point of contact for your manager and team. Ideally a senior team member. Responsibilities: set goals for team, serve as interim manager, liaise with executive team, final decision-making authority.

### Burnout Correlation Map (How tech workers really feel about work right now)
Shows what burnout correlates with across multiple dimensions of work life

How it works: Burnout correlates with:
- Career pessimism: 60% of extremely pessimistic respondents report high burnout vs only 8% of extremely optimistic respondents
- Quitting intentions: 67.8% of workers actively exploring or considering quitting are highly burned out
- Low engagement: Close to 30% of not-engaged workers are completely burned out

Burnout varies by:
- Company size: Higher at larger companies, dramatic spike at 500-1,000 employees
- Employment type: Full-time employees have nearly 2x the burnout rate of self-employed/contractors
- Industry: Hardware and infra highest burnout; emerging tech (AI) and specialized sectors (fintech, healthtech) lower
- Role: Founders and engineers have lower burnout than other roles
- Gender: Women ~3% higher burnout
- Overall: 44.67% of all respondents experiencing significant burnout (moderately, very, or completely burned out)

### Daily Task Grooming System (Geoff Charles)
A daily routine for managers in back-to-back meetings to process tasks and free up mental memory for strategic thinking.

How it works: Steps: 1) End of every meeting, write down tasks you owe and tasks others owe you with clear deadlines. 2) End of day, group tasks logically (tactical vs strategic). 3) Slack others what they owe with a reminder. 4) Create the next day's calendar aligned to these grouped tasks.

### Daily pen-and-paper to-do rewrite system (Part 2: Time management techniques that actually work)
A physical notebook-based to-do system where you rewrite tasks each morning on a fresh page, forcing daily reprioritization

How it works: Process:
1. Use a physical notebook (not an app) for all to-dos.
2. Each morning, tear out the previous day's page.
3. Transfer anything not done but still a priority to a fresh page.
4. Rethink your top 1-3 priorities for the day.
5. Remove items that have been transferred for too many days (they're probably not important).
6. Cross out tasks physically as you complete them throughout the day.

Why it works: Digital to-do apps become avoidance zones. A physical notebook is always visible and can't be swiped away. Rewriting forces active reprioritization rather than passive accumulation. Physical crossing out is more satisfying than checking a digital checkbox.

### Dance Floor vs. Balcony Metaphor (Rachel Lockett)
A mental model for stepping out of day-to-day execution to reflect on how things are going—applicable to co-founder relationships, executive teams, and personal career management.

How it works: Instead of being on the dance floor (in the hustle of running the business), periodically get on the balcony (step back to observe the bigger picture). Applied to: Co-founders—quarterly check-ins to assess relationship health. Executive teams—quarterly reflections on strategy and culture. Personal—creating space to tinker, reflect, and assess if you're in your gifts. Key insight: The urgency of daily work will always crowd out reflection unless you deliberately schedule balcony time.

### Deep Work vs. Shallow Work (Using your working time well - Issue 22)
Cal Newport's framework distinguishing between cognitively demanding, value-creating work and logistical, easy-to-replicate tasks

How it works: Shallow Work: Non-cognitively demanding, logistical-style tasks, often performed while distracted. These efforts tend not to create much new value in the world and are easy to replicate. Examples for PMs: answering emails, moving JIRA tasks, going to endless meetings.

Deep Work: Professional activities performed in a state of distraction-free concentration that push your cognitive capabilities to their limit. These efforts create new value, improve your skill, and are hard to replicate.

### Eat the Frog (Time management techniques that actually work)
Do the hardest, most dreaded task on your list first thing in the morning

How it works: "If it's your job to eat a frog, it's best to do it first thing in the morning. And if it's your job to eat two frogs, it's best to eat the biggest one first." Applied to productivity: identify the hardest/most important task on your 1-3 daily must-dos and tackle it first while your energy and willpower are highest.

### Eisenhower Matrix (My favorite decision-making frameworks, Using your working time well - Issue 22)
A 2x2 prioritization grid that maps tasks by urgency and importance to determine what to do first, schedule, delegate, or eliminate.

How it works: 2x2 grid with four quadrants:

Quadrant 1 (Urgent + Important): DO FIRST — Tasks that need immediate attention and have significant consequences
Quadrant 2 (Not Urgent + Important): SCHEDULE — Tasks that matter but don't need immediate action; plan time for these
Quadrant 3 (Urgent + Not Important): DELEGATE — Tasks that need to get done soon but can be handled by someone else
Quadrant 4 (Not Urgent + Not Important): ELIMINATE — Tasks that are neither urgent nor important; remove them

Best used for: Prioritizing tasks, projects, or investments.

### Feather, Brick, Dump Truck Phenomenon (Jonny Miller)
A mental model for recognizing the escalating warning signs of burnout.

How it works: Feather: subtle signs like waking up slightly tired. Brick: noticeable impacts like losing your temper or having an argument. Dump truck: full-blown crisis like a health failure or getting fired. The goal is to notice the feather and adjust.

### Five Strategies for Saying No (On saying no)
Tactical approaches for declining requests without spending excessive time or damaging relationships.

How it works: 1. Just don't reply: No reply feels better than a half-hearted reply. People assume you didn't see it or it's not a fit.
2. Pivot to email: When someone asks for a call/meeting, ask to keep the conversation to email. Saves time and gets to the root of the ask faster.
3. Create a policy and communicate it: Create blanket rules like 'I don't do talks/podcasts/events' or 'No CEOs/founders/VCs on the podcast' or 'I'm not taking on new projects until [Date].' When you tell people it's your policy, they understand and move on.
4. Block deep work time and never schedule over it: E.g., no meetings before 3 p.m. This creates focus time AND limits remaining hours, forcing more no's.
5. Ask to check back later: Tell the person to ping you again in 1-3 months. Over half the time they'll never follow up. Problem solved.

### Functional Workaholism (Three myths about sabbatical programs)
A concept describing high-performing leaders who remain productive while rapidly depleting personal resources, analogous to functional alcoholism — a precursor to burnout

How it works: Definition: Like functional alcoholics, functional workaholics manage to remain highly productive despite rapidly depleting personal resources. They're so busy they forget to take care of themselves, leaving PTO on the table and neglecting to disconnect outside of work hours. After enough time, this becomes a self-destructive way of life.

Key characteristics:
- You don't realize it until you've stepped away for enough time
- Research finding: Takes 6-8 weeks to fully disconnect from work life and fully feel yourself again
- Once recognized, people typically make big changes for themselves and their company

Contagion effect (the 'Work Devotion Norm'):
- Functional workaholism is contagious, especially top-down from leaders
- The 'work devotion norm' demands employees make work the sole focus of their lives — or else
- Studies: Managers in financial services who took family leave experienced fewer promotions and smaller salary increases vs. those who didn't
- Counter-finding: Studies on extended leave show people are more likely to take advantage of policies if their managers do

Implication: Leaders must take sabbaticals themselves to give permission to their teams.

### HIIT Recovery Exercise Protocol (How a traumatic brain injury made me a better PM—and person)
Heart-rate-based high-intensity interval training protocol for brain health and parasympathetic activation

How it works: Protocol:
- 1-minute ON intervals: Get heart rate up to a target range
- 1-minute OFF intervals: Rest and focus on deep breathing to bring heart rate down
- Equipment: Stationary bike (or other low-impact cardio)

Benefits:
- Short bursts of intense cardio increase brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF/neuroplasticity)
- Rapidly bringing down heart rate during off-intervals stimulates the parasympathetic nervous system

Quote from Dr. Peter Attia: 'Exercise is the single most potent tool we have in the health-span-enhancing toolkit—and that includes nutrition, sleep, and meds.'

Progression: Work with a physical therapist to slowly increase the maximum heart rate at which you can exercise without causing issues. Gradually reintroduce weightlifting alongside cardio.

### Hormetic Career Response (How to know when to stop)
The concept of strategic, conscious periods of intolerable stress that can improve your tolerance and performance, borrowed from the biology concept of hormesis.

How it works: From biology: hormesis means organisms develop adaptive mechanisms when put under a REASONABLE amount of stress, potentially improving tolerance or performance. Applied to careers: there are times when it makes sense to seek situations that produce a 'hormetic career response' - promotions or new roles requiring a step up in effort and responsibilities. Key principle: you should CONSCIOUSLY DECIDE when and why a wander into the intolerable makes sense, rather than drifting into it by default. Must be time-bounded - push hard, then recover. Andy's example: three deliberate acceleration phases over 17 years, each followed by 6+ months of rest and recovery.

### Identity Workspace / Liminal Space Model (On taking time off)
Academic concept explaining why sabbaticals work — they create a safe transitional space for identity exploration

How it works: Sabbaticals create a safe 'liminal space' for transitioning across identities. This 'identity workspace' — like a yoga room or dojo for personal development — allows people to reflect upon and experiment with their lives. Example: one interviewee spent several months working at an ecolodge in Hawaii to test if it was an attractive pre-retirement goal (it wasn't). The key insight is that it takes a long time (6+ weeks) to disentangle from work identity before this workspace becomes accessible.

### LNO Framework (EOY Review, Shreyas Doshi)
A task prioritization matrix based on the return on investment of effort.

How it works: L (Leverage) tasks: 10-100X return. Do these when you're most energetic, let your perfectionist shine. Examples: PRD for important feature, strategic alignment between teams, critical meeting notes. N (Neutral) tasks: ~1X return. Do these adequately but don't over-invest. O (Overhead) tasks: Less than 1X return. Minimize time spent. Examples: expense reports, routine bug reports, standard meeting notes. Key insight: The same type of activity (bug report, meeting notes) can be L, N, or O depending on context.

### Lazy Leadership (Andrew Wilkinson)
A management philosophy focused on delegating tasks you hate.

How it works: The goal is to be 'Teflon for tasks.' Scale the business quickly enough so you can hire employees to do the operational work you dislike, allowing you to focus only on your unique strengths.

### Leave Availability Options (How to create an exceptional coverage plan for your parental leave)
Two options for setting your availability during parental leave, with a clear preferred recommendation

How it works: **Option 1 [Preferred]: Not Available**
- Only your manager has your personal phone number and email
- Align up front with manager on types of things to be proactively notified about: performance reviews, org changes, changes to team scope and responsibilities
- For everyone else: 'I won't be checking Slack or email. If you need to reach me, reach out to my manager and they'll contact me if needed.'
- Most people probably won't reach out to your manager

**Option 2: Available Upon Request**
- Share contact methods in preference order: cell, work email, personal email
- Assume people probably won't bug you, but they might

**Key rules:**
- Stick to whatever you set — inconsistency confuses the team
- DRIs won't be empowered or motivated to cover if they think you're back early
- Don't feel guilty about being unavailable — that's the whole point of the coverage plan

### Lenny's 10 Time Management Techniques (Time management techniques that actually work)
A curated list of 10 daily-use productivity techniques that have stuck with Lenny over years of testing dozens of approaches

How it works: 1. Use your calendar for to-dos — Slot tasks into calendar events instead of a to-do app. Move them if plans change. Plan your week on Sunday evening.
2. Two-minute rule — If a task takes less than two minutes, do it immediately instead of adding it to your list. (From David Allen's Getting Things Done.)
3. Keep a 'waiting for' list — Whenever you delegate or ask someone for something, add it to a dedicated 'waiting for' list alongside your to-do list. (From Getting Things Done.)
4. Write down 1-3 must-dos each morning — Before opening your to-do list, identify 1-3 items that would make it a great day. Do the hardest first ('eat the frog'). (Inspired by Matt Mochary's Top Goal and Make Time's 'highlight'.)
5. Protect deep work time — Create 2-3 recurring calendar blocks per week for focused work. Kill distracting apps. Use binaural beats (Brain.fm). Push for company no-meeting days. Use Google Calendar Focus Time. (From Cal Newport's Deep Work.)
6. No meetings before [X] — Set a rule: no meetings before a set time (e.g., 10 a.m., 11 a.m., or even 3 p.m.). Use morning hours for creative/generative work.
7. Keep Do Not Disturb on — Leave Do Not Disturb mode on across all devices by default. For extra focus, put phone in another room and close Slack/Messages/X.
8. Keep work async — Default to email/async over meetings. Use a reply template to redirect meeting invites. Recognize the hidden cost of pre- and post-meeting time.
9. Get a virtual assistant — Use the LNO framework to identify low-impact tasks, then delegate to a virtual EA (e.g., Double, Athena, Fancy Hands). Expect to scale from near-zero to 20-30 hours/week.
10. Say no more often — Filter asks through your top 2-3 priorities. Communicate tradeoffs to your manager. Remove emotion from the decision. Apply the 'would I do this tomorrow?' test.

### Lenny's Five Strategies for PM Time Management (Using your working time well - Issue 22)
A five-part framework for making the most of your working time as a PM

How it works: 1. Block off deep work time (and defend it with all your might) — Schedule two-hour blocks of DEEP WORK TIME on Mon/Wed/Fri. Make it crystal clear to everyone not to book over these slots.

### P0/P1/P2 Daily Task Prioritization System (How a traumatic brain injury made me a better PM—and person)
A three-tier priority system for daily and weekly tasks, combined with a daily work-hour limit to force better prioritization

How it works: Tiers:
- P0: Must get done today before clocking off. Limit to 2 items per day.
- P1: Try your best to get through on any given day, but can slip if needed.
- P2: Stretch items you'll only get to if schedule permits.

Diagnosing stuck P2s: If a P2 task keeps getting pushed back day after day, investigate:
- Option A: Realize the task is very low-ROI → cut it entirely
- Option B: Realize aversion/anxiety is the real reason for delay (e.g., aligning with a difficult stakeholder) → elevate to P0 to force yourself to get it done

Additional tactic: Set a daily work-hour limit (e.g., ~2 hours less than your normal baseline). Shorter hours raise the bar for what's worthy of your time, naturally leading to better prioritization.

Cites Parkinson's Law: Work expands to fill time available; conversely, work shrinks when given less time.

### Personal Objective Function (John Mark Nickels)
A mental model for evaluating life and career choices based on long-term outcomes rather than short-term optics.

How it works: Evaluate Sunday night choices (e.g., working on a presentation vs. playing with kids) by asking what 'future you' in 5 years will care about. Avoid the trap of optimizing for short-term executive approval at the expense of long-term relationships.

### Placebo Productivity (Shreyas Doshi)
A procrastination-busting tactic where you intentionally fill 1-2 days with N and O tasks before tackling a high-leverage L task, creating psychological momentum

How it works: Steps: 1) Identify your L task that you've been procrastinating on. 2) For 1-2 days before the appointed day, intentionally fill your schedule with N and O tasks. 3) Remind yourself these are just 'placebo productivity' tasks. 4) On the appointed day, tackle the L task—ideally from a different physical location (coffee shop, co-working space). The contrast between days of low-leverage work creates psychological pressure and momentum to finally do the important thing.

### Range of Tolerance Framework (3-Part Life Balance System) (How to know when to stop)
A three-part framework for assessing and managing sustainable work-life balance: (1) Define your personal range of tolerance, (2) Pick your career progression, (3) Pick your life progression.

How it works: Part 1 - Define your range of tolerance: Take the stress response inventory, create your tolerance list (intolerables, boundaries, flourish items), and visualize it as a tolerance target diagram. Part 2 - Pick your career progression: Map your career on the S-curve with three phases: Foundation (skills building, IC work), Acceleration (climbing ladder, managing people and projects), Peak (managing organizations and escalating problems). Account for both 'office hours' (butt-in-seat work) and 'mind hours' (nervous system preoccupied with work stress, estimated 10-30 additional hours/week at executive level). Part 3 - Pick your life progression: Map your life on a parallel S-curve: Childhood (mystery/exploration), Acceleration (major expenses, life precariousness - cars, homes, kids, health issues), Peak (physical decline, legacy). After five intense years of creative output and promotion, interviews about personal journeys and where ideas come from, after years of wrapping up one project one day and jumping right into promoting another the next … I'm taking a step back. I recently maxed out pretty hard, and for the benefit of my work, I've got to take a break. Over the past month, I've canceled contracts and said no to new projects and interviews. I've started creating more space to explore and doodle again, to sit and do nothing, to wander and waste a day.

And, for the first time in five years, I'm finally in a place where there's no due date tied to every drawing, no deadline for ideas. And it feels really right. So, while I really wanted to do this with you, I respect you and your work, and I'm honored that you'd ask me to participate. And as capital S stupid as it is for me professionally not to do it, I'm going to have to say thank you, but … I've got to pass.

I'm simply not in a place to talk about myself or my work right now. Crazy for a highly verbal, only child to say. Hopefully, we will get a chance to talk somewhere down the line. I promise any thoughts I'll have for you then will be far more insightful than anything I could share with you right now. I hope the space created by my absence is filled by one of the brilliant people I suggested in my previous email. And really, thank you so much for your interest. I'll be kicking myself when the book comes out.

Wendy.

Key technique: Radical honesty about burnout, frames saying no as a creative necessity, acknowledges the professional cost, leaves door open for the future.

## Tools

### Calendar Energy Audit (Nickey Skarstad)
A method for evaluating job satisfaction by color-coding calendar events based on the energy they provide.

How it works: Review your calendar over the last few weeks. Mark meetings Green if they gave you energy/excitement, Yellow if they were baseline/fine, and Red if you were bored or stressed. Use the ratio to determine if a role is right for you.

### Concentration Crown (Nir Eyal)
A physical wearable item used to signal to family members that you are doing focused work and cannot be interrupted.

How it works: A light-up crown worn by a parent. The rule for kids: 'Unless you are bleeding, do not interrupt when the crown is on. I will be with you in 30 minutes or less.'

### Custom Host File Blocker (Gergely)
A Python script used to override the computer's host file to block distracting websites during focus blocks.

How it works: Write a script that alters the computer's host file to block sites like Twitter and LinkedIn. The friction of having to run a secondary script to unblock the sites prevents mindless browsing.

### Elena's AI Tech Stack (Elena Verna 4.0)
Specific AI tools used by Elena Verna for daily productivity.

How it works: Lovable (prototyping and internal tools), ChatGPT Deep Thinking Mode (brainstorming), Granola (AI meeting summaries), Wispr Flow (voice-to-text dictation).

### Indistractable Monitor Sign (Nir Eyal)
A physical cardstock sign placed on a computer monitor to deter colleagues from interrupting.

How it works: A red sign that reads: 'I'm indistractable at the moment. Please come back later.' Used to establish a cultural norm of focused work.

### NSDR (Non-Sleep Deep Rest) (Jonny Miller)
A guided audio practice to deeply relax the nervous system and recover energy.

How it works: Lie down with an eye mask and listen to a 14-20 minute guided body scan. Best used between 1 PM and 3 PM to get a second wind and train the body's capacity to downshift.

### Physical Fidgets for Zoom Fatigue (Donna Lichaw)
Physical objects used during video meetings to provide the nervous system with missing physical stimuli.

How it works: Use pointy fidgets in the morning to generate energy, and squishy fidgets in the afternoon to ground yourself and close the neurological circuitry that video calls leave open.

### Recommended Reading List for Productivity (Using your working time well - Issue 22)
Curated list of books, methods, and resources for diving deeper into time management and productivity

How it works: 1. Deep Work by Cal Newport — The foundational book on distraction-free focused work. Lenny calls it 'one of the most important professional development books I've ever read.'

5. Eat That Frog! by Brian Tracy — A book about overcoming procrastination by tackling your hardest task first.
