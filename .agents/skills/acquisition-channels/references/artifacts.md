## Frameworks

### 7 Types of Turbo Boosts (60 ideas to boost your growth)
A taxonomy of seven common strategies to get a burst of attention for your product, each with multiple real-world examples

How it works: Seven turbo boost strategies:
1. Create a viral video — A video that tells your story, demos your product, or delights people so much that they can't help but share it
2. Launch a mini-product, or 'drop' — A non-core product offering that gets attention, which reflects attention toward your brand and/or core product
3. Run an enticing limited-time offer — Running a promotion on your existing product, giving people a reason to pay attention and act
4. Coordinate an influencer-led promotion — Partner with an influencer to promote your product
5. Co-marketing — Collaborate with another company to promote your products
6. Organize an exciting offline experience — Organize an in-person experience that gets people fired up about your product
7. Pick a fight — Take a stand against a big company or competitor, which creates controversy and attention

### 7 Ways People Discover New Products (How people discover new products)
A comprehensive taxonomy of all the channels through which people become aware of new products, organized by context (online vs. offline, organic vs. paid, social vs. individual)

How it works: The 7 channels are:
1. Friends or colleagues tell you about it (e.g., invite, referral program, organic WOM)
2. You come across it organically while browsing online (e.g., SEO, social media, press, influencers, forums, Wirecutter)
3. You come across a promotion while browsing online (e.g., Facebook ads, Google ads, paid influencers, affiliates, channel partners)
4. You come across it organically while out and about (e.g., store shelf, seeing product in the wild, at a friend's home)
5. You come across a promotion while out and about (e.g., billboards, bus ads, taxi ads, flyers)
6. You come across a promotion while at home (e.g., TV ads, direct mail, podcast ads, radio, newspaper/magazine ads, packaging inserts)
7. Someone reaches out to you to tell you about it (e.g., outbound sales via phone, email, DM, LinkedIn, door-to-door)

### 7Ps of Community Program Design (A founder’s guide to community)
A framework for designing any community program with seven fundamental elements that should be defined before launch

How it works: Seven elements to define for every community program:
1. **People:** Who the program is focused on — define the target audience and their characteristics
2. **Purpose:** Why they need this program — the value proposition for members
3. **Place:** Where members will gather — physical location, online platform, etc.
4. **Participation:** What members will do — the activities and interactions expected
5. **Policy:** Guidelines and rules that will shape the experience — values, code of conduct
6. **Promotion:** How members will learn about the program — marketing and outreach channels
7. **Performance:** What success looks like — metrics and evaluation criteria

Revisit the 7Ps every 3-6 months to evolve the experience.

### Attribution Investment Goldilocks Principle (Yuriy Timen)
Two failure modes of attribution investment: under-investing (inertia spending) vs. over-investing (premature perfection-seeking), must match stage

How it works: Under-investment trap: Budgets scale up, broad channel portfolio, can't determine what works. Leads to inertia: 'Company's been growing roughly over the same time we increased spend, scared to break it, keep spending.' Over-investment trap: Reading horror stories, trying to get everything scientifically pure, but payoff doesn't justify the investment at current stage. Right level depends on: stage, budget size, channel complexity, what you stand to gain from better attribution.

### Blended vs. Channel-Specific Payback Decision Framework (What is a good payback period?)
A decision framework for determining whether to track blended or per-channel payback periods

How it works: Decision rule: If your growth is primarily organic → blended payback is fine (ideal payback = 0 because it's all organic, like Tinder). If paid is a significant growth channel → report payback on paid CAC separately, not blended. Reasons: (1) Most businesses either stay organic-only or come to rely heavily on paid, so blended is 'somewhat useless'—it either doesn't matter (organic-only) or converges to paid payback anyway. (2) If paid is a minority of growth, blended metrics disguise paid inefficiency behind free organic growth. (3) Teams should understand how customer value and retention differ for paid vs. organic cohorts. Starting point: segment paid vs. organic cohorts, then get granular into individual channel payback over time.

### Booking.com Performance Marketing Diagnostic Process (Top 5 most interesting things about Booking.com's early growth strategy – Issue 46)
A sequential diagnostic framework used by Booking.com's performance marketing team to identify why they were losing in paid ad auctions and what the supply team needed to fix

How it works: Sequential diagnostic process:
1. Do we have demand? (Are customers searching for this?)
2. Do we have inventory? (Do we have hotels in this market?)
3. Is it the right inventory? (Does it match what searchers want?)
4. Do we have availability? (Can people actually book for their dates?)
5. Do we have conversion? (Are visitors completing bookings?)

If losing on supply, diagnose further:
- Different supply needed?
- Cheaper supply needed?
- Different rank order needed?
- Better product content needed?
- Availability at other dates needed?
- Something else?

Key principle: Never assume competitors are being irrational. If someone else is winning, they are doing something better. Nothing is sacred.

### Click Share vs Impression Share Analysis (Timothy Davis)
A visualization comparing impression share (showing to everyone) vs click share (showing to the right person) over time to measure targeting effectiveness

How it works: Principle: Instead of optimizing for impression share (ego marketing / being #1 always), optimize for click share (serving the right user). How: Leverage user data — demographics, locations, in-market audiences, different bid strategies. Visualization: Plot impression share and click share over time. Success = click share increasing even if impression share stays flat. Use as test vs control comparison across campaigns. Verify with conversion rate and conversion volume.

### Community Scaling Formula (2x Multiplier) (A founder’s guide to community)
A gradual scaling approach for growing community membership after founding members phase

How it works: After founding members seed content for 1-2 weeks, scale membership using a 2x multiplier every two weeks:
10 → 20 → 40 → 80 → 160

Rationale: Don't overwhelm existing community. New members should feel part of a curated group, not lost in noise. Like opening rooms at a club — keep everyone in one room so it feels full, then open more rooms as it fills.

### Content Goal Alignment Framework (Content-driven growth)
A framework for deciding the primary goal of your content investment before committing resources, from HubSpot's Kieran Flanagan

How it works: Before investing in content, be intentional about which goal your content will serve:

1. Drive demand (generate leads/customers): Most common initial goal. Model available search traffic and conversion rates to estimate demand TAM. Example: 'Blogging could equate to 20% of the demand we need to generate.'

2. Build influence (shape audience perception): Create original stories, case studies, business trends. Measured by audience size and engagement. Example: Podcast with a large following to influence your audience.

3. Enable sales: Rework top-of-funnel content for sales team use (pitch decks, case studies, customer evidence).

4. Build brand: Create content that establishes thought leadership and is shared via word of mouth.

Process:
- Be intentional about goals so everyone is aligned
- Invest in being great at ONE thing first
- Master it, be 'better or different'
- Then expand to new channels
- Your competition is not just direct competitors — it's every tweet, post, podcast vying for your audience's time

### Content Graph vs. Intent Graph vs. People Graph (Ray Cao)
A framework for understanding how TikTok's ad platform differs from Google (intent graph / search-based) and Meta (people graph / friend-based), requiring different optimization strategies

How it works: Three platform paradigms: (1) Google = Intent Graph: Search-driven, based on user intent signals. (2) Meta = People Graph: Friend/connection-based, distributes to known social connections. (3) TikTok = Content Graph: Content-driven distribution, content shown to anyone based on relevance, not connections. Implication: Same strategies won't work across platforms. TikTok requires more creative testing, broader targeting, and trend-responsive content.

### Content-Driven Growth 2x2 Framework (Content-driven growth)
A 2x2 matrix for mapping all content-driven growth strategies based on two axes: what you're optimizing for (SEO vs. virality) and who's generating the content (users vs. employees). Yields five distinct strategies.

How it works: Axes:
- X-axis: Who generates the content? (Users/Data on left, Employees on right)
- Y-axis: What are you optimizing for? (SEO on top, Virality on bottom)

Five strategies that emerge:

1. UGSO (User-Generated SEO-Optimized) — Top left. Users generate public content that Google surfaces in organic search. Examples: Quora, Glassdoor, Reddit.

2. EGSO (Editorially Generated SEO-Optimized) — Top right. Employees/contractors create content to rank for certain keywords. Examples: Ahrefs, Slidebean, HubSpot.

3. DGSO (Data-Generated SEO-Optimized) — Top center (combination of UGC and Editorial). Company auto-generates thousands of pages from UGC and proprietary data. Examples: Thumbtack, Zapier, Tripadvisor, Yelp, Grubhub.

4. UGVO (User-Generated Virality-Optimized) — Bottom left. UGC leads to users sharing content with friends, pulling them into the product. Examples: TikTok (watermarked videos), Reface app, Airbnb listings, Zillow Zestimate.

5. EGVO (Editorially Generated Virality-Optimized) — Bottom right. Employees/founders create one-off viral content (thought leadership, brand building). Examples: Spotify Unwrapped, Mr. Beast, Stir drops, Superhuman blog posts.

### Coverage and Composition Partner Evaluation (Ecosystem is the next big growth channel)
A two-filter framework borrowed from advertising for evaluating ecosystem partner fit based on audience overlap.

How it works: Two filters for evaluating potential ecosystem partners: 1) Coverage: What percentage of your total target audience does this partner reach? Higher coverage = you can reach more of your TAM through this partner. 2) Composition: What percentage of their audience overlaps with yours? Higher composition = less waste—you aren't reaching people who don't care about your product. Evaluation matrix: - High coverage + High composition = Match made in B2B heaven (if shared values and goals align). Prioritize these partnerships. - High coverage + Low composition = Can still be beneficial but expect more waste; use broader awareness strategies. - Low coverage + High composition = Can still be beneficial for niche targeting; expect smaller but highly qualified reach. - Low coverage + Low composition = Likely not worth pursuing. Always pair this analysis with the win-win-win test.

### Frequency × Resonance = Recall Framework (When and how to run a billboard campaign)
A framework from Kira Klaas (Notion) for evaluating what makes OOH advertising memorable. Two levers drive recall: frequency (how often it's seen) and resonance ('this feels relevant to me'). You need either a lot of placements, a killer message, or a mix of both.

How it works: Frequency × Resonance = Recall.

- Frequency: How often your ad is seen by the same person. Achieved through volume of placements (bus ads, shelters, billboards) in a concentrated geographic area.
- Resonance: How relevant and emotionally impactful the ad feels to the viewer. Achieved through compelling copy, humor, controversy, or emotional connection.
- Recall: The outcome—whether people remember your brand when they're in-market to buy.

Implications:
- A single billboard CAN work, but only if resonance is extremely high (viral-level).
- For most campaigns, aim for 2-7 impressions per person per day in a city.
- Combine high-profile units (big billboards people look up to see) with high-frequency formats (street furniture, transit ads).
- Amplify on digital/social to increase both frequency and resonance.

### Incrementality Adjusted Factor (IAF) (Timothy Davis)
A measurement approach using Conversion Lift or GeoX tests to determine the true incremental value of paid advertising, ideally by region and platform

How it works: Method: Run Conversion Lift test — intentionally don't show ad to some users when you win an auction (control group). Google shows next bidder's ad instead. Measure: Opportunity cost (impression share % holdback, spend holdback). Calculate true incremental lift per platform, ideally per region. Apply IAF to planning and budget allocation. Prerequisites: Minimum ~$50K/month spend per platform. All platforms (Google, Meta, LinkedIn) willing to partner on these tests. Contact your platform rep to set up.

### Kasper's OOH Placement Evaluation Framework (When and how to run a billboard campaign)
An ethnographic approach to evaluating individual OOH ad placements for maximum impact

How it works: Two principles for placement selection:

1. MIX AD TYPES FOR RESONANCE + FREQUENCY
   - High-profile units (units people literally have to look up to see) → drive resonance
   - Wide-footprint formats (street furniture, transit) → deliver frequency
   - While people recall the big unit on sales calls, the smaller formats drive the recall that makes that possible

2. EVALUATE EACH PLACEMENT WITH AN ETHNOGRAPHIC EYE
   Questions to ask during market rides:
   - Is the highway littered with competing billboards to the point of attention blindness?
   - Are newsstands facing the street targeting drivers, or are they better suited for pedestrians 2-4 lanes away?
   - Is the font, contrast, and graphics appropriate for legibility from the actual viewing distance?
   - Are there obstructions (trees, poles, other signage) blocking the view?
   - Use Google Street View AND in-person scouting

### Marketing Measurement Triangulation Framework (How today’s top consumer brands measure marketing’s impact)
A three-method framework for triangulating true marketing performance by combining MTA, MMM, and CLS, with specific roles and cadences for each method

How it works: Three methods used together:

1. MTA (Multi-touch attribution/digital tracking): Use platform reports or analytics to provide directional data for small daily optimizations like turning off poor-performing ads or adjusting campaign settings.

2. MMM (Marketing mix modeling): Use your model to forecast different budget allocations between channels, determining how much you can spend in a channel before it becomes inefficient.

3. CLS (Conversion lift studies): Run lift tests every few months, and use the results to calibrate the accuracy of your MMM. If these two methods agree, you can be more confident making predictions.

Cadences:
- CLS: Once or twice a year minimum, more regular testing as budget gets into millions. For a $1M/year budget with 80% on Google Ads, run lift tests quarterly for Google Ads, annually for smaller channels like TikTok.
- MMM: Updated 2-4 times per month with modern approaches (vs. 1-2x/year with traditional vendors).
- MTA: Daily for tactical optimizations.

Strengths/weaknesses balance:
- CLS gets closest to true incrementality but is expensive/difficult to run frequently
- MMM is easier to get right when compared against CLS results; helps rule out least plausible model configurations
- MTA is used directionally for campaign/tactic-level optimizations since MMM isn't usually that granular

Practical example:
- Last lift test showed Google Ads had incremental cost per signup of $20 → calibrate MMM until it matches for that time period
- MMM estimates TikTok only counts 80% of conversions it generates → when TikTok reports $27 CPA, it's really achieving $22 CPA

### Netflix 'Shifting Through Gears' Partnership Strategy (All the ways to grow your product)
Marc Randolph's metaphor for how startups should progressively escalate their distribution partnerships, starting small and building credibility

How it works: Launching a startup is like getting a huge truck to move — you can't start in high gear.

**First gear:** Make noise in user groups and communities where enthusiasts congregate (small, organic).
**Second gear:** Partner with a willing second-tier player in the industry (e.g. Toshiba was #2 behind Sony and more willing to take a chance on an unproven startup). Place coupons/offers in their products.
**Third gear:** Bring on additional partners, including the market leader (e.g. Panasonic, Sharp, and eventually Sony). Achieve broad distribution.
**Result:** Watch numbers slowly accelerate from single thousands to tens of thousands. Use steady flow of customers to experiment on while finding repeatable, scalable business model.

### Payback Period over CAC/LTV (Sri Batchu)
Using contribution margin payback period instead of CAC or LTV-to-CAC as the primary efficiency metric for growth investments

How it works: Problems with alternatives: CAC ignores value (may attract cheaper but less valuable customers), LTV-to-CAC is too assumption-laden (like a DCF). Formula: Payback Period = Customer Acquisition Cost ÷ Monthly Contribution Margin. Example: $5,000 CAC ÷ $500/month contribution margin = 10-month payback. Board/exec sets target payback period, teams orient around driving blended payback period down. Contribution margin = revenue minus all variable costs (production, support, etc.).

### SPACES Model for Community Objectives (A founder’s guide to community)
A framework for identifying which business objectives a community can drive, with each letter representing a different objective. Used to align community investment to business goals.

How it works: Six community objectives:
1. **Support:** Create spaces for customers to answer questions and solve problems for each other. Example: Autodesk support community. Metrics: ticket deflection, time to resolution, cost savings.
2. **Product:** Create spaces for customers to share product feedback and ideas with each other and with your team. Example: Atlassian feedback section. Metrics: ideas submitted, feedback quality, feature adoption.
3. **Acquisition:** Build programs that help you grow your pipeline and customer base. Example: Branch's Mobile Growth community. Metrics: leads sourced, leads influenced, conversion rates.
4. **Contribution:** Enable members to contribute content, services, or something else of value to a platform you create. Example: Notion Template Gallery. Metrics: contributions, contributor retention.
5. **Engagement:** Connect customers to each other around their common interests in order to increase customer retention. Example: Culture Amp's Culture First. Metrics: retention rate, customer lifetime value.
6. **Success:** Enable customers to teach each other how to better use your product and be more successful in their careers. Example: Salesforce's Trailblazer program. Metrics: product adoption, certification completions.

Mapping to company stage:
- Pre-PMF: Focus on Product (feedback/insights)
- Growth: Focus on Acquisition and Engagement
- Maturity: Focus on Support and Success
- Platform businesses: Focus on Contribution from day one

### Time to Contribution Margin Break-Even (DoorDash's Paid Growth Framework) (Accelerating Growth at Scale 🔥 Phase 2 of Kickstarting and Scaling a Marketplace Business)
DoorDash's approach to evaluating paid marketing investments by measuring how quickly customer acquisition costs are recouped through contribution profit, rather than relying on long-term LTV projections

How it works: Framework: Instead of investing based on LTV (lifetime value), invest based on 'Time to Contribution Margin Break-Even.'

How it works:
1. Determine the cost to acquire a customer at time zero (e.g., $5)
2. Measure contribution profit per customer over time (revenue minus everything above fixed OpEx like salary and rent)
3. Calculate how many months it takes to generate equivalent contribution profit (e.g., $5)
4. Use this break-even timeline as your investment decision criteria

Rationale: Every market is different. Predicting unit economics 5+ years into the future for each market would be naive. This framework is more honest and practical than LTV-based approaches.

Additional principle: 'We invest in every channel. We have to constantly test and iterate to identify arbitrage opportunities.'

### Tinder Campus Launch Playbook (How to kickstart and scale a consumer business—Step 4: Find your early adopters by doing things that don’t scale)
