# Written Communication for Leaders - Frameworks, Templates & Checklists

*48 artifacts extracted from Lenny's Podcast and Newsletter*

## Frameworks

### Categories of Novelty (Julian Shapiro)
A classification of the different types of insights that trigger a dopamine hit in readers.

How it works: Categories include: Counterintuitive (not how I thought it worked), Counter-narrative (not what I was told), Shock and awe (crazy/unbelievable facts), and Elegant articulation (complex ideas boiled down beautifully).

### Combined SCR + Minto Pyramid Framework (The Minto Pyramid Principle and the SCR Framework)
The integration of both frameworks: SCR provides the narrative wrapper, and the Minto Pyramid structures the Resolution section specifically.

How it works: Structure:
1. SITUATION: What is the state of affairs today?
2. COMPLICATION: What has changed that requires action?
3. RESOLUTION (Minto Pyramid):
   a. Lead with the recommendation/answer/ask
   b. Supporting Argument 1
      - Data point 1a
      - Data point 1b
      - Data point 1c
   c. Supporting Argument 2
      - Data point 2a
      - Data point 2b
      - Data point 2c
   d. Supporting Argument 3
      - Data point 3a
      - Data point 3b
      - Data point 3c

Application modes:
1. Crafting: Open a new doc and fill in S, C, R sections when writing any proposal or ask.
2. Listening: Coach presenters to follow this flow during design reviews, strategy proposals, etc.
3. Templates: Use Lenny's 1-Pager and Strategy templates which already follow this structure.

### Core Product Documents (Eeke de Milliano)
The three essential documents every level of the organization needs.

How it works: 1. Charter (Mission, vision, strategy). 2. Goals (What you aim to do and how to measure success). 3. Roadmap (What you are shipping). Time horizon scales with company maturity.

### GSD (Get Shit Done) Updates (Farhan Thawar)
A weekly cadence where every project shares high-fidelity updates to the whole company.

How it works: Requires sharing progress every week, ideally with a demo or video, to leverage Parkinson's Law and maintain intensity.

### Language Affects Thought (Sapir-Whorf in Product) (Peter Deng)
Peter's application of Herbert Clark's thesis that language shapes thinking, applied to product writing, vision docs, PRDs, and slide decks.

How it works: Core thesis: Language doesn't just communicate thought — it shapes thought. Application: Wrong words in a PRD create multiplicative downstream misinterpretation. Peter spends hours on ~20 words in a slide deck. Example: Russian speakers with two words for shades of blue distinguish those shades faster than English speakers. Practical implication: Treat word choice in product documents as high-leverage work, not polish.

### Minto Pyramid Principle (Maggie Crowley)
A communication framework for writing concise emails and documents.

How it works: Put the headline and full conclusion first, and place your supporting arguments second. Do not build up to the point.

### Minto Pyramid Principle and SCR Framework (The Best of Lenny’s Newsletter 2023)
Communication frameworks for product managers to structure their thinking and presentations more effectively.

How it works: Two complementary communication frameworks: 1. Minto Pyramid Principle — start with the answer/recommendation, then group supporting arguments, then provide detailed evidence. Pyramid structure from top (conclusion) to bottom (details). 2. SCR Framework — Situation (context), Complication (the problem), Resolution (your recommendation). Both help PMs communicate more clearly in documents and presentations.

### Rule of Seven (Email Escalation Rule) (Shweta Shriva)
A team process rule that limits unproductive email threads: if an issue hasn't been resolved within 7 email exchanges, the parties must switch to a live conversation (call, huddle, or in-person meeting).

How it works: Rule: After 7 emails (or 10 in larger organizations) on the same thread without resolution, stop emailing and get on a call or in a room to resolve it live. Applies to back-and-forth between two people or multi-party threads where people keep piling on without convergence.

### SCR (Situation-Complication-Resolution) Framework (The Minto Pyramid Principle and the SCR Framework)
A narrative structure for business communication derived from the universal story arc. Provides context before the recommendation to avoid being too abrupt. Also known as SCQR, SCQA, or SCA.

How it works: Structure:
1. SITUATION: What is the state of affairs today? Facts, data, and unambiguous background that everyone will agree with.
2. COMPLICATION: What has changed that requires action? What is the problem?
3. RESOLUTION: What we need to do to resolve this complication. Use Minto Pyramid style — answer-first followed by supporting arguments.

Tips:
- Common tweak: Move Resolution to the top (R-S-C) when audience doesn't need convincing and is ready for the recommendation immediately.
- In business contexts, keep Situation and Complication short — colleagues want to be informed, not entertained.
- Can be applied to emails, strategy docs, presentations, 1-pagers, and any business communication.
- Also called SCQR (Situation-Complication-Question-Resolution), SCQA (Situation-Complication-Question-Answer), or SCA (Situation-Complication-Answer). SCR is the simplest.

### Situation-Complication-Resolution (SCR) Framework (What Seven Years at Airbnb Taught Me About Building a Business)
McKinsey's storytelling framework used to communicate a problem statement and business story to a wider audience.

How it works: Three-part structure: 1) Situation — describe the current state and context, 2) Complication — what has changed or what problem has arisen that demands action, 3) Resolution — the proposed solution or path forward. Used at Airbnb to communicate problem statements to broader audiences. Reference: https://speakingsherpa.com/how-to-tell-a-business-story-using-the-mckinsey-situation-complication-resolution-scr-framework/

### Slack Urgency Tags (Gia Laudi)
A communication protocol to protect team focus and time.

How it works: Using specific text or emojis in messages to indicate urgency: 'EOD' (End of Day), 'No rush', 'Timely', or an alarm emoji for 'ASAP/Deal with now'.

### Start Right Before You Get Eaten by the Bear (Wes Kao)
A storytelling and writing principle to cut backstory scope creep by starting your narrative at the most compelling moment, not at the beginning of the chronological sequence

How it works: Instead of starting with all the setup (going to REI, booking the campsite, the drive over), start right before the dramatic moment (a bear almost mauling you because someone left a Clif Bar out). Serve just enough context right before the juicy part. Write first, then go back and cut the preamble.

### Super Specific How (Wes Kao)
A content creation principle that says most writers spend too much time on the what and why and not enough on the how — cut the preamble and focus on actionable, nuanced implementation details

How it works: When creating content: 1) Don't elaborate on concepts your audience already agrees with, 2) Focus on specific how-to details, nuances, and edge cases, 3) Cut backstory and preamble — 'start right before you get eaten by the bear', 4) Use concrete examples that help readers internalize how something works in practice

### The Minto Pyramid Principle (The Minto Pyramid Principle and the SCR Framework)
A communication framework developed by Barbara Minto at McKinsey for structuring persuasive business arguments. Instead of building up from data to conclusions (bottom-up), you present top-down: recommendation first, then supporting arguments, then data.

How it works: Structure:
1. TOP: Start with the recommendation / answer / ask upfront
2. MIDDLE: Back up that recommendation with a handful of supporting arguments (typically 2-4)
3. BOTTOM: Back up each supporting argument with data, evidence, and examples

Key principle: 'You think from the bottom up, but you present from the top-down.'

Visual: Forms a pyramid shape — one recommendation at top, few arguments in middle, many data points at base.

Anti-pattern (the upside-down pyramid): Starting with all the data, building to conclusions, and only revealing the recommendation at the end — by which point the audience has tuned out.

### The Pyramid Principle (My favorite product management templates)
McKinsey's framework for structuring communication by leading with the answer and supporting with grouped arguments

How it works: A communication framework where you start with the conclusion/recommendation at the top, then support it with key arguments grouped logically, each backed by supporting data. Forces top-down thinking and MECE (mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive) grouping. Linked at: https://medium.com/lessons-from-mckinsey/the-pyramid-principle-f0885dd3c5c7

### Time Investment to Post Quality Correlation (500,000)
Lenny's observation that there's a strong correlation between time spent on a post and how well it performs

How it works: Median time per post: 10 hours
Some posts: hundreds of hours (e.g., the B2B startup series)
Strong correlation between time spent and post performance.

Key principle: Any time allotted for your newsletter that is NOT writing (and making your writing better) is time wasted.

Common time-wasting activities to avoid:
- Optimizing your website
- Creating a brand
- Crafting a big strategy

This is why having a full-time job makes it hard to do this well—you need significant time investment per post.

### Topic Selection Matrix (Julian Shapiro)
A tool for deciding what to write about to ensure completion.

How it works: Pair an Objective (e.g., open people's eyes, articulate the unspoken, contribute original research, tell an emotional story) with a Motivation (e.g., get it off my chest, solve a nagging problem, geek out with others).

### Topic Selection Venn Diagram (500,000)
Three-criteria framework for choosing the right content topic, deliberately ordered to prioritize energy and experience over audience demand

How it works: To find a topic that is right for you (in priority order):

1. Follow your energy: What topics give you energy to think about, write about, and talk about? What saps you of energy? Spend more on the former and less on the latter.

2. Make sure it's based on your real-life experience: You need to know what you're talking about. People can tell if you don't. What's at the center of the Venn diagram of your unique skills and background that together create an interesting topic?

3. Pay attention to what people value: (Deliberately third because many people start here, which is a trap.) What do people often ask you for advice about? What has been the most helpful thing you've shared with friends? Where do you find pull from others?

Warning: Don't get too niche. You have to write/think/learn about one narrow topic for a long time. If it's too narrow, you'll get bored.

Rule of thumb: Start by getting 1,000 people to think your newsletter is the best newsletter in the world.

### Word-Count First Writing Method (Julie Zhuo)
A method for overcoming writer's block inspired by NaNoWriMo, focusing strictly on hitting a daily word count rather than editing for quality.

How it works: Sit down for 30-45 minutes with a specific word count goal (e.g., 250 or 500 words). Do not edit while writing. Just get the words out, knowing the first draft will be junk, and revise later.

### Writing Quality Equation (Julian Shapiro)
A mental model for evaluating the overall quality of a piece of writing.

How it works: Writing Quality = Novelty (new, significant ideas) x Resonance (storytelling, examples, analogies, metaphors). Draft 1 focuses on finding novelty; Draft 2 focuses on increasing resonance.

## Templates

### 6-Part Ask Template (On asking for help (even when you really don’t want to))
A structured template for making effective help requests in professional contexts. Can be adapted based on relationship strength, goal overlap, and recipient expertise.

How it works: Structure your ask using these six components (not all required, order can vary):

1. **Signpost:** Signal that an ask is coming. Example: 'I'd like to ask for your help with something.'
2. **Clear request:** State what you need specifically. Example: 'Specifically, I need [type of help] with [specific task/problem].'
3. **Rationale:** Explain why it matters. Example: 'This is important because [reason].'
4. **Why them:** Explain why you're asking this specific person. Example: 'I'm asking you because [specific reason].'
5. **Timeline:** Set expectations for when you need it. Example: 'I'd need this by [date/time].'
6. **Opt-in/out:** Give them a graceful way to accept or decline. Example: 'Is this something you're able to help with?'

Tips for making it compelling:
- Create a relational connection first (shared goals, shared experiences, personal disclosure)
- Make the request as small, easy, and clear as possible
- Frame around THEIR goals, not just your needs
- Increment asks once momentum builds in the conversation
- Account for cultural, power/hierarchy, and remote/async dynamics

### Minimum Viable Process (MVP) Templates (Eeke de Milliano)
Standardized document templates that include an explicit 'escape hatch' for high performers.

How it works: Add a disclaimer at the top: 'If this doesn't work for what you're trying to explain, don't use it. But just know that this is the minimum viable thing. We're setting the bar here, but go higher if you can.'

### Slide Doc (Nancy Duarte)
A dense presentation meant to be circulated and read rather than presented live, combining the visual nature of slides with the detail of a memo.

How it works: Uses full sentences and prose, strong pictures, and can include a massive appendix. The front might be 5 summary slides followed by detailed reading material. Ideal for internal alignment without a meeting.

### Structured narrative for feature proposals (Amazon-style) (Ronny Kohavi)
A written document format replacing PowerPoint for feature proposals, with structured questions to answer, reviewed collaboratively with inline comments.

How it works: Originated from Jeff Bezos 'no more PowerPoint' mandate at Amazon. Structure: written narrative with predefined questions the proposal must answer. Reviewed as a team with inline comments (originally paper, now Google Docs/Word). Ronny carried this practice to Microsoft and Airbnb.

### TLDR + Context Message Structure (Wes Kao)
A format for written business communication that puts the most important point at the top followed by optional context below

How it works: Structure: 1) Put the most important point / recommendation / decision at the top (the TLDR or gist), 2) Add 'Context:' as a header below, 3) Include multiple paragraphs of supporting thinking, rationale, and factors for anyone who wants it. Key: if someone already agrees, they can stop reading; if they need convincing, they can continue. Separate action items/decisions clearly from background/FYI information.

### Two-Way Writeup (Lane Shackleton)
A document structure that embeds feedback and discussion directly into the content rather than relying on margin comments.

How it works: Includes three key elements: 1. 'Done reading' buttons to track who has consumed the doc. 2. A 'Dory' table for logging and upvoting questions. 3. A Sentiment/Pulse table (using 1-5 smiley faces) to gauge overall alignment.

### What, So What, Now What (Matt Abrahams)
A versatile three-part structure for product updates, feature descriptions, or feedback.

How it works: 1. What: Describe the product, feature, or specific behavior. 2. So What: Explain why it is important or relevant to the audience. 3. Now What: State the next steps, action items, or what needs to change.

## Checklists

### 59-Second Loom Best Practices (Product manager is an unfair role. So work unfairly.)
Guidelines for creating short async video messages that replace meetings.

How it works: 1. Aim for under a minute (make it feel lightweight for the viewer)
2. Film two takes (or more) to get your message clear and succinct
3. In your accompanying message, pitch the brevity and time ROI for everyone (e.g., '59 seconds that'll save us 30 minutes')
4. Use when the reason for a meeting is to communicate a nuanced, high-bandwidth concept
5. Treat your audience like an audience—be intentional about converting them to view and click
6. Treat this as a core PM communication skill, not just for major announcements or bug reports

### Amazon's Writing Tips (SEO keywords, career ladders, backlog tools, copywriting, OnlyFans, AMA with Pete Kazanjy and much more)
Amazon's internal guidelines for clear, effective writing, shared as an image in the newsletter.

How it works: Referenced as a screenshot/image of Amazon's writing tips. The image was shared in the context of improving copywriting skills. Specific tips visible in the image include Amazon's emphasis on clear, customer-focused writing.

### Email Etiquette Checklist (Sam Lessin)
Rules for professional email communication including the subtle hierarchy of To/CC fields

How it works: 1) Keep emails short — long emails are monologues, 2) Get to the point quickly, 3) Avoid emojis in business context (treat them like jokes — at your own risk), 4) Proofread everything, 5) Respond quickly even if briefly — acknowledge receipt, 6) Order To recipients by importance/relevance, 7) Use CC for 'FYI, no immediate response expected', 8) If you're far down the CC list, don't be first to respond, 9) Never put someone in the To field to check name spelling, 10) Assume the recipient is busy

### Lenny's Writing Improvement Toolkit (Lenny’s holiday gift guide)
Tools and resources for improving professional and creative writing

How it works: 1. Writer.com — browser extension for better writing, replaced Grammarly for Lenny
2. On Writing Well by William Zinsser — 'the book that taught me the most about writing well'
3. MUJI gel ink ballpoint pens (0.38mm) — quality, reliable, affordable pens for analog writing

### Novelty Editing Process (Julian Shapiro)
A step-by-step method for editing a draft to maximize reader engagement.

How it works: 1) Write the draft. 2) Have 20 friends read it and highlight sentences that make them go 'whoa'. 3) Map the highlighted areas. 4) Edit to condense the 'white space' between the highlights so the frequency of novelty is as high as possible.

### Remote Work Communication Best Practices (This Week #6: Cultivating good relationships with distributed co-workers, building trust to accelerate growth, and leveling up as a PM when you have extra time)
Three tactical best practices for building good relationships with remote engineers and designers, focused on reducing miscommunication

How it works: 1. Always write things down: On every call, document (a) options that were discussed, (b) decisions that were made, (c) next steps, and (d) who is going to do them. For recurring meetings, maintain a single document that incorporates an agenda and structured meeting notes of what was discussed each time. Fully and clearly document specs to ensure understanding. The more that gets documented, the better.

2. Screenshare when possible: When talking about work an engineer or designer is doing, screenshare and go directly into the tool where their work and tasks are documented (ticketing system, spec document, Invision prototype). Have a discussion, go back and forth on feedback, and collaborate in the appropriate tool while on the call so nobody has to remember to update things later.

3. Postmortem everything that's shipped: Especially larger bodies of work — marketing launches, product releases, big customer wins. Understand what went well and what didn't. Constantly assess how things are going and discuss how to improve. You can't just come up with a process and expect it to work well without reviewing and iterating it.

### SCR Framework Tips and Variants (The Minto Pyramid Principle and the SCR Framework)
Key tips for adapting the SCR framework to different business contexts.

How it works: 1. Move Resolution to the top (R-S-C order) when your audience doesn't need convincing and is ready to hear your recommendation immediately.
2. In business contexts, keep Situation and Complication sections short — colleagues want to be informed, not entertained. Get to the Resolution as quickly as possible.
3. SCR is also known as SCQR (Situation-Complication-Question-Resolution), SCQA (Situation-Complication-Question-Answer), or SCA (Situation-Complication-Answer). They're all the same thing — SCR is the simplest version.

### Slide Title Rule (Andy Raskin)
A simple rule for improving presentation decks.

How it works: Make the title of every slide the main takeaway rather than a generic label. For example, use 'Our team is veterans of X industry' instead of 'The Team'.

### Story Starting Formula (Matthew Dicks)
The two essential elements to include in the first sentence of any story.

How it works: Start every story with 1. Location (activates the audience's imagination immediately) and 2. Action (shows the story is moving forward). Example: 'I am in a place doing a thing.' This signals to the audience that a narrative has begun and commands their attention.

### The Dinner Test (Matthew Dicks)
A filter for ensuring your storytelling tone is natural and conversational.

How it works: Ensure the story is a slightly elevated version of what you'd tell at a dinner party. Remove performance art, unattributed dialogue (e.g., starting with a quote), and sound effects (e.g., 'Bang!').

### Three Ways to Apply SCR + Minto in Your Work (The Minto Pyramid Principle and the SCR Framework)
A checklist of three application modes for using the combined SCR + Minto framework day-to-day.

How it works: 1. CRAFTING: Whenever putting together a proposal or asking for something, open a new document and start with:
   - Situation: What is the state of affairs today? What are the facts that everyone will agree with?
   - Complication: What has changed that requires action?
   - Resolution: What do you recommend? Minto Pyramid-style — answer-first followed by supporting arguments.

2. LISTENING: When listening to a presentation (design review, strategy proposal), encourage the speaker to:
   - First share the context on where things stand today (Situation)
   - Then the Complication ('What problem are we solving?')
   - Then get right to the recommendation before sharing supporting arguments

3. TEMPLATES: Leverage Lenny's 1-Pager template and Company strategy template (which build on SCR format) instead of starting with a blank page.

### Three-Email Rule (Hari Srinivasan)
A communication heuristic to prevent endless text-based debates.

How it works: If a thread reaches three back-and-forths in an email, pick up the phone. If you are on the phone for 20 minutes without resolution, write to the designated decision-maker to make the call.

### Using AI as a Writing/Editing Partner (Seth Godin)
Seth's specific process for using Claude as an editorial assistant while maintaining authorial voice.

How it works: 1. Upload a list of 4 items → ask 'What did I miss?' → Claude suggests 3 completions you hadn't considered. 2. Upload chapters → ask 'What claims am I making that I'm not sustaining?' → identifies weak arguments. 3. Identify sentences that don't sound like you → ask Claude to help figure out why. Key principle: Every word published is still yours. Claude is a patient editor, not a ghostwriter. It fills the gap of editors who aren't paid enough or given enough time.

### Writing Quality Improvement Checklist (500,000)
Actionable steps for improving newsletter writing quality based on Lenny's experience and three recommended books

How it works: 1. Cut 30%-50% of your words
2. Cut every word that isn't necessary
3. Make your intro 50% shorter
4. Get into the meat of your post as quickly as possible
5. 'Start your story right before you get eaten by the bear' (Wes Kao)
6. Try removing the first paragraph of your first draft entirely—the post will likely get stronger
7. Ask: What am I adding NEW to the conversation? What new ideas, insights, and concepts am I contributing?
8. Do primary research to uncover insights that haven't been shared before
9. Do the work on behalf of the reader

Recommended books:
- On Writing Well by William Zinsser
- Nobody Wants to Read Your Sh*t by Steven Pressfield
- Several Short Sentences About Writing by Verlyn Klinkenborg

### Writing Strategy by Goal (Will Larson)
Different writing approaches depending on whether your goal is career advancement or building a long-term writing practice.

How it works: Goal A - Career advancement: Write 2-3 really good pieces. Spend extensive time drafting, revising, getting feedback. Create artifacts that show you're a deep thinker. Don't start a newsletter. Goal B - Long-term prolific writing: Publish almost everything you write. Before starting any piece, check 'Can I publish this?' If no, don't start. Write what gives you energy, abandon what doesn't. Write about topics related to your current work (aligns writing with job performance). Don't write for financial gain or on a schedule. Don't optimize for audience preferences. Accept that some pieces won't be good. Don't respond to negative feedback from strangers. Biggest risk is quitting, not growing too slow.

## Examples

### Before/After Minto Email: Bug Fix Sprint Request (The Minto Pyramid Principle and the SCR Framework)
A concrete before-and-after example showing how to restructure an email requesting a bug-fix sprint from bottom-up (anti-pattern) to top-down (Minto) to full SCR + Minto format.

How it works: BEFORE MINTO (anti-pattern — bottom-up):
'Hey manager, I'm getting concerned about our bug count. Look at this chart below of our growth bug growth over time, how many bugs we've been fixing, and where our bug count is projected to be next year. We're also hearing from CX and our research team that customers are starting to churn because of our bugs, and you can even see in this chart how our churn rate increase seems to be correlated with our bug count increase. Finally, there's evidence to show that bugs are starting to hurt brand perception, as you can see in these tweets, these stories from sales, and this complaint from our biggest customer. I recommend we devote our next sprint to fixing bugs. Let me know what you think.'

AFTER MINTO (top-down, no SCR context):
'Hey manager, I recommend we devote our next sprint to fixing bugs. Here's why:
1. Our bug count is growing faster than we're able to fix them
   - Chart showing bug growth over time
   - How many bugs fixed over same time
   - Where bug count expected to be next year
2. Bugs are leading to customer churn
   - Data from CX
   - Story from customer interview
   - Churn mapped alongside bug count
3. Bugs are leading to a hit on brand perception
   - Tweets from users
   - Complaint from top customer
   - Sales team feedback on declining close rates'

FULL SCR + MINTO:
'Hey manager, As you know, we've generally had a good handle on bugs. Our bug count has been steady over the last few months, and we've had a good system to keep our teams fixing bugs while shipping new product. [Situation]

Recently however, we've seen a large spike in bugs, and it's beginning to significantly impact our bottom-line. [Complication]

I recommend we devote our next sprint to fixing bugs. Here's why [Resolution]:
1. Our bug count is growing faster than we're able to fix them
   - Chart showing bug growth over time
   - How many bugs we've been able to fix over the same time
   - Where we expect our bug count to be in the next year
2. Bugs are leading to customer churn
   - Data from CX
   - Story from customer interview
   - Churn mapped alongside bug count
3. Bugs are leading to a hit on brand perception
   - Tweets from users
   - Complaint from top customer
   - Sales team feedback on ability to close

Let me know what you think!'
