# Hiring World-Class Product Talent - Frameworks, Templates & Checklists

*70 artifacts extracted from Lenny's Podcast and Newsletter*

## Frameworks

### 37signals Job Ad Philosophy (Adding a work trial to your interview process)
Jason Fried's approach to writing job ads that attract the right work trial candidates

How it works: Instead of listing:
- Years of experience required
- Tools/technologies used
- Bullet-point qualifications

Write about:
- 'What would you have done here last week if you had this job?'
- Specific recent work examples from the role
- Ask: 'Does this resonate with you? Is this something you feel like you could do?'

Principle: The work trial fizzles if you don't have the right five finalists. The job ad is the critical upstream filter.

### A++ Hiring Flywheel (Winning at early-stage hiring)
A compounding model showing how hiring great people early creates a self-reinforcing cycle of talent attraction

How it works: Three reinforcing mechanisms:
1. Social Proof: If great people are joining, it must be great (signals to future candidates)
2. Referral Network: Great people know other great people (expands sourcing pipeline)
3. Flywheel: A++ people join to work with other A++ people, and so on (self-perpetuating cycle)

Key insight from Siqi Chen: 'Raising the bar happens organically and actually becomes easier if you're hiring exceptional people. Your team will demand it, and because of the quality of the team you already have, it becomes easier to convince other exceptional people to join.'

Anti-pattern: Never lower your recruiting bar to fill a short-term need. It will have longer-term negative consequences.

### AI Role Classification Taxonomy (State of the product job market in 2025)
How Lenny and TrueUp categorize AI roles into three distinct buckets, useful for understanding what counts as an 'AI role'

How it works: Three categories of 'AI roles':
1. Roles at AI-native companies - Companies whose core product is AI (e.g., OpenAI, Anthropic, Scale)
2. Roles at AI-driven companies - Companies heavily built on AI technology (e.g., Cursor, Harvey, Lovable)
3. AI-specific roles at non-AI companies - Dedicated AI/ML positions at traditional tech companies (e.g., AI PM at Figma, AI/ML PM at Salesforce)

This taxonomy helps job seekers understand the breadth of AI PM opportunities beyond just working at 'AI companies.'

### AI-Era Designer Hiring Archetypes (Jenny Wen)
Three specific profiles to target when hiring designers for fast-moving AI product teams.

How it works: 1. Strong Generalists ('Block-shaped'): 80th percentile across multiple skills (PM, engineering, design), allowing them to flex across roles. 2. Deep Specialists ('Deep T-shaped'): Top 10% in the industry at one specific thing (e.g., highly technical prototyping or elite visual/icon design) to differentiate AI outputs. 3. Craft New Grads: Early career, humble, eager to learn, and unburdened by legacy design processes.

### Alpha vs. Beta (Matt MacInnis)
A mental model borrowed from finance to evaluate people and processes. Alpha is outperformance/innovation; Beta is volatility.

How it works: Use process to lower beta (volatility) in mature products (like payroll), but avoid process where you need high alpha (0 to 1 products). Hire high-alpha people for innovation, low-beta people for reliability.

### Content vs Process Hiring (Sanchan Saxena)
Hire leaders who know 'what to do when' (content) and teach them process, rather than hiring process experts and trying to teach them content.

How it works: Definitions: Content = what you do and when you do it (roadmap, strategy, customer understanding). Process = how you do it (scrum, agile, ceremonies). Key principles: 1) Content is correlated to startup success; process is not, 2) Hire content people and teach them process, 3) If a leader's entire team disappeared, they should be able to roll up their sleeves and say 'this is what we should build, when, and how,' 4) Watch for the warning sign: process becomes the product (80% of exec calendar spent managing process), 5) A process-heavy organization forces the founder to become the sole content person, which kills scaling. Inspired by Netflix Culture Deck and Brian Chesky.

### Data-Driven Hiring Using Public Metrics (Sri Batchu)
Using publicly available data (e.g., SimilarWeb) to identify which companies excel at the function you're hiring for, then sourcing from those teams

How it works: Steps: 1) Create target list of companies 1-2 stages ahead that you respect, 2) Use SimilarWeb or similar tools to measure which companies are best at the specific function (e.g., % of traffic from email for CRM marketing roles), 3) Source candidates from those high-performing teams. Complementary approach: Network-based search — ask network for best person in function, get intros, ask each one who the best person they know is, create ranked target list.

### Dehydrated Entity Hiring Model (Varun Mohan)
Only hire when teams are 'underwater'—the company should be like a dehydrated entity where each hire is a drop of water, and you only add when you're dehydrated again. This forces ruthless prioritization and prevents manufactured work.

How it works: Steps: 1) Set ambitious goals requiring focus. 2) Do NOT hire proactively or for potential future needs. 3) Wait until a team member raises their hand saying they're underwater. 4) Have a conversation: Can we deprioritize something instead? 5) Only hire if the work truly can't be deprioritized. 6) After hire, return to dehydrated state and repeat. Key principle: People's value ≠ size of their team. The most valuable person accomplishes the most with the fewest people.

### Elements of a Great Job Posting (Examples of great job postings)
A pattern language distilled from Lenny's analysis of five standout job postings, identifying the recurring elements that make them effective

How it works: Recurring elements across all five examples:

1. **Compelling product/company summary** — A short (2-5 line) description at the top that hooks the reader (Superhuman, Mighty, Levels)
2. **Show, don't tell culture and values** — Use word choice, tone, humor, storytelling, and emojis rather than a bullet list of values (Superhuman, Basecamp, DelightChat)
3. **Evidence and proof** — Links to walls of love, founder writing, investor perspective, product screenshots (Superhuman, DelightChat)
4. **Clear fit/anti-fit signals** — Explicitly state who the role IS and ISN'T for, or include a 'Why you may NOT be excited about us' section (Basecamp, Levels)
5. **Transparency** — Salary, employee handbook links, interview process details, in-depth FAQ (Basecamp, DelightChat, Mighty, Levels)
6. **Impact framing** — Communicate the large impact the person will have, not just responsibilities (Superhuman, Mighty)
7. **Personality and warmth** — Conversational language, humor, emojis, non-standard formatting, storytelling (all five examples)
8. **Narrative over lists** — Tell a story about the role and why it exists rather than defaulting to bullet-point requirements (Basecamp)
9. **Describe the team, not just requirements** — Show what the current team is like rather than only listing what the candidate must bring (Mighty)
10. **tl;dr at top** — A concise summary for scanning (Levels)

### Equity Preference Predictor Model (Why cash is king)
Logistic regression-based predictors of who is more likely to prioritize equity over salary, useful for founders crafting offers or recruiters segmenting candidates

How it works: Predictors of equity preference (from logistic regression):

1. Gender (strongest effect): Men are 2.3x as likely to prefer equity vs. women
2. Job level: Each increase in seniority (IC → people manager → executive) increases likelihood of preferring equity by 2.0x. Senior executives and founders show highest equity preference.
3. Years of experience: 4-9 years and 10+ years are 1.53x as likely to prefer equity vs. <4 years
4. Geographic location: U.S.-based workers are 1.75x as likely to prioritize equity. U.K. has strongest negative effect (0.44 odds ratio). EU respondents are 42% less likely to prioritize equity.
5. Company stage: Early-stage (pre-seed through series D+) employees are 1.34x as likely to prioritize equity. Public/later-stage employees prefer salary.
6. Company size (smallest effect): As company size increases, equity preference slightly decreases (0.94 odds ratio)

### Five PM Archetypes (Peter Deng)
A framework for categorizing product managers into five types to build balanced, complementary teams. Each person has a primary and secondary archetype.

How it works: 1. Consumer PM: Half designer, half PM. Obsesses over details, delight, craft, pixel-perfection. Feels the 'vibe.' 2. Growth PM: Half data scientist, half PM. Numbers-first, skeptical, insists on testing and proving. 'Show me the data.' 3. GM/Business PM: Half MBA, half PM. Starts with business model, margins, incentives, value creation. Marketplace thinking. 4. Platform PM: Deeply wired to build tools for others. Builds internal systems that make the company go faster. Often overlooked. 5. Research/AI PM (formerly Algorithms PM): Half researcher, half engineer, half PM. Deeply understands tech/models AND has product taste. Affects model training to build amazing products. Everyone has a primary and a secondary archetype.

### Five Pillars of Early-Stage Hiring Success (Winning at early-stage hiring)
A framework of five consistent themes that separate startups that hire world-class people from those that struggle, distilled from interviews with successful early-stage founders

How it works: 1. Captivating Vision: Articulate a crystal clear, ambitious vision that makes candidates feel their work would be truly meaningful. Get feedback from brutally honest people. The mission can range from solving climate change to creating financial transparency.
2. A++ Early Team: Set the bar for first hires even higher than you think. Never lower the bar for short-term needs. Creates a compounding flywheel: social proof → referral network → more A++ people joining.
3. Putting in the Work: Budget ~100 hours per hire. Sell at every stage (cold outreach, first call, every interview). Treat hiring as strategy—define roles well before needing to fill them. Best hires are people who weren't looking when first contacted, won over ~6 months.
4. Founder's Network: Tap previous companies, investors, co-founder networks, and referrals. Use investor/advisor network to help close candidates. Bootstrap network via connected investors (a16z, Sequoia) or communities (YC, OnDeck). This advantage fades quickly.
5. Pitching Prowess: Pitch candidates like you pitch investors. Make the offer experience memorable (custom decks, team surprise Zooms). Strong correlation between pitching candidates, selling to customers, and fundraising.

### Founder's Compelling Offer Framework (Why cash is king)
Guidelines for founders to structure compensation offers that resonate with today's talent priorities

How it works: For founders trying to attract top talent:
1. Don't rely solely on equity to attract candidates—75% of candidates prioritize salary
2. Pair a strong salary offer with meaningful equity
3. Frame the offer around two pillars:
   a. Immediate financial stability through competitive salary
   b. Long-term growth opportunities through equity
4. Demonstrate a clear path to liquidity for equity
5. Communicate how the company is positioned for success (to make equity feel less like a 'lottery ticket')
6. Consider that different candidate profiles value equity differently—men, senior executives, U.S.-based workers, and early-stage employees are more equity-oriented

### Hire the Absolute Greatest Framework (The unconventional Palantir principles that catalyzed a generation of startups)
A hiring philosophy that targets the literal #1 person on Earth for each critical discipline, not just top-percentile candidates, based on the compounding network effects of being at the very top of a field.

How it works: Core principle: The difference between #1 and #50 in skills is practically indistinguishable, but #1 has an order of magnitude more access to knowledge, opportunity, and talent. This cascades through hiring generations.

Two reasons this matters:
1. The best are better at the specific work
2. The world opens doors for people at the very top — media attention, top VC attention, top founder attention → better connected → disproportionately more access to other best people → accelerates learning and ability to make the impossible happen

Three-step process:
1. For each discipline on the critical path to success, hire the world's #1
2. To be sure, ask others in the community if those people are the best (reputation-based validation)
3. Look in unexpected places for the talent — it's the experience, not the fame (sometimes buried in bureaucracy, sometimes they don't even know they're the world's best at a particular problem)

Examples:
- Palantir hired the most decorated and well-known Navy SEAL commanders alive for military customer engagement
- A space startup hired an engineer who has built more satellites in the sky today than any other human to build a satellite fabrication facility
- Quindar Space founders all built satellite ground software together at multiple prolific satellite companies including OneWeb

### Hiring Pipeline Systems Model (Will Larson)
A stocks-and-flows model for diagnosing hiring pipeline problems by tracking conversion rates at each stage to identify the actual bottleneck.

How it works: Stocks: Potential candidates (infinite) → Sourced candidates → Recruiter screen passed → Hiring manager screen passed → Offer stage → Offer accepted → Hired. Flows/rates: Sourcing rate (function of number of sourcers), recruiter screen conversion rate, hiring manager screen conversion rate, offer extension rate, offer acceptance rate. Three common failure modes: 1) Hiring managers won't extend offers (can't reach conviction). 2) Offers extended but not accepted (closing problem). 3) Not enough candidates entering pipeline (sourcing problem). Pull historicals from ATS (Greenhouse etc.) to diagnose.

### Hiring Playbook (Luc Levesque)
A three-chapter mental model for recruiting top 1% talent.

How it works: Divided into Finding, Assessing, and Closing. Key criteria: Look for 3 'signs of excellence' (e.g., winning a gold medal, founding a company, or having a former boss leave a company and come back to poach them).

### Hiring Timing Heuristic (Before vs. After PMF) (This Week #15: When should a SaaS startup raise money 💰)
A rule of thumb from Sam Altman for when to hire: hiring before PMF slows you down, hiring after PMF speeds you up.

How it works: Rule: Hiring before you get product/market fit slows you down. Hiring after you get product/market fit speeds you up.

Before PMF, your two priorities are:
a) Live as long as possible (minimize burn)
b) Iterate as quickly as possible (small team, fast cycles)

After PMF, hiring accelerates growth and is worth the increased burn rate.

### Long-term Recruiting Framework (Ayo Omojola)
A mental model for recruiting top talent by acting as a matchmaker and providing value long before a job is available.

How it works: 1. Identify someone you want to work with. 2. Tell them directly. 3. Find out what they want right now. 4. Connect them to it (intros, advice, etc.) with no strings attached. 5. Wait for the timing to align ('You pick the people, they pick when').

### Marketing, Sales, and Product Hiring Approach (Jason Shah)
A three-pronged approach to recruiting and closing talent.

How it works: 1. Marketing: Building a positive brand and reputation so candidates know you before interviewing. 2. Sales: Understanding what motivates the candidate and pitching to their specific needs rather than giving a generic pitch. 3. Product: Iterating on the job description and role to fit a great candidate, rather than rigidly sticking to a predefined spec.

### PM Hire Timing Decision by Startup Type (When they hired their first PM)
Framework for deciding when to hire a PM based on the nature of your startup's core risk

How it works: The timing of your first PM hire depends on where your startup's core risk/insight lies:
- Technical insight/risk → Hire core engineers first (PM can wait)
- Regulatory risk → Hire someone familiar with regulators early
- Product risk (UX, product design, product-market fit) → Hire PM early, potentially as first employee
- Engineering-oriented product with product-minded engineers → PM can wait longer (Stripe example)
- B2B with complex stakeholder relationships → Hire PM sooner
- B2C with straightforward experience → Be very careful; PM may disempower engineering and design

Examples:
- Coda: Product risk → PM was essentially first hire
- Snyk: Product problem (not tech problem) → PM hired very early
- Stripe: Engineering-oriented → Waited a long time, hired product-minded engineers instead
- Snap: Designers took PM duties → Eventually chaotic, hired PM for monetization

### PM Hiring Funnel Benchmarks (How to interview product managers)
Conversion rate data at each stage of the PM hiring funnel based on 75,000+ applications across high-growth tech companies

How it works: Based on 75,000+ recent applications across high-growth tech companies:
- Only 13% of inbound applicants make it to first screen
- Average of 23 PM candidates need to be talked to in order to hire one
- Key insight: Most teams underestimate candidate volume needed; inbound alone is usually insufficient; must supplement with sourced candidates and referrals
- Approximate pass-through rates: Recruiter Screen ~50%, Hiring Manager Screen ~40-50%, Full-Day Interview to Offer ~low percentage

### Product Leader Archetypes (Lauren Ipsen)
A categorization of the three main types of product leaders to help founders define what specific skills they need to hire for.

How it works: 1. Platform/Infrastructure leaders. 2. Core/Consumer Product leaders (often indexed on UX/Design). 3. Specialists (hyper-focused on Growth, Monetization, or Ads).

### Ramp's Three PM Hiring Attributes (How Ramp builds product)
The three core attributes Ramp looks for in product hires, emphasizing slope over experience

How it works: 1. High slope, not intercept. Hire people who will succeed 6 months from now when scope is bigger. Requires incredible learning ability and growth mindset. Greatly discount domain expertise in favor of intrinsic curiosity. PMs need to love problem-solving.

2. High agency. Hire former (and future) founders. Three PMs who left Ramp all started companies. High agency doesn't come from ruthlessness — it comes from self-awareness: understanding and reshaping the limiting stories we tell ourselves of why something can't be done.

3. High humility. Because taking risks demands humor, kindness, and gratitude. Because we focus on asking the right questions, not having all answers. Because PMs are first sales and support hires and must roll up their sleeves. Because we empower people closest to the problem.

### Three Factors That Delay First PM Hire (When to hire your first product manager)
Conditions that explain why some successful companies wait longer than average to hire their first PM

How it works: 1. Founders with deep product sense (e.g. Shopify) — The founder can effectively serve as PM longer.
2. Building a product that isn't primarily user-facing (e.g. Stripe) — Less complexity in user research and experience design.
3. Long early beta period (e.g. Airtable) — Extended development phase before scaling where PM work is more limited.

### Three Signs You Need a Full-Time PM (When to hire your first product manager)
A diagnostic framework to evaluate whether it's time to hire your first product manager, based on three observable signals

How it works: Sign 1: 🐌 Frequent bottlenecks — Team members (usually engineering and design) are frequently unable to ship because of you (the founder/CEO). Example: Ryan Hoover at Product Hunt realized he was the bottleneck and should have hired a PM sooner.

Sign 2: ⚔️ Frequent misalignment — Team members are frequently out of sync on priorities, timelines, or the problem being solved. Look for: mistakes from ideas not being thought through, conflict between team members, general misalignment. Example: Microsoft created PM for Excel when developers were writing and rewriting code because ideas hadn't been connected across the team.

Sign 3: 💡 A burning need to go deep on a problem (post PMF) — You've found product-market fit and need to get ahead on a key initiative. Examples: Twitter and Snapchat each hired their first PM to lead monetization. Zimride hired their first PM to free up founders to invent Lyft.

### Typical First PM Hire Benchmarks (When they hired their first PM)
Data-driven benchmarks from 30 companies on when the first PM was hired

How it works: Typical timing: 2-3 years after founding. Typical team size: 10-15 engineers, 15-25 total employees. More than half hired their first PM before finding product-market fit (especially B2B). Most first PMs were IC PMs or senior PMs. Almost a fourth were engineers. Very few were directors, VPs, or heads of product. No founder regretted bringing on a PM, though some regretted waiting too long (e.g., Notion).

## Templates

### Custom Economic Outcomes Notion Doc (Winning at early-stage hiring)
A Notion document template used by Vori to present candidates with a detailed economic case for joining the startup, used during the offer/closing stage

How it works: Three-part document:
1. Market Structure & Opportunity Valuation: Outlines the structure of the market and how the company values the opportunity
2. Gradient of Potential Outcomes: Walks through various end-state scenarios ranging from 'impending death' to 'IPOing as the Alibaba of the West' (or equivalent ambitious outcome)
3. Total Compensation Comparison: Compares the candidate's current TC (estimated via Glassdoor) to the startup's package

Key principle: Be candid about risks and the low likelihood of success in startups generally, and the specific challenges the company will face. This candor builds trust and actually makes candidates believe in you more.

### Custom Offer Deck (Winning at early-stage hiring)
A personalized slide deck presented to each candidate during the offer stage, walked through with all founders present

How it works: At the offer stage, present a custom offer deck and walk through it with all founders and the candidate present. The level of detail makes candidates feel welcomed and loved. Treat this like an investor pitch deck but tailored to the specific candidate.

### The 'No Agenda' Networking Message (Lauren Ipsen)
A low-pressure outreach approach to build relationships with top talent before you have an open role.

How it works: Message structure: State clearly that there is no agenda, you are not trying to hire them tomorrow, you have heard great things about them, and you simply want to know great people and pick their brain.

## Checklists

### 3 Signals Your Team is Telling You to Hire a PM (Joining as the first product manager)
Ken Norton's signals from the team that indicate it's time to hire the first PM

How it works: Your team will tell you when it's time through these signals:
1. 'You're slowing us down.' — The founder is becoming a bottleneck
2. 'You're detached from the details.' — The founder can't stay close enough to the product
3. 'The rest of our business needs you.' — The founder's time is needed elsewhere
Note: Ken recommends waiting until after product/market fit and erring on the side of hiring later rather than earlier.

### 3 Tactics to Close PM Candidates (How to interview product managers)
Three proven strategies from founders for convincing top candidates to accept your offer

How it works: 1. Surprise Zoom celebration (Julianna Lamb, CTO of Stytch): 'We see the actual offer stage as a big place you can stand out. We do a Zoom to surprise the candidate with everyone from their interview panel to share why they're excited about the candidate potentially joining.'

2. Treat closing like closing an investor (Siqi Chen, CEO of Runway): 'We treat closing a candidate almost exactly the same as closing an investor. The best candidates will do even more diligence than the average investor. Candidates are investing something even more valuable than capital: their time. Our Series A pitch/demo is pretty much exactly the pitch/demo we do for candidates.'

3. Commit ~100 hours per hire (Sam Corcos, CEO of Levels): 'Be prepared to commit ~100 hours for each hire you make. Interview a lot of people. Ask your network for introductions. Reach out on LinkedIn. Get creative on sourcing. Write excellent job descriptions. There are no shortcuts.'

### 30-Day Hiring Evaluation (Uri Levine)
A calendar-based system to force hard firing decisions within the first month of every new hire.

How it works: Steps: 1) When you hire someone new, immediately set a calendar reminder for 30 days. 2) On day 30, ask: 'Knowing what I know today, would I hire this person?' 3) If YES: Go to the person, tell them they're exceeding expectations, give them more equity — buy their loyalty for life. 4) If NO: Fire them immediately — they're on a trajectory of failure and creating damage to the team. 5) If unsure: Ask top performers 'Assuming [person] is going to leave, how sore are you going to feel?' Their answer provides confirmation.

### 4-Step Headcount Justification Process (How Ramp builds product)
A structured process to avoid organizational bloat by exhausting alternatives before hiring

How it works: Step 1 — Value: Will focusing on this opportunity accelerate progress toward our OKRs?

Step 2 — Deflection: If yes, can we accomplish this with automation or do we really need a person? (Example: Ramp's Outbound Automation Team gave SDRs 'superpowers' and outbound became their largest growth channel)

Step 3 — Leverage: If not automatable, is there someone in the organization who can do this job today? (Examples: Customer Success led by a founding engineer; Head of People started in risk operations)

Step 4 — Hire: If none of the above work, hire the best, and hold the manager accountable for the impact defined.

### Bonus Tactical Hiring Advice (Winning at early-stage hiring)
Five additional tactical tips from successful early-stage founders for improving hiring outcomes

How it works: 1. Define values and ways of working first (Nick Handel, Transform): The team determines the culture. Clearly articulate how you want to work and what values you want to protect so recruits can self-select into an environment they'll thrive in.
2. Use unfair advantages in your network (Kevin Tan, Snackpass): Identify unique candidate pools others don't have access to. Snackpass recruited directly from their college-campus users who already understood and loved the product.
3. Treat closing candidates like closing investors (Siqi Chen, Runway): Best candidates do even more diligence than the average investor. Use the same pitch and demo you'd use for Series A fundraising. Candidates are investing something more valuable than capital: their time.
4. Make specific asks when tapping your network (Sam Corcos, Levels): Don't say 'send us more engineers.' Define the specific persona you're looking for—what role, what skills, how many people. Specific asks yield much better results.
5. Create a custom economic outcomes Notion doc (Brandon Hill, Vori): Show market structure, gradient of potential outcomes from failure to IPO, and compare their current TC to your offer. Be candid about risks—this builds trust.

### Employer Checklist for PM Talent Attraction and Retention (How much do U.S. product managers really make?)
Strategies for employers to attract and retain PM talent based on compensation survey insights

How it works: 1. Understand that experienced senior ICs command salaries rivaling management roles
2. Offer competitive salaries for IC tracks, not just management tracks
3. Create clear growth paths and leveling for ICs (titles, rubrics, progression)
4. Provide well-rounded compensation packages (salary + equity + benefits + work-life balance)
5. Include clear and competitive salary information in job postings (especially as pay transparency laws spread)
6. Recognize that paying more money alone won't guarantee employee satisfaction (correlation is only 0.31)
7. Benchmark against market data by level, function, and geography — not by years of experience

### First PM Hiring Criteria (When they hired their first PM)
Key traits and considerations when evaluating your first PM candidate

How it works: Look for:
1. Strong IC superpower (e.g., design/UX, technical depth, or subject-matter expertise in your field)
2. Passion for the problem and the customer/ecosystem—PMs benefit from this more than other roles
3. Good product intuition—ability to translate customer requests into what the product should actually deliver
4. Willingness to work in a fluid, scrappy environment without rigid role definitions
5. Cross-functional chemistry—ensure engineering leads and design leads have a say in hiring

Consider internal transfers:
- They already have deep product knowledge and customer understanding
- Trust is pre-built
- Companies that did this successfully: Notion, Looker, Figma, Robinhood, Segment, Amplitude, Linear
- Contract-to-hire can work well as a lower-risk approach (Linear example)

### Internal Team Recruiting Playbook (How to ship like a startup)
How to recruit the right people to join your product initiative from across the org

How it works: 1. Find your early adopters: If you need to sell someone really really hard to join, they're probably not the right fit. The best teammates are the ones begging their managers to work on your project. Make your project known across the org and ideally the whole company so they can find you.
2. Look for 'run with it' energy: The best team isn't always the most senior—it's the one that can move forward without perfect specs. Find engineers and designers who thrive in ambiguity.
3. Recruit scrappily: Don't let org swimlanes prevent you from reaching out. Work with engineers and designers both on and off your team. If they like working with you, they'll make time. If they love working with you, they'll do whatever they can to join.
4. Empower every team member to pitch: Your team should be your biggest evangelists. The best pitches come from folks on your team. Ensure they know when recruiting is a priority and that you are available as a resource.

### Non-Obvious Recruiting Strategies for Startups (How Ramp builds product)
Four strategies for finding and hiring top talent that big tech can't replicate because they don't scale

How it works: 1. Find early talent before anyone else. Focus on young talent with early signs of being a founder — winning programming competitions in high school, building businesses in college, dropping out to work at startups. CTO Karim found engineers playing Fortnite. Build a powerful campus brand through college internship program to start a talent flywheel.

2. Allow only the best to interview. Get extremely explicit about what you're looking for to avoid implicit bias. Standardize interview process and attributes. Long interviewing apprenticeship process with debriefs after each step. Build a broad base of signals while warding off implicit bias.

3. Don't hire managers, hire stellar individual contributors. You can learn to manage over time, but if you're not a good PM/designer/engineer, you can't set the bar. The best ICs will manage. All managers first earn respect by showcasing IC mastery. All managers, including VP of Product, own some IC workstreams.

4. Always place new PMs on a team with top performers. Maximally sets them up for success. If you've only changed one variable on a team of all-stars, you immediately know if there's a problem to coach through.

### PLG Talent Hiring Phased Approach (Five steps to starting your product-led growth motion)
A three-step phased approach to building PLG expertise and talent in your organization

How it works: Phase 1: Hire a seasoned PLG growth advisor — ideally with relevant experience to your industry/segment. Validates PLG fit and jump-starts the motion.

Phase 2: Identify internal talent — People who may not have done PLG before but have the passion and skill set. Example: Hila's advisory client's internal PLG leader was a former head of investor relationships (analytical, can mobilize resources across the company).

Phase 3: Hire externally for a growth leader or key growth talent — Once PLG is validated and you have early wins, hire the best-fit growth leader/team for your specific needs.

Rationale: Start with phases 1 and 2 to validate fit, get early wins, and avoid moving too quickly too prematurely.

### PM Hiring Criteria for Marketplace Teams (Tim Holley)
Three key traits Tim evaluates when hiring product managers at Etsy

How it works: 1) Collaboration excitement — not just willingness but genuine excitement to consult and incorporate multiple viewpoints before deciding; 2) Decisiveness — ability to make calls and move forward when data is ambiguous, owning the decision and its consequences; 3) Curiosity/growth mindset — willingness to work on new/different domains, learn from unfamiliar problems, adapt to shifting priorities in a small org.

### PM Readiness Evaluation (When to hire your first product manager)
Questions to determine if your current team can handle PM responsibilities or if you need a dedicated hire

How it works: For each of the three PM jobs (Shape, Ship, Synchronize), evaluate:
1. Is someone currently filling this role?
2. Are they good at this job?
3. Do they want to do this job?
If the answer is 'no' to any of these for any of the three jobs, you're better off hiring a full-time PM.

### Product Talent Portfolio Mapping (Eeke de Milliano)
A bi-annual exercise to assess and balance the skills of a product management team.

How it works: Every six months, chart out the current team, list all strengths and weaknesses, and specifically open headcount to hire for the missing skills (e.g., balancing homegrown culture carriers with external rigorous PMs).

### Recruiter Calibration Test (Lauren Ipsen)
A method for founders to evaluate if an executive recruiter is capable and aligned with their needs before signing them.

How it works: Step 1: Provide direction on the role and what you are looking for. Step 2: Ask them to recite it back to test their listening skills and comprehension. Step 3: Put them on the spot and ask for 2-3 real-time candidate ideas to test their network and calibration speed.

### Three Conditions When Web3 Startups Should Hire a PM (A product manager’s guide to web3)
Criteria for when a web3 startup actually needs a product manager

How it works: Hire a PM when:
1. ENGINEERS HAVE BECOME INEFFICIENT: Engineering team has grown too large to prioritize and self-coordinate projects; PM adds efficiency (e.g., multiple L1 protocols and EVM solutions).
2. THE PRODUCT EXPERIENCE IS COMPLEX OR CRITICAL TO TRACTION: Product suite is large and UX is complex/consumer-facing, as in gaming (e.g., Axie Infinity). Products competing by cloning + adding tokens need less PM support.
3. THERE'S A MIX OF EXTERNALLY FACING ACTIVITIES: Marketing, growth, BD, and community work the devs don't want and doesn't need a dedicated hire — a versatile PM can help. Over time specialist PMs needed (e.g., L1 like Flow, marketplace like NBA Top Shot).

### Three Questions to Decide If You Need a PM (When they hired their first PM)
Self-assessment questions for founders deciding whether to push out or accelerate their first PM hire

How it works: If you're looking to push out your first PM hire, the questions you need to ask yourself are:
1. Do you *want* to be doing this work?
2. Are you *good* at it?
3. What are you *not* spending time on that you could be, if you had a PM?

### When to Hire Your First PM — 3 Conditions (Joining as the first product manager)
Jonathan Golden's (first PM at Airbnb) three conditions that must all be met before hiring a first PM

How it works: Hire your first PM when ALL three conditions are met:
1. You've achieved product/market fit and need to scale
2. Your engineering team is greater than seven people
3. You (the founder) are mentally ready to let someone else control the roadmap at some level
This typically happens right after Series A financing.

### Linear's In-House Recruiting Over Agencies (Hiring your early team)

### Real-World First PM Hiring Stories (When to hire your first product manager)
