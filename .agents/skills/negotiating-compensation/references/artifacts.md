## Frameworks

### Compensation Evaluation Priority Framework (Why cash is king)
A hierarchy of what tech workers prioritize in compensation packages, based on 5,000+ survey responses, organized into three categories: salary, equity, and other levers

How it works: Priority breakdown from survey (n=5,035):
- 75% prioritize salary over equity
- 13% prioritize equity over salary
- 4% prioritize other considerations beyond salary or equity
- 8% N/A or preferred not to answer

Key reasons for salary priority:
1. Salary = stability: Predictable cash flow needed for bills, HCOL areas, family responsibilities
2. Equity skepticism: Past experiences of equity not materializing, viewed as 'lottery ticket' or 'Monopoly money'

Other levers beyond salary and equity (three categories):
- Financial options: Sign-on bonuses, performance-based bonuses, 401(k)/pension plans
- Non-financial benefits: Vacation days, work-life flexibility, insurance, family planning benefits
- Career growth: Titles, mentoring opportunities, L&D funds, opportunities for early promotion

### Decision-Maker Meeting Preparation Framework (The 10 commandments of salary negotiation)
How to prepare for follow-up meetings with senior decision makers to build your negotiation leverage

How it works: Who you can request meetings with:
- Director+: Any VP and possibly C-level execs
- VP+: CEO and even board members

Come prepared with three things (tailored to who you're meeting):
1. Questions about how you can create meaningful impact
2. Ideas based on your interviews so far
3. Bonus: Discuss obstacles to your taking the role and make them sell you on it

Purpose: If everyone wants you, you'll be calling the shots in negotiation later.

### How to Read a Box Plot for Salary Benchmarking (How much do U.S. product managers really make?)
A guide to interpreting box plots used for salary data analysis, as presented in the newsletter

How it works: Components of a salary box plot:
- Box (IQR): Shows the interquartile range — the middle 50% of data. More informative than full range as it's not sensitive to outliers.
- Left edge of box: 25th percentile (25% of data points below this value)
- Right edge of box: 75th percentile (25% of data points above this value)
- Line inside box (Median): The 50th percentile / midpoint of data. If not centered, data may be skewed. Focus on median over average.
- Arrow inside box (Average): Less reliable than median in salary data because it's affected by outliers and skewed distributions.
- Whiskers: Extend to the smallest and largest non-outlier data points within 1.5x the IQR. Show overall range.
- Small circles: Outliers beyond the whiskers, highlighting unusual/extreme salary values.

Key interpretation tips:
- Wider boxes = more spread in the middle 50% of salaries
- Senior roles show wider distributions with more outliers
- Entry/mid-level roles show tighter, more consistent distributions

### Initial Offer Interpretation Framework (The 10 commandments of salary negotiation)
How to interpret your initial offer level at a large company and determine your negotiation strategy

How it works: For an L6 role at a big company:

- LOW OFFER: Team felt you have 'room for growth.' Strategy: Ask interviewer to share feedback from interviewers to fix misconceptions BEFORE negotiating. Don't argue you deserve more if team felt you were 'meh.'

- MIDDLE OF THE ROAD (80%+ of people): You got 'the number' — the standard opening offer used to reduce lawsuit risk. Likely means no strong advocate on interview loop. Strategy: Do NOT negotiate until you match with a team and have a manager batting for you.

- TOP OF BAND: Likely was a discussion about giving you a higher level. Strategy: Push for 'out-of-band' offer — getting paid at L7 level while being an L6.

### Negotiation Myths vs. Reality (The 10 commandments of salary negotiation)
Six common negotiation myths debunked with the correct approach

How it works: MYTH 1: 'I need a competing offer.' REALITY: You don't need multiple offers. Just say you're speaking to other companies; you can quote expected salaries for other roles.

MYTH 2: 'I need to provide copies of my other offers.' REALITY: No. You signed NDAs before every interview — use that as your reason to decline.

MYTH 3: 'I should send an email with my ask and justification.' REALITY: Email negotiation leads to worse outcomes. Have the conversation over the phone.

MYTH 4: 'If I find a number online, I can quote it as a reason to get more.' REALITY: Nothing angers recruiters more than 'It says X on Glassdoor.' Have arguments specific to your situation.

MYTH 5: 'The best way to get more is to reiterate how qualified I am.' REALITY: They already know your qualifications — that's how you got the offer. Instead, use interview intel about team challenges to increase scope, discuss why leaving your current role is hard, create obstacles the recruiter needs to overcome to close you.

MYTH 6: 'I need to be aggressive and threaten to walk.' REALITY: You'll get a mediocre increase worded as a 'final offer.' For $100K+ increases, collaborate with your recruiter, don't make them an enemy.

### Negotiation Sequencing Strategy (The 10 commandments of salary negotiation)
The correct order in which to negotiate compensation components

How it works: Step 1: Negotiate overall total compensation first (e.g., ask for '500K')
Step 2: Then negotiate individual components (e.g., 'Can I have X more equity?')
Step 3: When all other avenues are exhausted, ask for a signing bonus last

### Salary-as-Foundation Strategy (Why cash is king)
The argument that maximizing base salary is a long-term wealth-building strategy, not a short-term move, because other compensation components compound off of it

How it works: Core insight: Base salary serves as the foundation for total compensation growth.

How salary compounds:
1. Bonuses are structured as percentages of base salary → higher base = higher bonuses
2. Raises are often calculated as percentages of base → higher base = larger raises
3. A higher salary at current role sets a higher bar/anchor for your next role's negotiation
4. Provides immediate financial stability while still allowing investment for long-term wealth

Conclusion: Prioritizing salary doesn't mean missing out on long-term wealth—it can be the strategic way to build it.

### The 10 Commandments of Salary Negotiation (The 10 commandments of salary negotiation, The Best of Lenny’s Newsletter—2024 Edition)
A sequential 10-step framework for negotiating compensation at tech companies, from first recruiter call through final ask

How it works: 1. Negotiation starts earlier than you think — Don't give a number when asked about salary expectations. Ask for the salary band instead. Script: 'Can you tell me the salary band for this level? Happy to let you know if it's within my range, and we can discuss specific numbers later when I've met the team.' Bonus: Time interviews to get offers simultaneously (junior/mid); get press before meeting people (senior).
2. Mine for intel during interviews — Ask strategic questions: What's the biggest priority for the team? Why is this role open? What's the biggest challenge for someone stepping in? How does the org structure work?
3. Don't give in to the pressure — Post-offer, recruiters shift to closing mode. Don't give your number even when they say 'I'll go to bat for you.'
4. At FAANG, your recruiter may have no say — Compensation committees set salary formulaically. Use 'new information' (another offer, retention counter-offer) to reopen negotiation.
5. Read between the lines — Low offer: ask for interview feedback before negotiating. Middle offer (80% of people get this): don't negotiate until you have a manager advocate. Top-of-band: push for out-of-band offer (paid at next level).
6. At a startup, the playbook is different — Understand financing/runway/profitability, the plan for your role, and equity value. Ask for strike price, preferred price, outstanding shares. Ask 'What is the valuation based on?' and expect to ask 5 times.
7. Win hearts and minds — Before counteroffering, ask for follow-up meetings with decision makers (VPs, C-level, even board). Prepare: questions about meaningful impact, ideas from interviews, obstacles to taking the role.
8. Get good data — Evaluate: Is offer competitive? How does vesting work? Is equity value over-inflated? Consider taxes on bonuses and stock. Are goaled comp targets realistic?
9. Compare offers holistically — Beyond comp: company trajectory, promotion mechanics, manager influence, team visibility/resourcing, company brand value to earnings trajectory.
10. Make the ask — Negotiate by phone, not email. Don't need competing offers (mentioning you're interviewing elsewhere suffices). Don't share offer copies. Don't cite Glassdoor. Don't just reiterate qualifications — use interview intel about team challenges to create obstacles the recruiter must overcome. Collaborate, don't threaten. Negotiate total comp first, then equity, then signing bonus last.

### Three Key Drivers of PM Compensation (How much do U.S. product managers really make?)
The three factors that have the biggest impact on PM salary, used to structure compensation analysis and benchmarking

How it works: The three biggest drivers of PM salary are:
1. Function (product management vs. other roles)
2. Geography (U.S. vs. non-U.S. — U.S. skews significantly higher)
3. Level (job level within the IC or management hierarchy)

Anti-pattern: Years of experience is NOT a reliable predictor. Per Pave CEO Matt Schulman: 'A product professional with 10 years of experience could be at an IC 5 level at one company and an IC 3 level at another. The more important relationship is between level and salary, and years of experience is only a correlated variable to job level.'

## Checklists

### Interview Intel-Gathering Questions (The 10 commandments of salary negotiation)
Questions to ask during interviews to gather negotiation ammunition

How it works: 1. What's the biggest priority for the team right now?
2. Why is this role open?
3. What's the biggest challenge for someone stepping into this role?

### Offer Comparison Criteria (Beyond Compensation) (The 10 commandments of salary negotiation)
Factors to consider when comparing multiple job offers beyond just the dollar amount

How it works: 1. Which company has a better trajectory?
2. How do promotions work?
3. Is your manager influential enough to pull for you when needed?
4. Is your product or team visible enough to get good resourcing?
5. What's the company brand worth to your earnings trajectory?

TL;DR: Getting paid more up-front doesn't always mean you'll make the most overall.

### Offer Evaluation Checklist (The 10 commandments of salary negotiation)
Five questions to ask yourself to fully evaluate an offer before negotiating, especially important for women

How it works: 1. Is the offer competitive? (Browse offers on Candor's database of 5.3M offers)
2. How does it work? (When do I vest? What is the stock worth?)
3. Is it all true? (Is the value of the equity over-inflated?)
4. Does it add up? (Consider taxes on bonuses and stock)
5. If you have goaled comp, are the goals realistic?

### PM Compensation Optimization Decision Checklist (How much product managers make in the U.S., Europe, and Canada)
Strategic guidelines distilled from the salary data to help PMs maximize their compensation

How it works: 1. To maximize base salary (stability): Join a public company in a Tier 1 U.S. city. 2. To maximize upside: Get into a senior IC or manager role at a private company in the U.S. 3. If choosing between IC and management: Know that new managers (M3) make LESS than senior ICs ($265K vs. $425K total comp). Management only pays more at M5+. 4. Target the biggest compensation jumps: L4→L5, M3→M4, and VP→CPO are the inflection points. 5. If in Europe: Aim for U.K. or Ireland for highest comp. Even so, expect 65% (U.K.) or <50% (rest of Europe) of U.S. comp. 6. If at a private company: Expect 15-20% lower base but potentially much higher equity (especially at 90th percentile senior roles). 7. Equity growth accelerates at senior levels in private companies: M3 ~$200K → M4 ~$500K → M5 ~$800K → M6 ~$1M (90th percentile).

### Salary Maximization Strategies for PM Job Seekers (How much do U.S. product managers really make?)
Actionable strategies for product managers to maximize their salary based on survey data and expert advice

How it works: 1. Focus on advancing your job level — level is the strongest predictor of salary, not years of experience
2. Don't assume management always means higher pay — senior ICs often out-earn managers
3. If you're an experienced IC who enjoys IC work, leverage your expertise to negotiate strong comp without moving into management
4. Find companies with a 'nexus of great people' where you'll learn the most (Tamar Yehoshua) rather than chasing highest pay
5. Focus on 'moving the business forward' rather than just hitting goals or demonstrating next-level skills (Tamar Yehoshua)
6. Do a really good job at whatever your current job is, however simple it may seem (Tamar Yehoshua)
7. Advocate for differentiated senior IC titles at your company to create career path and compensation progression (Tal Raviv)
8. Push for clear levels and rubrics for IC roles, just as you would for management tracks (Tal Raviv)
9. Remember salary isn't everything — consider equity, benefits, and work-life balance for overall satisfaction
10. Use level-based benchmarks (not experience-based) when researching and negotiating salary

### Startup Equity Evaluation Checklist (The 10 commandments of salary negotiation)
Questions to ask when evaluating equity at a startup, treating yourself as an investor

How it works: Understand three things:
1. Current state of the company (financing, runway, profitability)
2. The plan for your role/function
3. The value of the equity considering exit scenarios

Data to request from recruiter:
- Option strike price
- Preferred price
- Number of outstanding shares
- 'What is the valuation based on?' (expect to ask ~5 times before getting a straight answer)

Additional steps:
- Ask to meet with the company's investors (100% OK for early-stage companies)
- Use Holloway Guide ranges as a starting point for typical equity levels
