# Defining Product Strategy

Transform vision into a concrete plan of action by making hard choices that drive customer behavior.

Help the user with defining product strategy using insights from 27 guests and posts across Lenny's Podcast and Newsletter.

## How to Help

1. **Diagnose the crux** - Help the user identify the primary obstacle or high-stakes challenge that is currently holding the product back.
2. **Guide the choice cascade** - Walk the user through defining where they will compete and how they will uniquely win in that space.
3. **Structure the narrative** - Assist in distilling complex directions into a simple 'why' and a set of memorable strategic pillars.
4. **Stress-test the plan** - Use AI or external critiques to identify blind spots, common consensus thinking, or lack of defensibility.

## Core Principles

### Distill to a simple 'why'
Anneka Gupta: "When people say, 'I want someone that's strategic,' what they're really saying is, 'I want someone that can come up with and articulate a compelling and simple why behind the decisions and the direction of the company and product.' So that's number one. And the second piece is, 'I want someone that's going to champion and be a change agent to do things that may be hard but actually best for the long-term interest of the product or company, even though those things are not going to be easy to execute on.'"

True leadership requires the ability to take complex company directions and compress them into a single, compelling narrative that provides clear purpose for the team.

### Strategy as an integrated set of choices
Annie Pearl: "Strategy is really just an integrated set of choices that outline how you're going to win in whatever marketplace you choose. And so, a good product strategy is going to answer questions like what's your winning aspiration? But maybe more importantly, where are you going to play?"

Avoid lists of goals by defining exactly where the company will compete and the specific mechanisms it will use to win in that marketplace.

### Challenge conventional wisdom
Dharmesh Shah: "Some of the best startup advice I've heard is startups should focus on one thing and be really, really exceptionally world-class at that one thing. And one of our early zigs is we are going to do exactly the opposite of that."

Significant success often comes from making high-conviction bets that intentionally diverge from standard industry advice regarding product scope.

### Prioritize clarity over perfection
Jag Duggal: "We may not be right, but at least we are clear. Even if your strategy isn't right, you have a very clear idea of what was supposed to be happening."

Being clear about the intended path is more valuable than being certain of the outcome because it allows for faster learning when expectations and reality diverge.

### Diagnosis, policy, and action
Richard Rumelt: "Well, a strategy is a design for overcoming a high-stakes challenge. It's a mixture of policy and action designed to deal with a challenge."

A legitimate strategy defines the high-stakes challenge through a diagnosis, sets a guiding policy for dealing with it, and outlines coherent actions to take.

## Questions to Help Users

- "What is the specific, high-stakes challenge or obstacle that is holding your product back from its next level of growth?"
- "Which customer segments or market opportunities are you intentionally choosing to ignore to maintain focus?"
- "How will your product be 10x better or uniquely defensible compared to the nearest competitor?"
- "Can you summarize your strategy in a single, simple sentence that a new hire could use to make an independent trade-off?"
- "What are the foundational megatrends or shifts in technology that make this specific strategy viable right now?"
- "If you had to pick just one 'crux' problem to solve this quarter, what would it be?"

## Common Mistakes to Flag

- **Mistaking goals for strategy** - Listing ambitious performance targets without identifying the actual obstacles or explaining how to overcome them creates planning theater.
- **Relying on speed as power** - Operational excellence and 'moving fast' are necessary for survival but do not provide the structural barriers required for a durable strategy.
- **Solving for everyone at once** - Horizontal products often fail by trying to solve one problem for everyone rather than deeply capturing the workflow of a specific audience.
- **Lack of high-stakes diagnosis** - Teams often skip the hard work of diagnosing the reality of their situation, leading to strategies that address symptoms rather than root causes.
