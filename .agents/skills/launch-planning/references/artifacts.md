## Frameworks

### 10 Strategies for Generating Launch Buzz (Generating buzz)
A categorized framework of the ten most common strategies companies use to launch something 'worth remarking about,' based on analysis of 49 real-life examples. Used to brainstorm and select a launch strategy.

How it works: The 10 strategies for launching something worth remarking about:

1. Release a remarkable video — Create a viral video that showcases your product in an entertaining or unexpected way (Examples: Poo Pourri, Descript, Dollar Shave Club)

2. Create a remarkable product demo — Build a demo so compelling people share it before the product even launches (Examples: mmhmm, Dropbox, Mailbox)

3. Offer a remarkable value prop — Make your core offering so different from the status quo that it's inherently newsworthy (Examples: Robinhood '$0 commission stock brokerage', Dropbox 'Throw away your USB drive', Gmail 'Free email with 1GB of storage', Netflix 'No Due Dates, No Late Fees', Warby Parker 'Try on five pairs of frames at home for free before buying any', Spotify 'All of the world's music – for free', Coin 'Replace eight of your credit cards with just one', Eero 'Finally, WiFi that works')

4. Execute a remarkable offline stunt — Do something in the physical world so unexpected it generates press and social sharing (Examples: Half.com renamed a town in Oregon, Salesforce hired fake protesters and commandeered taxis, WePay dropped 600 lbs of ice at PayPal's conference, Snapchat dropped vending machines at random LA locations, Tommee Tippee turned parenting books into baby wipes)

5. Start a remarkable controversy — Launch something that provokes debate or seems absurd (Examples: Yo app raising $1M, Quibi raising ~$2B pre-product, Hey.com calling out Apple, Stolen letting you 'buy' friends on Twitter, Zillow Zestimates)

6. Offer a remarkable giveaway — Give away something so unexpected it generates attention (Examples: Push for Pizza's pizza scholarship delivered by Lamborghini, Fast's $5 hoodies, Uniswap's free token distribution)

7. Include a remarkable viral mechanic — Build referral loops with escalating rewards into your launch (Examples: Jet.com gave away 100k stock options to top referrer → 350k pre-launch users; Dropbox gave free storage per referral → 100k to 4M users in 15 months; Harry's tiered free product rewards → 100k emails in one week; Robinhood priority access referrals → 1M users before launch; Ritual $1 lunch for referrals)

8. Get a remarkable hard-to-get product shared by influencers or press — Combine invite-only scarcity with influencer seeding (Examples: Hey.com DHH promotion + invite-only → 180k waitlist; Superhuman visible in email signatures + invite-only → 275k waitlist; Clubhouse tech influencer mentions + invite-only; ThirdLove press + influencer marketing → 1.3M waitlist; OnePlus One invitation-only cheap smartphone → 1.5M units in 12 months; Mailbox known founder + visible waitlist → 800k waitlist)

9. Carry out a remarkable pre-launch tease — Build mystery and anticipation before revealing the product (Examples: 👁️👄👁️ cryptic campaign → 20k email signups + $200k for charity; Chobani fruit countdown on Instagram; Segway year of speculation before reveal)

10. Be remarkably everywhere — Saturate a specific channel or location (Examples: Toucan had investors/friends each 'claim' a word and tweet → 100% MoM growth; Venmo mysterious 'Lucas uses Venmo' subway posters → 40% increase in daily signups; Twitter/Foursquare visualization screens at SXSW)

Bonus: Maintain a remarkable ongoing drum-beat of buzz (Examples: MSCHF, Levels, Stir, Fast, Lambda School)

### 4-Part Go-To-Market Framework (Jonathan Lowenhar)
A simplified way to build a GTM motion by breaking it into four sequential buckets.

How it works: 1. Ideal Customer Profile (qualifications, discovery questions, kill criteria), 2. Marketing/Positioning (uncommon denominator against competitors, branding), 3. Demand Gen (channels, high-impact/low-effort experiments), 4. Sales (playbook, discovery, demo, close).

### 5 Product Hunt Launch Myths (How to successfully launch on Product Hunt (when it’s right for your startup))
Five common misconceptions about Product Hunt launches that waste time and reduce impact

How it works: Myth 1: You need a well-known hunter to succeed. Reality: Hunter follower notifications no longer exist. Engaging products succeed on their own merits (examples: Dora AI, Bento, Framer AI, Amie).
Myth 2: Winning Product of the Week/Month is significantly more valuable than Product of the Day. Reality: Better to capitalize on Day attention by improving conversions and engaging new users.
Myth 3: More upvotes equals higher ranking. Reality: Algorithm considers upvote velocity, engagement quality (comments/replies), and geographic diversity of upvoters.
Myth 4: Only new products can launch. Reality: Significant updates, new features, and relaunches are welcome. Companies can launch multiple times per year.
Myth 5: Direct linking to your product page will get you penalized. Reality: Linking to your PH page is allowed and encouraged.

### 7 Things to Get Right to Win on Product Hunt (How to successfully launch on Product Hunt (when it’s right for your startup))
Leo Bosuener's comprehensive 7-step framework for a successful Product Hunt launch, validated by Product Hunt CEO

How it works: 1. Set the right goals: Aim for conversion over popularity. Set business KPIs (website visits, signups, trial conversions) not just ranking.
2. Warm up your audience: Start 30 days before launch. Build anticipation, get PH accounts created early, engage high-value users, leverage time zones, engage maker network.
3. Be strategic about launch day: Use time zones (3 major pushes: 12:01am PT, 7am PT, 2pm PT), schedule up to 10 email drips, stagger social media, recruit local influencers in different time zones.
4. Pick the right day of the week: Mon/Fri for higher rank with less competition; Tue/Wed/Thu for most traffic; Sat/Sun for best #1 chances.
5. Understand the PH algorithm: Factors include upvote velocity/timing, engagement quality, upvoter quality/karma, product quality. Avoid launch parties (same-location votes trigger spam), upvoting rings, bots.
6. Invest in the right assets: Tagline (action verb, clear value prop), high-quality images (with embedded captions, min 3), video (3-5 min product walkthrough, Loom is fine), maker's comment (origin story, pain points, differentiation, CTA with discount code).
7. Go beyond upvotes: Focus on quality comments and reviews that convert visitors. Get power users to write detailed reviews. High comment counts increase CTR.

### B2B SaaS GTM Decision Framework (GTM motions of 30 B2B SaaS companies)
A three-decision framework for planning your go-to-market motion, covering market segment, target persona, and acquisition approach

How it works: Three decisions to make when planning your GTM motion:

1. WHAT MARKET SEGMENT TO GO AFTER:
   - VSB (very small business): <10 employees
   - SMB (small or midsize business): 10-99 employees
   - Mid-market: 100-1,000 employees
   - Enterprise: >1,000 employees

2. WHO TO GO AFTER WITHIN THE ORGANIZATION:
   - Top-down: Founders, VP of x, Head of y, etc.
   - Bottom-up: Internal tools engineers, IC designers, etc.

3. HOW TO GET TO THEM:
   - Product-led: A self-serve product that users discover through SEO, ads, or referrals
   - Sales-led: A gated product that users discover through outbound sales

### Building in Public vs. Stealth Assessment (Startup to exit: Lessons from a first-time founder)
Framework for evaluating whether to build in stealth or public, with strong bias toward public

How it works: Core principle (from Bill Gurley): 'Shout from every rooftop, tell whomever will listen.'

**Why stealth is always a mistake:**
- Stealthiness inhibits frequent market signal
- Greatly increases time to PMF
- Any perceived competitive gains from secrecy are lost by taking longer to achieve PMF
- For companies building new markets, competitors may actually be valuable in evangelizing the category ('if a rising tide lifts all boats, you want the tide to rise faster')

**Why building in public wins:**
- Startups don't win because their idea is original — they win because of hard-earned execution
- In early days, many people will think your idea is dumb (doesn't matter — they're not your customers)
- Building publicly invites inbounds from customers, employees, and investors
- A subset of these inbounds are typically accelerants for your business

**Costs of stealth (Savvy's experience):**
- Delayed sales inbounds from customers
- Delayed understanding of which segments most needed the product
- Delayed market feedback
- Estimated ~6-month setback
- When speed is your only advantage as a startup, the costs of stealth far outweigh the benefits

### Figma's Influencer Identification and Nurture Process (How today's fastest growing B2B businesses found their first ten customers)
A step-by-step process for using social media data to identify influential users, build relationships through product feedback, and convert them into launch amplifiers

How it works: Step 1: Talk with every potential user you personally know
Step 2: Use social media (Twitter) to identify the most influential people in your target user base
Step 3: Filter the list to people who are personally inspirational to you
Step 4: Cold email or find warm introductions to these people
Step 5: Show them the product and collect feedback
Step 6: Keep them in the loop as the product evolves
Step 7: By launch, you've built relationships and many will amplify your message

### Launch Goals Framework (Ryan Hoover)
A framework for identifying the real goals of a product launch beyond customer acquisition, including recruiting, fundraising, feedback, partnerships, SEO, and team morale.

How it works: Before launching, ask 'Why are we launching?' Goals may include: 1) Customer acquisition, 2) Recruiting - getting talent excited about what you're building, 3) Fundraising - creating momentum and heat around a deal, 4) Feedback - from users and serendipitous sources, 5) Partnerships - encountering new partnership opportunities, 6) Team morale - giving the team a moment to celebrate and share with family, 7) SEO - positive articles and Product Hunt launches improve search rankings.

### Launch Metrics Maturity Progression (How Gong builds product)
A framework for evolving product metrics through four stages as a new product matures from pre-launch to post-launch

How it works: Stage 1 — Stability metrics: Technical reliability indicators. Example: connect rates for web dialer.
Stage 2 — Outcome metrics: Customer value delivery indicators. Example: number of meetings booked by a typical seller.
Stage 3 — Operational metrics: Deployment and adoption efficiency indicators. Example: how long it takes a new customer to launch.
Stage 4 — Business metrics: Revenue and growth indicators. Example: attach rates to existing customers, ARPU.
Each stage transition happens when confidence is established in the previous stage (e.g., 'as we became convinced that the product is stable, we focused our attention more on outcome metrics').

### Launch Scope Decision Framework (Feel vs. Features) (What working at Figma taught me about customer obsession)
Figma's approach to deciding what to include in a product launch, prioritizing polish and feel over feature completeness

How it works: Core principle: Limit launch to a core set of features that feel polished and well-designed, instead of cramming in features. Example: Figma's official launch excluded components, styles, AND multiplayer — they shipped 'just a simple design tool that lived in the browser and felt great.' Rationale: Starting with something that feels bad — even with all the right features — creates a credibility problem. You then spend the majority of your time convincing people that you aren't bad anymore. Starting with something that feels great — even with fewer features — builds trust. Customers believe you will continue to improve and add features based on their feedback. Decision test: If you're not sure whether to invest in feel vs. functionality, ask users. Zoom all the way out and ask: Do you like using our product? If not, why? Listen for responses like 'It just feels clunky' — don't dismiss this, lean into it.

### Lightning Strikes vs. Peanut Butter (Christopher Lochhead)
A marketing resource allocation model that favors massive, concentrated events over evenly distributed, continuous spending.

How it works: Stop spreading budget evenly across quarters ('peanut butter'). Plan 1-2 massive launches per year for B2B, or 2-3 for B2C ('lightning strikes'). Model the launch after Hollywood movie premieres. Focus all effort on being undeniable to the ideal customer profile for a short, concentrated window.

### Minimal Lovable Product (MLP) (Jiaona Zhang)
A framework for determining launch scope by focusing on high polish for fewer features rather than low polish for many.

How it works: Assess user alternatives (e.g., manual spreadsheets vs. competitor tools). Choose 5 features to execute perfectly with 'pixie dust' (delightful details like keyboard shortcuts or templates) instead of 15 viable but clunky features.

### Product Hunt Algorithm Factors (How to successfully launch on Product Hunt (when it’s right for your startup))
Key factors that influence Product Hunt's ranking algorithm, including what to do and what to avoid

How it works: Positive ranking factors:
- Upvotes: velocity and timing matter more than raw count. Steady stream > spike.
- Engagement: Comments and discussions show genuine community interest.
- Upvoter quality: Product Hunt highlights notable upvotes. Users with high 'karma' scores carry more weight.
- Product quality: Innovative, well-crafted products are valued.
- Geographic diversity of upvoters.

Pitfalls to avoid:
- Launch parties: Simultaneous votes from the same location trigger spam filters → shadow ban.
- Upvoting rings: Secretive community groups that coordinate upvotes are being cracked down on.
- Bots/fake accounts: New account floods (even from competitors) can trigger deranking.
- Sudden spike in votes raises algorithm flags.

If you notice influx of comments from new users (red balloon icon), use reporting function to mark as spam.

### Product Hunt Day-of-Week Selection Matrix (How to successfully launch on Product Hunt (when it’s right for your startup))
Framework for choosing the optimal launch day based on your campaign goals (ranking vs. traffic vs. brand awareness)

How it works: Monday & Friday: Great engagement metrics, significantly lower competition. Choose if higher rank is more important than traffic.
Tuesday, Wednesday & Thursday: Highest traffic metrics but fiercest competition. Choose if you're OK with lower rank but want maximum incoming traffic.
Saturday & Sunday: Absolute best chances for #1 rank, but less total traffic. Choose if priority is #1 badge for brand awareness, social proof, and credibility.
Most clients aiming for brand visibility choose Monday or Friday. Traffic-focused clients choose midweek. Note: launch date is locked once page goes live at 12:01 AM PT.

### Product Hunt Readiness Assessment (How to successfully launch on Product Hunt (when it’s right for your startup))
Criteria for determining whether a startup should invest time in a Product Hunt launch or pursue alternatives

How it works: Product Hunt is a good fit if you want to:
1. Catch the eye of tech bloggers, influencers, or investors to raise their next round
2. Drive a short-term growth boost
3. Receive genuine user feedback from tech enthusiasts
4. Leverage SEO juice from a high-authority backlink

Product Hunt may NOT be right if:
- You can't invest 50-120 hours in preparation
- You're looking for sustained increase in signups (paid ads may be more efficient)
- Your product doesn't have self-serve onboarding (especially B2B)
- Your target audience is not tech enthusiasts/early adopters
- 70% of founders who approach Leo's agency are told they aren't ready

Alternative: Paid ad campaigns are often more efficient for testing initial messaging or scaling marketing sustainably.

### Product Marketing vs. Product Ops Division of Labor (Christine Itwaru)
A mental model for separating the responsibilities of PMMs and Product Ops during a launch.

How it works: Product Marketing focuses on external positioning, lead gen, and helping the revenue team sell. Product Ops focuses on internal education, explaining added value, and teaching teams how the change impacts their specific day-to-day roles.

### Soft Launch vs. Hard Launch (Janna Bastow)
A process for decoupling development delivery dates from marketing launch dates to reduce stress and improve quality.

How it works: 1. Devs finish and release the feature (Soft Launch). 2. Marketers get access to the functional product to gather testimonials, create videos, and plan the campaign. 3. Marketing executes the public release (Hard Launch) on their own timeline while devs move to the next build.

### The 'Remarkable' Test (Seth Godin) (Generating buzz)
A mental model for evaluating whether your launch strategy will generate organic buzz — is it literally worth making a remark about?

How it works: Core insight: 'Remarkable' doesn't just mean 'neat' — it means 'worth making a remark about.' The test for any launch strategy is: Will someone who encounters this feel compelled to stop what they're doing, pay attention, and share it with their friends? If the answer is no, it won't generate buzz. As Marc Andreessen noted, 'everyone's time is already allocated' — so your launch must be remarkable enough to break through that allocation. Apply this test to every element of your launch: your video, your demo, your value prop, your stunt, your referral mechanic.

### The Launch Triangle (Quality, Features, Deadline) (Dylan Field)
A decision-making framework for scoping a new product launch.

How it works: When launching a new product, you can only choose two of the three constraints: Quality, Features, and Deadline. You can ship on time with high quality by cutting features, or ship all features on time by cutting quality, etc.

### Time-Zone Outreach Strategy (Three-Wave Model) (How to successfully launch on Product Hunt (when it’s right for your startup))
A structured approach to spreading Product Hunt engagement across the 24-hour launch window using three major pushes

How it works: Wave 1 - 12:01 AM Pacific Time: Launch announcement. Activate supporters in Asia-Pacific and early European time zones.
Wave 2 - 7:00 AM Pacific Time: U.S. users come online. Major push to American network, email lists, and social channels.
Wave 3 - 2:00 PM Pacific Time: Maintain afternoon momentum. Activate remaining network, late European contacts.

Additional tactics:
- Schedule up to 10 email blasts throughout the day, segmented by time zone
- Stagger social media posts following the sun around the globe
- Identify and recruit key influencers in different time zones in advance
- Engage entire network: employees, users, investors
- Prioritize engagement from long-standing PH accounts over new accounts

## Checklists

### 15-Minute Quality Polish Checklist (Zoelle Egner)
Quick quality assurance process before any public-facing content ships, to punch above your weight as a small company

How it works: Steps: 1) Have at least one other person reread every email before sending. 2) Click every link to make sure none are broken. 3) Invest in a decent photo or illustration (not placeholder). 4) Replace generic sample content (e.g., 'Jane Doe' x12) with industry-specific references that signal you built it for your target audience. 5) For blog posts: define three things to check every time. 6) Assign one person as the 'avatar of quality' who owns this process.

### 30-Day Pre-Launch Audience Warm-Up Checklist (How to successfully launch on Product Hunt (when it’s right for your startup))
Step-by-step checklist for preparing your audience in the 30 days before a Product Hunt launch

How it works: 1. Build anticipation: Let your community know you'll be launching on Product Hunt. Plant a seed that you'll ask for their help later.
2. Account creation: Encourage your network to create a Product Hunt account 30 days prior (the algorithm values engagement from seasoned members more than newcomers).
3. High-value user engagement: Focus on engaging users who are active on Product Hunt and have a significant following.
4. Global strategy: Identify supporters in multiple time zones to spread votes throughout the 24-hour period.
5. Maker network: Be active on Product Hunt in weeks leading up to launch. Support other makers' launches with meaningful feedback and comments (they reciprocate). Target users who recently launched (better response rates).
6. ICP targeting: Identify Product Hunt users within your ideal customer profile. Connect with them on LinkedIn. Tailor outreach to those for whom your product is most useful.
7. Craft outreach messages using templates.

### Caviar's 3-Step City Launch Playbook (Marketplace city expansion strategy)
Caviar's focused approach to launching new food delivery markets by targeting market-maker restaurants

How it works: 1. Identify the part of town with the highest density of restaurants and most affluent customers.
2. Focus disproportionate effort on signing up major 'market maker' restaurants — super-popular among locals with limited or no existing delivery.

### Community-Driven Product Launch Playbook (9 Steps) (What 5 years at Reddit taught us about building for a highly opinionated user base)
A step-by-step playbook for launching product features with community involvement at each stage, from initial concept through general availability.

How it works: Step 1: One-pager and Community Manager review
- PM completes 1-pager (Lenny's template recommended: https://docs.google.com/document/d/1541V32QgSwyCFWxtiMIThn-6n-2s7fVWztEWVa970uo/edit)
- PM meets Community Managers, reviews 1-pager
- Get CM feedback on potential issues or risks the community might raise

Step 2: Product spec writing and design process initiation
- Write product spec incorporating Community Manager feedback
- Start the design process

Step 3: Advisory council call
- Timing: Once early low-fidelity wireframes or sketches are complete
- Facilitated by the Community Team
- Walk council through: (a) your understanding of the user problem, (b) the proposed solution, (c) designs
- Listen to feedback and answer questions (expect lots of questions)

Step 4: Design iteration
- Iterate on designs based on advisory council feedback

Step 5: Pilot phase
- Introduce feature as a pilot to a small number of individuals
- Pilot testers should cover a broad range of personas/customer profiles (power users AND casual users)
- Include members from your advisory council
- Reach out to testers and ask for feedback throughout
- Consider hooking up a feedback form directly to team's Slack channel for real-time alerts

Step 6: Feature iteration
- Iterate on the feature based on pilot feedback (ongoing throughout pilot)

Step 7: Advisory council call (second)
- Share pilot results
- Share a summary of the feedback received
- Share what you've changed
- Crucial step for showing their importance and priming them to support the launch

Step 8: Beta phase
- Announce feature in a more public setting
- Increase access to it
- Highlight what you learned during pilot phase and what changed
- Encourage advisory council to join public discussion, provide trustworthy context, and advocate on your behalf
- Continue to provide a communication channel for beta users to give feedback

Step 9: General availability
- If the feature is driving value, make it generally available to all users

### Launch Buzz Strategy Selection Checklist (Generating buzz)
A checklist of 6+ distinct launch buzz strategies to evaluate for your product launch, each with real examples to reference

How it works: When planning your launch, evaluate each strategy:

☐ 1. Remarkable Video — Can we create an entertaining, shareable video? (Best for: products with visual appeal or personality-driven brands. See: Dollar Shave Club, Descript)
☐ 2. Remarkable Product Demo — Can we demo the product in a way that creates a 'wow' moment? (Best for: products with novel technology or UX. See: Dropbox, Mailbox, mmhmm)
☐ 3. Remarkable Value Prop — Is our core value prop so differentiated it generates buzz on its own? (Best for: products that are dramatically cheaper, simpler, or more generous than alternatives. See: Robinhood, Gmail, Netflix)
☐ 4. Remarkable Offline Stunt — Can we do something bold in the physical world that generates press? (Best for: products competing against a well-known incumbent. See: Salesforce vs. Siebel, WePay vs. PayPal)
☐ 5. Remarkable Controversy — Can we take a provocative stance or build something deliberately polarizing? (Best for: founders with public profiles or products that challenge norms. See: Hey.com, Yo)

### Scarcity + Influencer Launch Playbook (Generating buzz)
The pattern used by Superhuman, Clubhouse, Hey.com, and others combining invite-only access with influencer seeding to generate massive waitlists

How it works: Two-part pattern for scarcity + influencer buzz:

Part 2 — Visible influencer usage: Get the product into the hands of people with large audiences who will visibly use or mention it. The visibility mechanism matters:

### School/Community Launch Playbook (Lessons on building a viral consumer app: The story of Saturn)

Scaling note: At 18 schools, they consolidated from white-labeled apps into a single app (Saturn) to unlock significantly faster school launch rate while preserving personal touches.

### Canva's Multi-Channel Launch Playbook (How to find and win your first 10 B2B customers)
Canva combined a 2-year wait list, coordinated press embargoes, community building with social media managers, and creative guerrilla tactics

How it works: Timeline and approach:
1. Built a wait list over 2 years (~10,000 people) from conferences, user testing, friends, family, random connections
2. Lined up press for launch day with embargoes — TechCrunch, ZDNet, and other outlets
3. Gave pre-demos to press before launch
4. On launch night, sent email to entire wait list
5. Set up real-time Google Analytics dashboard

Reality check: Launch night was anticlimactic. Users dripped in one at a time. First week: ~2,000 signups. Second week: ~5,000. Slow burn, not overnight success.

Key insight: 'It is really hard to convert press into actual users of your product, often because the articles don't necessarily link to your product.'

Guerrilla tactic: An old Daft Punk visualization on Cameron's personal site got millions of visitors when Daft Punk teased an album at Coachella. They put a banner pointing to Canva — got more visitors than from TechCrunch.

Force multiplier: Early users were social media managers and bloggers — people who naturally talk about tools. They became best advocates through tweets and blog posts, enabling first 6-12 months of growth.

Overall lesson: 'There was never one silver bullet. It's always just bits and pieces and that slowly agglomerates into a full acquisition channel.'
