# Executive Communication - Frameworks, Templates & Checklists

*35 artifacts extracted from Lenny's Podcast and Newsletter*

## Frameworks

### Aristotelian Triptych Presentation Framework (What jury duty taught me about product management)
A three-part structure for persuasive presentations and executive arguments, connected to the Minto pyramid principle

How it works: Structure any persuasive presentation or argument in three parts:
1. Tell them what you'll tell them — Open with your conclusion and key points up front (connects to Minto pyramid: start with the answer)
2. Tell them — Present the evidence, data, and supporting arguments
3. Tell them what you told them — Summarize and reinforce your key points and conclusion

This prevents the audience from jumping to conclusions prematurely and keeps them oriented throughout your argument.

### Bad News Communication Framework (Communicating bad news - Issue 26)
A mental model for how to communicate negative project results to leadership, centered on empathy and putting yourself on the other side of the table

How it works: The core principle: Imagine being on the other side of the table — what would you want to know if YOU were in charge? If you're like most people, you'd want to know: (1) Everything — full transparency about what happened and why, (2) As soon as possible — don't delay sharing bad news, (3) Exactly how to best move forward — come with a clear plan or recommended next steps. Note: The full framework and detailed steps are behind the paywall in the subscriber-only portion of the newsletter.

### CEO Test for Tradeoff Framing (How to communicate tradeoffs so leaders will listen)
A simple mental model for ensuring your tradeoff communication hits the right level — ask yourself what the CEO would want to know.

How it works: Put yourself into the CEO role: If you were responsible for the overall success of the business over the long term, what would you want to know about a prioritization decision? What information or arguments would help you choose? Now jump back into your PM body and figure out how, when, and with what tools you can present that in a clear way to leadership.

### Chapter 1 vs Chapter 6 Communication (Casey Winters)
A mental model for presenting to executives by finding the right level of context to start the story.

How it works: Chapter 1: Company strategy, metrics, assumptions. Chapter 6: The deep weeds. Find the last point in the story that is completely obvious to the audience and start there to avoid confusing them or wasting time on things they already know.

### Communicating Failure to Leadership — 7-Step Framework (This Week #7: Effectively communicating about a failure to execs, managing founder expectations, and hiring a Director of PM)
A structured approach for presenting a failed project or missed goals to executive stakeholders, including mindset preparation and delivery tactics

How it works: MINDSET PREPARATION:
1. Failure is super normal — most technology projects fail because you're doing something never done before.
2. You aren't the project — don't equate project success with personal success.
3. It's not that big of a deal — assuming no one got hurt and you didn't blow massive resources, it will likely be forgotten within the year.

DELIVERY FRAMEWORK:
1. Be up front — Don't hide it. Lean into it: 'This project failed. Let me tell you what happened, what we've learned, and what we're recommending as next steps.' Leaders respect clarity and responsibility.
2. Stay positive — Avoid looking down and depressed. Smile, be friendly, convey optimism. Your energy influences how others feel.
3. Separate the idea from the execution — Was the idea bad, or was it executed badly? Don't assume; take a hard look at whether a different approach might work.
4. Describe the journey — Paint a succinct picture of the sequence of events. Remind people why they were bullish. Include early successes, user quotes, and learnings. Consider using a visual timeline.
5. Share what worked — What did you learn? What theories were disproved? What surprised you? Measure progress by how much you've learned (per Eric Ries / Lean Startup).
6. Have a clear plan for next steps — This is the MOST important part. Turn failure into opportunity. Options: kill the project quickly, pivot to something showing signs of success, or take a new approach to the same problem.
7. Show you care — Make clear you are committed and excited about the work and moving forward.

### Dinosaur Brain Executive Reviews (Ami Vora)
A framework for structuring product reviews based on the limited cognitive load executives can dedicate to any single project.

How it works: Assume executives have a 'dinosaur brain' and can only hold 3 facts at a time. Do not present all your data hoping they make the decision. Instead: 1. Provide the minimal information needed. 2. Make a clean, opinionated recommendation. 3. Rely on the executive to provide pattern matching, industry context, and cross-company alignment.

### Flash Tags (Brian Tolkin, Dharmesh Shah)
A communication system using hashtags to clarify the intent and mandate level of feedback given by a leader to a team.

How it works: 1. #FYI: Found this interesting, no response expected. 2. #suggestion: A thought to consider, no response expected, no obligation to do it. 3. #recommendation: I've researched this and think we should do it; if you choose not to, I expect a response explaining why. 4. #plea: I strongly believe in my soul we must do this; still not a mandate, but a heavy request.

### Flash Tags for CEO Communication (Tom Conrad)
A system for CEOs to label the weight of their input so teams know whether something is an FYI, suggestion, or directive

How it works: Four levels of CEO communication: 1) FYI - just sharing information, 2) Suggestion - one option to consider, 3) Do this - please make this happen, 4) I will die on this hill - non-negotiable directive. Tom notes this is easier said than done—even clearly labeled 'just my opinion' feedback from a CEO causes people to reprioritize their work.

### McKinsey SCR (Situation-Complication-Resolution) Storytelling Framework (How to get into product management)
A storytelling framework for structuring business pitches and presentations, referenced as an excellent tool for PMs

How it works: Three-part structure for telling a business story:
1. Situation — Set the context. What is the current state?
2. Complication — What is the problem or challenge that has arisen?
3. Resolution — What is the proposed solution or path forward?

Lenny calls this 'an excellent tool for laying out your pitch' and links to SpeakingSherpa's detailed guide on it.

### McKinsey Situation-Complication-Resolution (SCR) Framework (This Week #3: Finding product/market fit, interviewing for a Director of PM role, and structuring discussions with senior leaders)
A storytelling framework for structuring business communications, especially presentations to senior leaders. Can be applied to any medium and many contexts.

How it works: Structure: 
- **Situation**: Describe the current state or context (what everyone agrees on)
- **Complication**: Identify what has changed or what the problem/challenge is
- **Resolution**: Present your proposed solution or recommendation

Lenny says this framework 'changed the way I present information ever since.' Link to deeper explanation: https://speakingsherpa.com/how-to-tell-a-business-story-using-the-mckinsey-situation-complication-resolution-scr-framework/

### McKinsey Situation-Complication-Resolution (SCR) Framework for Storytelling (Startup PM vs. big company PM)
A storytelling framework for structuring business pitches and presentations

How it works: Structure your pitch/story in three parts:
1. **Situation**: Set the context — what is the current state? What does the audience already know or agree on?
2. **Complication**: Introduce the problem, tension, or change — why can't we continue as-is? What's the challenge?
3. **Resolution**: Present your solution, recommendation, or path forward — what should we do and why?

This framework works for presentations, docs, emails, and any persuasive communication.

### Memification (Yuhki Yamashata)
The practice of distilling data insights, research findings, or strategic narratives into meme-like, highly memorable nuggets that executives and stakeholders can spontaneously cite in meetings, serving as the primary vehicle for knowledge transfer within a company

How it works: Test: Can a leader cite your insight from memory in the middle of a meeting? If yes, you've successfully memified it. Process: 1) Start with an important insight or data point, 2) Distill it to a single memorable statement, 3) Repeat it in context until it becomes part of organizational vocabulary, 4) Success = when leaders cite it unprompted.

### Minto Pyramid Principle and SCR Framework (The Best of Lenny’s Newsletter—2024 Edition)
Communication frameworks for structuring arguments and presenting information to leadership

How it works: Two complementary communication frameworks: 1) Minto Pyramid Principle - start with the answer/recommendation, then provide supporting arguments, then supporting data. Inverts the typical narrative structure. 2) SCR Framework - Situation (context), Complication (the problem), Resolution (your recommendation). Used for executive communication and presenting tradeoffs.

### SCQA / Pyramid Principle for Executive Communication (Essential reading for product builders—part 2)
A four-part framework for structuring any executive communication: Situation, Complication, Question, Answer. The answer sits at the top of a pyramid with evidence underneath.

How it works: S (Situation): Start with the state of affairs. Fact-based, unambiguous, not controversial. Anyone on any side of the issue should read it and agree it's fair.

C (Complication): A crisp, short statement about what has changed or what's making things harder.

Q (Question): Falls automatically out of S and C. It's almost always 'What should we do?'

A (Answer): Answer first at the top of a pyramid. Pyramid-shape your evidence underneath it. The answer must resolve the complication 100%.

Practice tip: Every conversation in your life is an opportunity to SCQA—friends, loved ones, conversations at the deli counter, everything.

### Slide Annotation Status System (Nancy Duarte)
A visual process for tracking the completion status of individual slides in a massive enterprise deck.

How it works: Uses a visual annotation in thumbnail mode to show exactly what percentage complete a slide is and what specific elements need focus, avoiding version control nightmares when multiple people are editing.

### User-Centric Reframing Approach (A PM’s guide to influence)
A technique for reframing business-focused proposals as user-centric initiatives to win over user-driven executives

How it works: Principle: 'Negotiation is the art of letting the other side have your way.' — Chris Voss

Steps:
1. Identify the stakeholder's primary lens/value (e.g. user experience, revenue, engineering quality)
2. Reframe your proposal's benefits through that lens
3. Lead with anchor data points that speak to their values
4. Let the proposal naturally follow from those anchors
5. Set guardrails that align with their concerns

Example structure for user-driven executives:
- Lead with user insight data (not revenue projections)
- Frame the problem as 'How do we help users [get more value]?' instead of 'How do we increase [business metric]?'
- Show that the business outcome is a natural byproduct of solving the user problem

### Zuckerberg's Exec Review Conviction Spectrum (Sriram and Aarthi)
A framework for how leaders should communicate their level of conviction on decisions during reviews, ranging from 'I don't care' to 'just do this'

How it works: Spectrum of conviction: 1) I don't care, I don't know why you're talking to me. 2) I kind of care, glad you're updating me. 3) I really want you to do this, but if you overrule me, that's fine. 4) I'm the founder/CEO, just do this. Additionally: articulate WHY you believe what you believe by sharing your logic tree, so teams know exactly which assumptions to challenge to change your mind.

## Templates

### Bad News Communication Deck/Doc Structure (Communicating bad news - Issue 26)
A recommended structure for the deck or document used to present negative project results to leadership for the first time

How it works: Recommended structure for communicating failed project results:

1. BACKGROUND: Why this project was initiated — remind leaders why they decided to give this a shot in the first place
2. INITIAL HYPOTHESIS: What you were trying to prove or improve
3. HOW IT CAME TOGETHER: The approach taken, timeline, key decisions
4. POSITIVE QUALITATIVE FEEDBACK: User feedback, quotes, or other positive signals received
5. RESULTS: The actual metrics and data showing negative performance
6. LEARNINGS: What good came out of the experience, what the team learned, what to avoid in the future
7. RECOMMENDATION: Your crystal clear POV on what to do next (iterate, kill, or ship and claw back)

### Buy-in Meeting Narrative Structure (Megan Cook)
A framework for opening a pitch or proposal meeting with executives.

How it works: 1. State the 'ask' clearly (e.g., need a decision, need feedback on a specific angle). 2. Explain the current situation. 3. Explain what has changed. 4. State the implications (the problem to solve or opportunity to go after). 5. Provide supporting data.

### Executive Onboarding 'Hot Takes' Document (Ami Vora)
A document created during the first 90 days to provoke strategic discussion and build alignment with the CEO.

How it works: Alongside a standard 'Observations' document (what's going well/poorly based on 1:1s), write a 'Hot Takes' document. Include provocative ideas for fundamental changes to the business or product. Review this with the CEO/Exec team to test alignment, uncover hidden context, and build a trusting, mind-meld relationship.

### Executive Patterns Notes Doc (A PM’s guide to influence)
A running document to track executive behavior patterns—questions they ask, feedback they give—to anticipate reactions in future meetings

How it works: Structure: A notes document with entries organized by executive/stakeholder name. For each person, track:
- Questions they frequently ask in meetings
- Feedback patterns they exhibit
- Topics they care most about
- Their decision-making style and preferences

Purpose: Identify patterns to anticipate questions and reactions for future discussions when you need their support. Updated continuously by observing what stakeholders say in other meetings and forums.

### PREP (Matt Abrahams)
A structure for making a clear, persuasive point on the spot.

How it works: 1. Point: State your main idea. 2. Reason: Explain why you believe this. 3. Example: Provide a concrete instance or data point. 4. Point: Restate your main idea to conclude.

### Weekly Leadership Update (How to make an impact in your first 90 days)
A regular async written update format for new leaders to build trust and visibility

How it works: Format: Weekly written update containing:
1. Observations (what you're noticing)
2. Personal areas of focus (what you're working on)
3. Kudos (recognizing others' work)

Purpose: Scale leadership visibility in an async way, especially valuable in remote environments.

Companion artifact: Build a 'personal' data dashboard of metrics that matter to your success. Benefits: helps you learn the data tools, think about your goals, and get in the habit of looking at performance regularly.

## Checklists

### 5 Steps to Align Product Ideas with Executives (This Week #4: Motivating engineers to hit deadlines, PM career ladders, and aligning with execs)
A checklist for navigating executive pushback when your product proposals are rejected as not 'actionable' enough

How it works: 1. Get data — What evidence backs up your ideas? Compelling user research quotes? Metrics that point to your ideas being right? A hunch you want to test? Build your case logically on data or experience. Be prepared to be proven wrong.
2. Assume good intentions and listen — Dig further when execs say ideas aren't actionable. Ask: Why not? What would make them more actionable? When they share blue-sky ideas, find ways to make them more concrete. Feedback is a gift.
3. Figure out the ideal product and work backward — Remove all constraints and envision the ideal user experience. Then put constraints back on and figure out what it would actually take. How much work would it really take? Is it that crazy? Pitch it. Reference: Airbnb's approach from Lenny's Medium article.
4. Get out of the building — Stop debating opinions internally. Show concepts to at least 5 real users. Use their feedback to guide thinking.
5. Work backward from your goal — Identify the KPI you're trying to drive (specific action, purchases, invites). Align on this KPI with execs first. Sort ideas by which will make the biggest dent in the metric. If there's disagreement, find the quickest test to validate the assumption.

### Choosing the Right Medium for Executive Check-ins (This Week #3: Finding product/market fit, interviewing for a Director of PM role, and structuring discussions with senior leaders)
Decision criteria for selecting how to present information to senior leaders

How it works: Consider these factors when choosing your presentation medium:
1. What is your audience expecting? (Are decks encouraged? Are they banned?)
2. What sort of information are you communicating? (Designs? Strategy? A bunch of data?)
3. What is your personal style of communication? (Do you like to point to things on a screen? Do you prefer attention on you?)

Process:
- Pick whatever you're most comfortable with
- Tell the story you want to tell
- See how it goes and iterate
- Watch to see if leaders stay engaged and understand what you want them to understand
- If not, ask for feedback from those leaders or your manager
- Try something different next time

### Crisis Communication Hourly Update (Ethan Evans 2.0)
A step-by-step communication cadence to manage executive anxiety during a severe outage or failure.

How it works: 1. Own the failure immediately. 2. State exactly where you are. 3. State exactly what you will do in the next hour. 4. Commit to the exact time of the next update. 5. Deliver the next update on time and repeat.

### Exec Review Meeting Hygiene (Sriram and Aarthi)
A set of practices for running effective executive reviews, drawn from Facebook and other experiences

How it works: 1) Clarify meeting type upfront: update (listen and acknowledge) vs. decision (pros/cons/trade-offs). 2) Watch for meeting type drift (update sliding into decision). 3) Watch for teams pushing accountability to you ('We can't decide, you decide'). 4) Send pre-reads before the meeting. 5) Ensure the right people are in the room (not everyone, but no key people missing). 6) Pay complete attention. 7) Make sure everyone gets a chance to talk. 8) Have regular rhythms (weekly/monthly) to avoid 'hero meetings' where everything rides on one high-stakes review. Concept of 'hero meetings' credited to Gokul Rajaram.

### Executive Meeting De-risking Process (Casey Winters)
Steps to prepare for a high-stakes executive review.

How it works: 1. Roleplay the specific executives attending. 2. Anticipate their unique questions (e.g., CFO asks X, CEO asks Y). 3. Answer questions in the early story before they are asked. 4. Hold pre-meetings with key individuals to surface concerns early.

### Failure Communication Presentation Checklist (This Week #7: Effectively communicating about a failure to execs, managing founder expectations, and hiring a Director of PM)
Checklist of elements to include when preparing a presentation or update about a failed project to leadership

How it works: Before the meeting:
☐ Separate in your mind: is this an idea failure or an execution failure?
☐ Compile early successes and user quotes from the project journey
☐ Create a visual timeline of the project's sequence of events
☐ Document key learnings — what theories were disproved, what surprised the team
☐ Prepare a clear recommendation for next steps (kill, pivot, or retry with new approach)

During the meeting:
☐ Open with a clear, direct statement that the project didn't meet goals
☐ Describe the journey and remind stakeholders why they were initially bullish
☐ Share what was learned and what worked
☐ Present your next-steps recommendation
☐ Convey optimism through body language and energy
☐ Demonstrate commitment and excitement for the path forward

### Operator's Manual for Communication (Book of Kim) (Ian McAllister)
A set of tactical rules for clear, executive-level business communication.

How it works: Key rules: Avoid weasel words, answer first and then explain, own your problems, and always answer a 'when' question with a date.

### Tradeoff Decision Meeting Facilitation Guide (How to communicate tradeoffs so leaders will listen)
Step-by-step guide for running a meeting where you present a tradeoff decision document to executives.

How it works: Steps: 1. Quick orientation — Why we're here. 2. Silent reading period — ~8 minutes depending on document length (busy execs don't do pre-reads). 3. Restate recommendation — Clearly state your proposed decision. 4. Set decision timeline — 'I'd like to gavel on this decision at the end of the meeting — the default is that we'll move forward with this approach.' 5. Open floor for new information — 'Anything we didn't account for in this document?' 6. Discuss new data if any (ideally steelmanning already covered it). 7. At 75% of the meeting, assess convergence vs. divergence. 8a. If converging — Conclude with the decision and pre-outlined next steps. 8b. If diverging — Flag it as soon as possible. End with specific actions: follow up 1:1 with decision maker, spend more time with the customer to clarify, come back with an update. Key principle: The only 'bad' meeting is one that ends without clear next steps and a timeline to reach a decision.

## Examples

### Amazon Appstore 'Test Drive' Launch Failure (Ethan Evans 2.0)
A real-world case study of handling a critical launch failure in front of the CEO.

How it works: The feature failed at 6:00 AM on launch day. Ethan owned the mistake immediately, provided hourly status updates to Jeff Bezos to prevent micromanagement, accepted help from AWS principal engineers (who provided 500 servers to brute-force the bad design), and later met Bezos in person to humanize the interaction and rebuild trust.

### Executive Connection Rituals (Elizabeth Stone)
Scheduled recurring events to maintain connection between executives and individual contributors.

How it works: 1. Biweekly Office Hours: 20-minute 'speed dating' slots that anyone in the company can book months in advance to discuss work or top-of-mind issues. 2. Ask Me Anything (AMA) sessions: Scaled to different team sizes where any topic is fair game to build approachability.

### Watershed 'Transform Measurement' — Long-Term Vision Example (How to communicate tradeoffs so leaders will listen)
PM Steve at Watershed proposed automating carbon measurement instead of scaling with manual analysts, using long-term projections to overcome leadership's fear of an upcoming demand surge.

How it works: Context: Watershed measured and reported carbon emissions. A demand surge was approaching. Default plan: hire many analysts for manual process + build features to increase analyst efficacy. Steve's alternative: Automate measurement using AI. Short-term risk: potentially lower quality bar and impact analyst throughput. Long-term benefit: dramatically lower marginal operating cost, become truly software-driven vs. hybrid software/services. How Steve persuaded leadership: (1) Deeply detailed picture of 3-month and 15-month outlook under his automated-future vision. (2) Roadmap of specific features showing the long-term path required a more minimal short-term tradeoff than assumed. Key insight: He addressed the core fear (demand surge going badly) by showing enough overlap between the automation path and the short-term need. Result: Company leadership made the automation path the top companywide priority for the year. Steve gave it a catchy name ('Transform Measurement!') that spread virally.
