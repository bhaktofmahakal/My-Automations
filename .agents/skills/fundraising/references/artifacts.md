## Frameworks

### 4-Step Fundraising Process Framework (A playbook for fundraising)
Marc McCabe's end-to-end framework for managing a Series A fundraise, breaking it into four distinct phases with specific activities and timelines for each

How it works: Step 1: Preparation (4+ weeks) - Determine why and when to raise, pull together key metrics and wins, prepare materials, harden the pitch with 4-6 test audiences. Step 2: Outreach - Build list of 50-60 funds, identify target partners, secure warm introductions, cultivate pre-existing relationships. Step 3: Navigating the Process - Coordinate first meetings within 2-3 weeks, max 4-5 pitches per day, track all funds' progress, keep funds on similar timelines, manage follow-ups. Step 4: Partner Meetings + Closing - Work with champion partner as co-conspirator, present at partner meetings, handle term sheets, negotiate terms, sign SPA, close round. Total timeline: 8-16 weeks from start to term sheet, plus 3-6 weeks from term sheet to money in bank.

### Abundance Mindset for Investor Asks (How to activate your investor network)
Mental model shift: investor requests are not scarce chips to cash in — they are renewable if you make good asks

How it works: Common founder mistake: Thinking you only get a certain number of 'chips' to cash in with investors. This scarcity mindset develops after unresponded-to emails, but the real problem is that the asks are bad, not that you've used up goodwill. Reality: People like being helpful, especially investors — it's a big part of their job. By not asking, you're denying them the joy of helping. The fix is not to ask less but to ask better: make asks targeted, time-bounded, and specific. Set investors up for success.

### Acquisition Funnel Qualification Criteria (Startup to exit: Lessons from a first-time founder)
Criteria for evaluating and prioritizing potential acquirers for a startup

How it works: When building a list of ~50 potential acquirers, evaluate on three dimensions:
1. **Size and scale**: Bias toward well-capitalized, mature companies (they have budget and infrastructure to close acquisitions)
2. **Ability to move quickly**: Bias toward venture or PE-backed companies (they have board pressure to grow and experience with M&A processes)
3. **Likelihood of penetrating senior leadership**: Use LinkedIn Sales Navigator to map org charts and find paths to C-suite decision makers

Categories to include: Companies across your industry and adjacent categories (for Savvy: health-care payments, insurance brokerage, insurance companies, HR software companies)

### Ann Miura-Ko's Deep Due Diligence Process (Lessons from 140+ angel investments)
A professional VC's approach to evaluating startups over 2-3 weeks, useful as a benchmark for what thorough diligence looks like

How it works: Timeline: 2-3 weeks getting to know the founder and business
Strategy: Invest in only 2-5 companies per year, spend a lot of time with each

Diligence areas:
1. Review investor updates — to assess how founder's thinking evolves over time
2. Review feature updates — to assess speed of building
3. Deep dive on core beliefs — what they believe that they are not willing to let go
4. Talk to customers (if they have them)
5. Reference calls for the founders

### Ann Miura-Ko's VC Justification for 'Why Now' (Why now?)
Explains the structural reason why VCs care about 'why now' — it justifies the venture capital model itself

How it works: The importance of 'why now' connects to why venture capital exists as an asset class. VC works because there is justification for being an unprofitable company at the outset because the opportunity later on is so huge, compounded by the fact that getting in early enables the startup to gain advantage. A 'why now' increases the probability that the future opportunity will indeed be huge, making the early unprofitable period a worthwhile bet.

### Capital Raise Decision Framework for Profitable Companies (This Week #15: When should a SaaS startup raise money 💰)
A framework from Tomasz Tunguz for deciding when a profitable company should raise capital.

How it works: When should a profitable company raise capital, if ever? The answer is the same for all businesses regardless of financing history: Raise capital when you are confident that you can use it to attain a higher growth rate AND the higher growth rate is worth a trade-off in dilution. Higher growth rates increase the value of a business. Higher growth rates fetch greater multiples.

Key evaluation: Is the growth rate increase achievable with the capital? Is that growth rate increase worth the dilution you'll take on?

### Eigenvector Centrality Network Strategy (How to activate your investor network)
A framework for building a diverse investor network by optimizing for eigenvector centrality (how many people all the people you know, know) rather than degree centrality (how many people you know directly). The goal is to know the person with the highest degree centrality in each separate dense network.

How it works: Degree centrality = knowing many people in one dense network (e.g., all financiers). This is limiting. Eigenvector centrality = knowing the most connected person in many different networks. Build your cap table with maximum diversity of backgrounds. Example first 15 investors at Levels: insurance founder, health tech founder, health/wellness writer, YouTube creator, professional basketball player, product leader at major tech company, partnerships leader at organic foods company, growth leader at DTC software company, talent network founder, partner at major consulting firm, international expansion leader at major tech company, podcast host, sales leader at Fortune 500, edtech founder, wellness-focused VC. Key principle: Having a single entry point into a network is infinitely more valuable than having zero. Reference: Mark Granovetter's 1973 paper 'The Strength of Weak Ties'.

### Four Paths to Investor Access (Lessons from 140+ angel investments)
Four ways to become someone founders want on their cap table, which is the key to getting access to the best deals

How it works: 1. Useful Knowledge: Become very smart about something founders need help with — hiring, fundraising, growth strategy, product, marketing, scaling internationally, etc. Example: Jamie Quint's growth experience at Twitch, Reddit, Mercury, and Notion.
2. Audience: Build an audience founders can rely on to amplify their startup's story. Examples: Packy McCormick, Harry Stebbings, Erik Torenberg, Sahil Bloom.
3. Signal: Build status as an investor such that your being on the cap table becomes a strong signal to other investors. Examples: Elad Gil, Cyan Banister, Naval Ravikant.
4. Reputation: Build an amazing reputation with founders such that they tell all their founder friends about you. Focus on 'Founder NPS' — every interaction is memorable for the founder even if it's routine for you.

### Fundraising Success Metrics Benchmark (A playbook for fundraising)
Expected conversion rates and success metrics for a Series A fundraising process

How it works: Key benchmarks: Target 50-60 funds for Series A outreach. Having 5 VC partners still interested after diligence phase = success. Success means failing with 90%+ of funds on your list. Y Combinator estimates you need to speak to 30 funds to get one term sheet. Each partner at a fund generally only makes 1-3 early-stage investments per year. Max 4-5 pitches per day during outreach phase. Leave suitable buffer on each side of pitches for energy restoration, travel, and meetings that run long.

### Fundraising Timeline Calendar (A playbook for fundraising)
A visual timeline showing the overall timing of different elements of a fundraising round, from 12 months before to closing

How it works: Key milestones and timing: 12 months before: Start thinking about fundraising, begin cultivating relationships with top-tier VCs. 8 months runway minimum when going out to raise. If only 4-6 months runway, consider raising a bridge round first. 8-16 weeks: Total time from start to term sheet. 4+ weeks: Building deck and supporting materials. Weeks 3-4: Hardening the pitch with 4-6 test audiences. 2-3 weeks: All first meetings with 50-60 funds (max 4-5 pitches per day). Following weeks: Second pitches, customer references, data diligence. After term sheet: 3-6 weeks from term sheet to money in bank (legal diligence, stock purchase agreement). Seasonal note: If in July and contemplating raising, wait for late August or early September to begin outreach. Starting early in the year makes sense.

### Fundraising as Trust-Building Exercise (A playbook for fundraising)
Core mental model that frames the entire fundraising process as building trust over time through staged information sharing and relationship development

How it works: Core principle: 'Fundraising is an exercise in building trust.' Key applications: 1. Information staging: Show positive elements to get investors interested but leave good content in reserve — don't share everything in the teaser deck. 2. Relationship building: Trust is built over many interactions over a long period of time, like any relationship. 3. Covid adaptation: With fewer in-person meetings, find more digital approaches to building relationships (Loom videos, content appendices, customer testimonials). 4. Pre-fundraise cultivation: Start coffee meetings with top-tier investors 6-12 months before raising. 5. Process coordination: Create a market of multiple buyers rather than one, to build competitive pressure.

### Hardening the Pitch Process (A playbook for fundraising)
A structured approach to testing and refining fundraising materials before formally beginning the pitch process with target investors

How it works: Process: 1. Prepare materials to approximately 70% completeness - coherent story with great supporting data (not necessarily heavily designed). 2. Identify 4-6 test audiences from three categories: (a) Founders who have fundraised at Series A for their own startup, ideally in the same or similar space, (b) Friendly investors who know the space, perhaps from your seed investors, (c) Growth stage investors. 3. Practice your pitch with each of them. Benefits: Builds confidence and reps on the pitch, provides helpful feedback on materials and thesis, can generate warm introductions to relevant funds. Timeline: Should happen during weeks 3-4 of preparation phase, before the 2-3 week formal outreach begins.

### Investment Confidence Tracking System (Lessons from 140+ angel investments)
Simple system for recording and reviewing investment confidence to learn from your own data over time

How it works: At time of each investment, record your confidence level as one of three ratings:
- OK: Good enough bet to invest but low conviction
- Good: Solid conviction
- Great: Very high conviction

Also track:
- Deal source: Friends, other investors (angels/solo capitalists), founders directly, colleagues
- Signal level: Hot deal (top-tier VC leading) vs. low-signal
- Check size

Periodically review your ratings against actual outcomes to calibrate your picking ability and understand where your best deals come from.

### Series A Readiness Assessment (Credible Value Hypothesis) (A playbook for fundraising)
Framework for evaluating whether a startup is ready to raise a Series A round based on the concept of proving a credible value hypothesis

How it works: Series A = 'The Product-Market Fit Round'. Evaluation factors: 1. Market you're attacking - size and opportunity. 2. Features you've built to exploit the opportunity. 3. How well your product is finding its fit in the market. 4. Growth rate relative to peers (can make up for lower ARR if business seems defensible and sustainable). 5. Revenue metrics (e.g., $1M ARR common benchmark for SaaS, but not sufficient alone and doesn't always apply for consumer businesses). 6. Evidence you've convinced a slice of your market that your product is valuable enough to pay for (or if free, use very regularly). 7. Growing user base. Key distinction: Seed rounds explore an idea and find some fit; Series A pours fuel on the fire. Recommendation: Speak with seed investors early about what milestones would support a new round.

### Seven Surprises of Angel Investing (Lessons from 140+ angel investments)
Lenny's seven biggest data-backed surprises from 140+ angel investments, serving as strategic principles for angel investors

How it works: 1. You're usually wrong about which investments will do best: Only 1/3 of best investments were rated 'Great' confidence at time of investing. Invest even without full conviction if there's a path to 100x.
2. Most deal flow comes from other investors: Not from founders, friends, or colleagues. Build relationships with active angels and solo capitalists.
3. Great deals are currency among investors: Share deals to build reciprocal relationships. Find 3-5 angel investor friends.
4. Angel investing is more about access than picking: Three parts — capital, access, picking. Access is most important. Over 2/3 of biggest winners were 'hot' deals.
5. Become someone founders want on their cap table: Four paths — useful knowledge, audience, signal, reputation.
6. Follow high-signal leads (but not only): 80% of investments with top-tier investors are on track to succeed. Allocate ~70% high-signal, ~30% low-signal. Caveat: prolific investors like a16z/Tiger are less signal than less frequent investors like Sequoia/Benchmark.
7. Power laws are real: 70% of paper gains from 1 company, 80% from 4 companies. Need ~30+ investments for a shot at one rocket ship.

### Sip Seed Round (Dan Shipper)
A fundraising structure designed to preserve optionality and reduce burn-rate pressure.

How it works: Investors commit a set amount (e.g., $2M) on a SAFE at a set cap, but founders only pull down the capital as needed. It can include a modified SAFE that allows conversion to equity in 3 years even without a priced round.

### Startup Hero's Journey (Mike Maples Jr)
A storytelling framework for pitching and marketing, adapted from Joseph Campbell's Hero's Journey.

How it works: The customer/investor is the Hero (Luke Skywalker). The founder is the Mentor (Obi-Wan). The product/insight is the Magic/Tool (Lightsaber). The narrative must constantly toggle between 'the world that is' (status quo) and 'the world that could be' (the future).

### Story Framing Test (Uri Levine)
A quick diagnostic for whether your startup story is problem-focused or solution-focused based on how you open your pitch.

How it works: Three ways to start your story: 1) 'Our company/system/AI is...' = Solution-focused (worst). 2) 'The problem we are solving is...' = Problem-focused (better). 3) 'The value we create for you is...' = User-focused (best). Test: look at your pitch deck, emails to investors, and website — which framing do you use?

### The 'Mission Impossible' Pitch Structure (Merci Grace)
A storytelling framework for startup pitches and presentations.

How it works: Start the pitch in the middle of the action (the climax or the unique founder insight) to immediately grab attention, rather than starting with standard, boring market size slides. Backfill the context later once you have their attention.

### Three Principles of a Good Investor Ask (How to activate your investor network)
A framework for crafting investor requests that maximize response and conversion rates

How it works: Every investor ask should be: 1. TARGETED — Only send to people who are relevant to the specific request. Skim your investor list and identify who has the right background/connections. 2. TIME-BOUNDED — Executing the request should ideally take less than a minute for the investor. For bigger asks (e.g., reviewing interview recordings), be mindful of who you send to and acknowledge the time commitment. 3. SPECIFIC — Be specific in your request and limit scope. Example: 'Who are the two best engineers you've ever worked with?' is better than 'Do you know any good engineers?' Specifying a number reduces cognitive load and increases response rate. Reference: chunking bias.

### Three Stages of a Startup (Search → Growth → Maturity) (This Week #15: When should a SaaS startup raise money 💰)
A simple three-stage model for understanding when a startup should raise money and scale. Fundraising is best done in stage two (Growth), after product/market fit has been found.

How it works: Every successful business goes through roughly three stages:

Stage 1 — Search for Product/Market Fit: You are trying to figure out if anyone even wants what you're building. High-risk to raise here because you'll spend it looking for PMF (building, iterating, learning, repeating). Avoid raising money here if possible to minimize dilution and time pressure. Premature scaling (spending significant money on growth before PMF) is one of the most common ways startups die.

Stage 2 — Growth (Best to raise money here): You're fairly confident you've got PMF. Focus shifts to getting your product into people's hands. Funding is most valuable here — accelerating hiring, investing in paid growth channels and/or a sales team, and practical needs like larger office space. Be very thoughtful about how much you raise, who you raise from, and what it means for your business.

Stage 3 — Maturity: Business is established and scaling.

Key decision question: What do your new hires (and spending) need to achieve in order for the raise to become a net win for your business? And how confident are you that this will happen?

### Three lenses for evaluating angel investors (Top angel investors in the U.S.)
A framework for analyzing angel investors across three dimensions: activity volume (past 5 years), current momentum (most recent year), and success rate (exits). Each lens surfaces different investors and different insights.

How it works: Three questions to ask when building your angel investor target list:
1. Who have been the most active angel investors over the past five years? (Shows consistency and volume)
2. Who are the most active angel investors currently? (Shows who is deploying capital right now and is most likely to invest)
3. Who have been the most successful angel investors historically? (Shows quality of judgment via exit count)

Key insight: Investors who appear on the exits list but NOT on the activity lists may have a higher hit rate — they invest less frequently but pick better. Lenny specifically calls this out as the most interesting signal.

### VC Capital Infusion Value Framework (Your startup idea probably isn’t venture-scale)
Alex Taussig's framework for understanding when venture capital is the right fit — discrete capital infusions that unlock discontinuous value at key de-risking steps

How it works: Venture capital is best suited to companies that:
- Require **discrete capital infusions** to unlock **orders of magnitude more enterprise value**
- Are at **key de-risking steps** along the journey
- Have **discontinuous value unlocks** because the investment thesis is out of consensus in early rounds

Specific risk factors VCs are willing to underwrite:
- Unproven technology
- Unproven team
- Unproven market
- Unproven business model

VCs take a 'flyer' on these risks when they have a strong POV that one of these factors is **misunderstood** and the company can tap into **unbounded upside** if the POV turns out to be right.

### VC Funding as a Drug - Risk Assessment (Startup to exit: Lessons from a first-time founder)
A framework for evaluating whether you should raise VC funding and understanding the psychological/operational costs

How it works: Warning signs that you're raising for the wrong reasons:
- You're raising because it's 'what startups in your cohort do' (Demo Day FOMO)
- You don't have conviction in the business yourself
- Your metrics aren't compelling (only a handful of beta customers)
- You're raising on vision/story rather than traction
- Fear of a market crash is driving your timing

Consequences of premature raising:
- Takes longer to convince investors, distracting from building
- Commits you to a set path (investors backed this vision, so it must be right)
- Creates external pressure to spend more and hire faster
- Investors will push for Series A timing and faster headcount growth
- Delays honest assessment of product-market fit

Alternative approach (Retool model):
- Keep team to co-founders only for a full year after seed
- Stay nimble to pivot quickly until achieving PMF
- Spend slowly until you find traction
- Only increase burn when you have reason (PMF signals) to pour gasoline on growth

Surgeon General warning: 'Beware. VC funding may lead to dependence and complication. Some users have reported extreme anxiety, loss of control, and nightmares of being on a never-ending treadmill.'

### Investor Expectation-Setting Email (How to activate your investor network)
Email snippet sent to potential investors before accepting their money to set expectations about active involvement

How it works: Purpose: Set expectations that you'll be reaching out for help regularly. Key elements: 1. State that you expect investors to be actively involved. 2. Give them permission to opt out if they don't have bandwidth. 3. Frame it as a matching problem (finding the right fit), not a sales problem. This gives you permission to hold them accountable later. Example response from investor who opted out: graceful decline citing bandwidth — which is totally fine. The key is preventing situations where expectations aren't set early and one side feels the other is unreasonable.

### Investor Follow-Up / Thank You Note (How to activate your investor network)
A simple follow-up email to close the loop after an investor delivers on a request

How it works: After an investor delivers on a request (e.g., makes an introduction, provides a candidate referral), send a thank-you note at the conclusion of whatever they delivered. Key elements: 1. Thank them for the specific help. 2. Share the outcome (e.g., 'We spoke with [person], and it was very helpful because...'). 3. Close the loop so they know their effort wasn't wasted. Even a simple note goes a long way and significantly increases the likelihood they'll help again. Sam notes that 'almost nobody puts in the work to do them' despite how little time they take.

### Investor Tracking Google Sheet (How to activate your investor network)
A simple Google Sheet for tracking all investors, their backgrounds, and request history

How it works: Structure: A Google Sheet listing all investors with at minimum: Name, Background/expertise, Contact info, what kinds of things they typically help with, request history. Usage: When you have an ask, spend 5-10 minutes skimming the list and personally send an email to each person who might be willing and able to help. The EA team updates it monthly. Also track: total requests sent, responses received, conversions (requests that resulted in meaningful value). Sam tracked 2,626 requests over 3.3 years. Key insight: 'Computers are good at remembering things (like maintaining a list), and humans are good at connecting the dots.'

### Monthly Team Needs Collection Process (How to activate your investor network)
A monthly process where an EA collects needs from the team to fuel investor asks

How it works: Process: 1. Every month, the founder's EA sends a message to the team. 2. Ask each team member for TWO things they could use help with. 3. Consolidate all responses. 4. Add them to the monthly investor update. 5. Add them to the founder's running 'asks' list. 6. Use these as the basis for targeted investor outreach. Additionally, the founder should maintain a TextExpander snippet called 'asks' that can be pasted anytime someone says 'How can I be helpful?' — then pare it down to requests relevant to that specific person.

### Angel Investing Access Self-Assessment (Lessons from 140+ angel investments)
A simple way to track whether you have sufficient deal access

How it works: Whenever you see a big fundraising round or great exit, ask yourself: 'Did I have a chance to invest in that company?'

If the answer is consistently no, focus on building access through:
1. Building useful skills for founders
2. Building an audience
3. Building signal as an investor
4. Building reputation with founders

### Consequences of Taking VC Money for Non-Venture-Scale Ideas (Your startup idea probably isn’t venture-scale)
Five specific downsides that occur when a non-venture-scale business gets on the VC treadmill

How it works: Once you take VC money, here's what changes:

1. **High growth expectations**: VCs need companies that grow big enough fast enough. If your business isn't suited for rapid growth, it leads to undue pressure and unrealistic expectations.
2. **Loss of control**: VCs buy 10-20% of your company and often get a board seat. This leads to loss of control over direction and decision-making, especially if you aren't growing fast enough.
3. **Misalignment of goals**: You may prioritize product quality, profitability, sustainability, or values; VCs prioritize rapid expansion and ROI.
4. **Ongoing dilution**: If you raise additional funding while underperforming, you raise at lower valuations, diluting yourself, employees, and early investors.
5. **Exit pressure**: Investors expect an exit (acquisition or IPO) within 5-10 years, leading to decisions that prioritize short-term gains over long-term health.

### Deal Sharing Best Practices for Angels (Lessons from 140+ angel investments)
Tactical tips for sharing deals with other angel investors to build reciprocal deal flow

How it works: 1. Find 3-5 awesome angel-investor friends to form a sharing group
2. Once you decide to invest in a startup, ask the founder if they're looking for more great angels. If yes, suggest your co-investors.
3. Send a weekly email to your angel friends sharing deals you're looking at. Share just URLs and a short blurb, unless you have permission to share the deck.
4. Once you decide to invest, tell your angel friends ASAP in case they want to try to join the round.
5. If a founder asks you to keep their round confidential, respect that. But in most cases, if you ask, founders are happy to meet more awesome angels.
6. Only send deals when you're investing (or very close to it) so your 'signal' stays strong — you want other investors to be excited when your deal is in their inbox.
7. Don't just share deals you're asked to share or deals that need help being filled (adverse selection). Proactively share your best finds.

### Fundraising Presentation Optimization (Uri Levine)
Tactical advice for structuring investor presentations to maximize impact given how quickly investors form opinions.

How it works: 1) First slide (title slide) is the most important — displayed longest. Put your strongest point here (market size, traction, team, problem statement). ### Startup Acquisition Playbook (Startup to exit: Lessons from a first-time founder)
Step-by-step guide for navigating a startup acquisition process, from building a pipeline to closing the deal

How it works: **Engage Early Suitors:**
- If acquirers come knocking, always take the meeting ('serve them tea')
- Understand their interest and schedule infrequent touch points as you build
- Don't naively decline without entertaining offers — they may not come back later

**Approach Strategy — Startups Are Bought, Not Sold:**
- Don't signal that you're actively looking for a buyer (risks perception as failing)
- Use product partnership interest as your Trojan horse
- Once you feel strong pull, raise the idea of doing something 'more strategic'

**Build an Acquisition Funnel (treat like a sales pipeline):**
1. Identify ~50 companies in your industry most interested in your product
2. Include companies across adjacent categories (for Savvy: payments, brokerage, insurance, HR software)
3. Qualify based on: size/scale (bias toward well-capitalized, mature companies), ability to move quickly (bias toward venture or PE-backed), likelihood of penetrating senior leadership
4. Use LinkedIn Sales Navigator to map decision makers
5. Expect the list to narrow to a handful after two months of conversations

**Target Decision Makers:**
- Get in front of C-suite directly — don't waste time with subordinates
- A subordinate introducing a deal is less compelling than the CEO bringing you in
- Pursue board-level intros through your investors (highest-impact path in)
- Make it easy for investors: do the research, identify the target person, write the intro email text

**Know Your Acquisition Type:**
- IP/technology acquisition: most accretive, least likely
- Acqui-hire: vehicle for another company to interview/hire your team; not a cakewalk
- Company acquisition (IP + team + customers): ideal but rare
- Be honest about what's realistic and let this inform your target list
- Consider running parallel processes for different types

**Close Aggressively — Time Is Not Your Friend:**
- Momentum is fragile; drawn-out processes increase likelihood of stalling
- Reply to acquirer emails within the hour
- If no response, send text messages referencing emails
- Implement twice-daily standup calls with lawyers to increase velocity
- Don't let signing slip from today to tomorrow
- Push through late-night obstacles (locked accounts, missing signatures)
- Be willing to have uncomfortable conversations to get the deal across the line
- In a bear market, urgency is even more critical — big M&A deals die in downturns

### Term Sheet Handling Checklist (A playbook for fundraising)
Steps and considerations for handling term sheet offers during a Series A fundraise

How it works: When you receive a term sheet: 1. Establish clearly how much time you will need to decide (typical range: 24-48 hours aggressive, up to 1-2 weeks max). 2. Have your legal team review the term sheet and walk you through the terms. 3. Ask legal clarifying questions like 'when will this actually impact me?' to distinguish important vs. theoretical concerns. 4. If other funds still interested, let them know you have a term sheet and they need to decide soon. 5. Generally do NOT share which fund offered the term sheet or the valuation — but transparency can signal confidence. 6. If term sheet is from a top-tier fund, sharing this can positively impact remaining VCs. 7. If not your first choice fund, asking to speak to references can buy time and provide insight. 8. Be cautious about using a term sheet to force another fund's hand if they haven't completed diligence — it can force a pass. Key terms to negotiate: board seats (one vs. two), option pools, founder stock revesting in later rounds, valuation (focus comps around top end of range). After signing term sheet: Do NOT publicly celebrate or tell company outside founders. 3-6 weeks to money in bank. Identify which existing investors will take pro-rata. Find value-add angels and small investors for the round. Reference: YC's standard Series A term sheet guide at https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4P-a-standard-and-clean-series-a-term-sheet
