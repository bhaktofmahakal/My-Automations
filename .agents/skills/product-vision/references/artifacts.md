# Product Vision Creation - Frameworks, Templates & Checklists

*41 artifacts extracted from Lenny's Podcast and Newsletter*

## Frameworks

### 15 out of 10 Product Design (Unconstrained Thinking) (Sanchan Saxena)
Brian Chesky's approach of designing the ideal unconstrained experience first, building it perfectly in one location, then scaling what works.

How it works: Steps: 1) Think unconstrained — design the 15 out of 10 experience (not 7 out of 10 constrained by time/resources), 2) Pick one location/instance, 3) Build the best possible lovable product in that one instance (best barista, best seats, best everything), 4) Understand what worked and what didn't, 5) Scale the pieces that actually worked. Example: Airbnb Lounge — build one perfect lounge instead of figuring out how to scale a mediocre lounge in 120 countries.

### 4 Elements of a Good Vision (Ebi Atawodi)
Criteria for evaluating if a product vision is effective and inspiring.

How it works: 1. Lofty (exciting/scary). 2. Realistic/Attainable. 3. Devoid of today's tech limitations (built in a vacuum). 4. Grounded in a clear, potent user problem.

### Big S Strategy Process (Chandra Janakiraman)
A 6-month, design-led process for creating an aspirational 5-10 year product vision.

How it works: Steps: 1. Analyze macro trends (cultural, social, tech). 2. Leadership interviews on long-term futures. 3. Cluster into 3 distinct futures. 4. Build prototypes ('concept cars'). 5. UXR testing to find winning components. 6. Live product testing.

### Column A vs. Column B Thinking (Melanie Perkins)
A mental model for strategic planning. Column A is looking at the resources (bricks) you have today and seeing what you can build. Column B is imagining the perfect, magical future you want to exist, and working backward to build it.

How it works: To use this: 1. Ignore current constraints. 2. Define 'wild success' in 10 years. 3. Define 'terrible failure' in 10 years. 4. Identify the microscopic first step to move toward the wild success.

### Concept Car Prototyping for Long-term Strategy (Strategy Blocks: An operator’s guide to product strategy)
An approach to generating exploratory prototypes for long-term strategy that are not intended to be production-ready but spark inspiration and identify winning components.

How it works: Analogy: Concept cars are never commercialized, but they spark inspiration and certain components/technologies make it into mainstream production. Similarly, Big 'S' prototypes are meant to help with discovery and deeper understanding. They are tested with carefully selected users to answer key learning goals. Through testing: sub-ideas are eliminated, some are modified, others are combined. Eventually, 'winning components' emerge that are likely to resonate with users even in the present-day context, accelerating the company toward the desired future.

### Curation to Recommendation to Generation (Gustav Söderström)
A mental model for understanding the three eras of the internet and how product interfaces must adapt to each.

How it works: 1. Curation (users digitize and organize content). 2. Recommendation (algorithms suggest content, requiring new UIs). 3. Generation (AI creates content, requiring entirely new product paradigms).

### Ideal Experience and Work Backward (Airbnb Approach) (This Week #4: Motivating engineers to hit deadlines, PM career ladders, and aligning with execs)
A product design approach where you remove all constraints to envision the ideal user experience, then work backward to implementation

How it works: Step 1: Remove the constrained cap — don't limit yourself by what's easy. Think about the 'ideal user experience' with truly no constraints.
Step 2: Put the cap back on — think about what it would actually take to achieve that ideal.
Step 3: Evaluate honestly — How much work would it really take? Is it actually that crazy?
Step 4: Pitch it — present the ideal to execs and see if they'd be on board with making it possible.
Key insight: 'Often we are limited not by what's possible, but what we think is possible.'
Reference: https://marker.medium.com/what-seven-years-at-airbnb-taught-me-about-building-a-company-e1d035d49c56

### North Star Scoping Variables (The PM 🤝 Design Partnership)
A four-variable framework for determining how much to invest in product vision/north star work with your design partner

How it works: Four variables to consider when scoping a north star exercise:
1. Scope — How much of the product experience to include? A single screen can be envisioned in a day, whereas a new product can take months.
2. Fidelity — How robust should the north star be? Consider the audience: words, journey map (like a comic strip), wireframes, or full-fledged prototype.
3. Time horizon — How far into the future are you planning? Near-term vision has more knowns and is quicker to create, but doesn't afford much runway.
4. Buy-in — Who do you need to involve to inform and evangelize the work? Feedback cycles take time but are critical when different parties need alignment.

Key principle: It comes down to how complex the problem is. Simple/minor/easily changeable = short whiteboarding exercise. Complex (new product or overhaul) = more formal vision work with ample time and fidelity.

### Pain Point, Solution, Proof Point Pitch Structure (Mihika Kapoor)
A structure for vision pitches that alternates between identifying a pain point, showing the solution, and providing a proof point.

How it works: Instead of traditional market sizing, alternate: Pain point -> Solution -> Proof point (like a user testimonial or prototype mock).

### Purpose Brainstorming Exercise (Arielle Jackson)
A structured method to discover a company's purpose by mapping cultural trends to product benefits.

How it works: 1. List cultural tensions/zeitgeist relevant to the business. 2. List ways to describe the brand's 'best self' (what it delivers when working perfectly). 3. Pick one from each list. 4. Finish the prompts: 'The world would be a better place if...' and 'We exist to...'.

### Rigid Mission, Flexible Feedback (Samurai Sword Model) (Sam Schillace)
Product builders must hold rigid conviction about their mission while remaining highly flexible about feedback and implementation details — like a samurai sword that's hard on the back but flexible on the edge.

How it works: Hard side (mission): Know where you're going. The world will push back. Don't waver on your core mission. Flexible side (feedback): You're probably wrong about many details. Listen to signals. Be open to surprises. You're always wrong about products — you have to put them in front of people. Key challenge: Distinguishing good pushback (real problems to solve) from bad pushback (fear-based resistance to disruption). No one can fully design products in their head.

### Roman Pichler's Product Vision Board (My favorite product management templates)
A structured canvas for articulating product vision including target group, needs, product, and business goals

How it works: A visual board template with sections for: Vision (the overarching goal), Target Group (who the product is for), Needs (what problems it solves), Product (what the product is and what makes it stand out), and Business Goals (how it benefits the company). Linked at: https://www.romanpichler.com/blog/tips-for-writing-compelling-product-vision/

### Stewart Butterfield's 2014 Slack Master Plan (Tamar Yehoshua)
Four-box strategic vision for Slack created in 2014 that guided all product development for years.

How it works: Four boxes: 1) Build a product people love. 2) Build a network (Slack Connect). 3) Build a platform that makes all your other SaaS products more valuable (Slack Platform). 4) Do some magic AI stuff. The master plan never changed — only what was worked on each year changed. Example: Slack Connect was always in the plan even though it shipped much later.

### The 10-Star Experience Framework (First-principles thinking)
Brian Chesky's thought experiment for designing extraordinary customer experiences by imagining progressively more extreme versions and then reverse-engineering the realistic but delightful version

How it works: Process: Take your key moment of truth (e.g. check-in at Airbnb) and imagine progressively more extreme versions:

5-star: Nothing bad happened (baseline)
6-star: Thoughtful personal touches (bottle of wine, fruit, handwritten note)
7-star: Curated, personalized experience (limo pickup, surfboard waiting because they know you surf)
8-star: Absurdly amazing (elephant parade in your honor)
9-star: Celebrity-level treatment (5,000 fans cheering, press conference)
10-star: Impossible/sci-fi (Elon Musk takes you to space)

Key insight: You probably can't deliver 8-10 stars, but most people only aim for 'good enough.' If you can design for 6-7 stars using handcrafted thinking, you can then reverse-engineer how to industrialize that experience millions of times. The result: people love your product and tell everyone about it.

### The Ladder of Needs (My favorite product management templates)
A technique for building a rockstar product vision by mapping customer needs in a hierarchy

How it works: A framework for articulating product vision by mapping customer needs from functional to emotional to aspirational in a ladder structure. Linked at: https://medium.com/@kit_ulrich/a-surprisingly-simple-technique-for-a-rockstar-product-vision-the-ladder-of-needs-ae624d81ca6b

### The Two-Step Plan (Melanie Perkins)
A macro business strategy that intertwines extreme commercial success with extreme philanthropy.

How it works: Step 1: Build one of the world's most valuable companies. Step 2: Do the most good we can do. Includes taking the '1% Pledge' (giving 1% of time, money, equity, and profitability) early on.

### Three-Year Painted Picture (How Miro builds product)
A high-level, bold, and ambitious vision document co-developed by the Leadership Team that defines outcomes for users and the company over three years

How it works: The painted picture is a three-year vision that: (1) Is co-developed by the Leadership Team (not top-down from one leader), (2) Defines desired outcomes for both users and the company, (3) Is intentionally bold and ambitious, (4) Establishes a general direction for the entire company. It feeds into the annual strategy, which is built collaboratively in three steps between November and January.

### Vision and Mission Framework (Ben Williams)
A structured approach to defining a team or company's vision and mission statements.

How it works: Vision: A 5-10 year nirvana state, bound to the target market, with strictly no mention of the company, product, or solution. Mission: How you will realize the vision, describing your fundamental approach and encoding your unique differentiating advantage.

### Vision to Values (Tomer Cohen)
A LinkedIn planning framework from Jeff Weiner that asks: if you're successful, what change would happen in the world?

How it works: For a company or product, answer: 'If you're successful, what change would happen in the world?' Used to ground product strategy in purpose and potential rather than incremental metrics.

### Working Backward from Ideal Experience (What Seven Years at Airbnb Taught Me About Building a Business)
A methodology combining Amazon's working-backward approach with Airbnb's Snow White storyboarding to envision the perfect user experience and then systematically build toward it.

How it works: Three key ingredients: 1) Write or draw out what the ideal experience looks like and feels like — before diving into short-term optimization, sketch out the ideal flow on paper and write a sample blog post describing what you'd announce if it became real. 2) Create a framework to make the problem tractable — break it down into manageable chunks. Example: Airbnb broke Instant Book gaps into 'can' problems (am I able to use it?) and 'want' problems (do I want to use it?), then worked through them in priority order. 3) When it feels uncomfortable, get more data — before giving up on a bold idea, validate assumptions through quick experiments, user research, or historical data. Airbnb's Snow White project: Inspired by Disney's storyboard technique, Airbnb's founders looked at the service as a story with a beginning, middle, and end, creating storyboards of ideal guest and host experiences to identify emotional moments, gaps, and opportunities.

## Templates

### Amazon PRFAQ (Press Release & FAQ) (Jason Shah)
A document written before building a product to clarify the vision, customer value, and rollout plan.

How it works: Structure includes: 1. Introduction (announce the product), 2. Problem (one paragraph, clear language), 3. Solution (briefly describe the product), 4. Customer Quote (put yourself in the shoes of a specific customer, avoid subjective words like 'great'), 5. Leadership Quote (how it fits the company strategy), 6. Call to Action (specific rollout plan and availability).

### Amazon's Working Backwards PR Template (My favorite product management templates)
Amazon's internal press release template for working backwards from the customer experience to define new products

How it works: A mock press release template written as if the product has already launched. Forces teams to think backwards from the customer benefit. Includes sections for headline, subheading, summary paragraph, problem statement, solution description, quote from company spokesperson, how to get started, customer quote, and closing call to action. Linked at: https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/working-backwards-press-release-template-example-ian-mcallister/

### App Store Vision Mockup (Ebi Atawodi)
A low-fidelity visualization exercise to define the core value propositions of a future product.

How it works: Print a screenshot of an app store page with 4 blank rounded rectangles. Have the team draw the 4 'hero' screenshots that solve the identified user problems.

### Founding Documents Suite (Claire Hughes Johnson)
The three core documents every scaling startup needs to align employees.

How it works: 1. Mission: A one-line statement of purpose (e.g., 'Increase the GDP of the internet'). 2. Long-term goals: 3-5 year objectives that explain how you will achieve the mission (e.g., 'Advance the state of the art in developer tools'). 3. Operating Principles: The behavioral values expected of the team.

### Future Headline Exercise (Ebi Atawodi)
Writing the future press release or news article to force clarity on the product's value proposition.

How it works: Write the headline you want to see in the future, add a subtitle, paste it into a mock TechCrunch or App Store page, and optionally write the full article.

### Google's Shared Team Vision Template (My favorite product management templates)
A presentation-based exercise for building a shared team vision

How it works: Google Slides presentation template for facilitating a shared team vision exercise. Linked at: https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/1cVWgMmIzDb-BXxgngQ-4IQyHSbkO7K79bc0RP69SC7M/edit

### Once Upon a Time Vision Story (Ebi Atawodi)
A Mad Libs-style storytelling framework to articulate a product vision simply.

How it works: Structure: 'Once upon a time [current state/problem]. And then one day [date/launch]. And because of that [benefit]. And finally, [the lasting feeling/dent in the universe].'

### One Year From Now Slide (Daniel Lereya)
A single presentation slide used at yearly kickoffs that describes exactly what the product and customer experience will look like in one year.

How it works: Written in present tense as if a year has passed (e.g., 'Our CRM continues with a very strong momentum and becomes a product suite...'). Used to start the following year's presentation to measure actual progress.

### PR/FAQ (Press Release / Frequently Asked Questions) (Bill Carr)
A document used to propose new products by working backwards from the customer experience.

How it works: Contains three 'money paragraphs': a short description of the product, a clear problem statement, and a solution statement. Includes a hypothetical launch date to signal complexity.

### Product Vision Template (Elevator Pitch) (Janna Bastow)
A fill-in-the-blank statement to define the product's core value proposition and differentiation, based on Geoffrey Moore's Crossing the Chasm.

How it works: Structure: For [target customer] who [statement of need/opportunity], the [product name] is a [product category] that [reason to buy]. Unlike [alternative], our product [statement of differentiation].

### Strategic Brainstorming Board (Nickey Skarstad)
A structured digital whiteboard setup for cross-functional vision planning.

How it works: Pre-fill a Miro/FigJam board with headers for discussion topics. Attach a competitive landscape pre-read to the invite. Use built-in timers and music to give people 10 minutes of silent ideation. Synthesize by bucketing similar ideas together live in the meeting.

### Three-Length Story Format (Petra Wille)
A structure for preparing a product narrative in different lengths for different contexts.

How it works: Prepare the story in three lengths: a 75-second elevator pitch, a 6-minute pre-planning version, and an 18-minute all-hands version (TED talk length).

### Airbnb Instant Book Case Study (What Seven Years at Airbnb Taught Me About Building a Business)
How Airbnb transformed their marketplace from 5% instant bookings to the vast majority by working backward from the ideal user experience.

How it works: Problem: Booking on Airbnb had many steps including a waiting period while the host reviewed the request. Only ~5% of bookings were instant. Approach: Instead of micro-optimizing individual funnel steps, the team stepped back to define the ideal booking experience — a guest being able to instantly book any home without waiting. Initially seemed impossible to convince every host. Process: 1) Sketched ideal booking flow on paper and wrote a sample blog post announcing the feature. 2) Identified the biggest gap: giving hosts more control over who could book instantly. 3) Broke gaps into 'can' problems and 'want' problems, worked through in priority order. 4) When internal resistance arose (people assumed instant booking would lower quality), did a data dive that showed otherwise. Result: Over a couple of years, transformed the marketplace so the vast majority of bookings became instant.

### Airbnb Snow White Storyboard Project (What Seven Years at Airbnb Taught Me About Building a Business)
How Airbnb used Disney-inspired storyboards to map the ideal guest and host experience, identifying key emotional moments and strategic gaps.

How it works: Inspired by Disney's Snow White film (one of the first to use storyboards), Airbnb's founders reframed the service not as a website but as a story with a beginning, middle, and end. They developed storyboards of the ideal guest and host experience, identifying key emotional moments along the journey. These storyboards became a key tool for identifying biggest gaps and opportunities, and informed early company strategy. References: TechCrunch article (https://techcrunch.com/2012/07/18/airbnb-brian-chesky-snow-white/), Fast Company article (https://www.fastcompany.com/3002813/how-snow-white-helped-airbnbs-mobile-mission), and team video (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nT7Irq8YuSo).

### Lyft Rider App North Star (The PM 🤝 Design Partnership)
Real example of how Lyft used a north star vision to guide the transformation of their rider app from rideshare-only to multi-modal transportation

How it works: Context: Lyft started with rideshare but over time added trains, buses, bikes, scooters, and rental cars. This was a big change requiring coherent design.
Approach: A multidisciplinary team of designers, engineers, and PMs created a north star for how the app should work with all transportation forms included.
Scope: The north star covered more than could be done short-term, but clarified next steps to head in the right long-term direction.
Timeline: High-fidelity prototype for the full rider app reimagination took ~6 months with bi-weekly exec reviews.
Contrast: A vision exercise for just the sign-up flow in sketch form took less than a week.
Outcome: Once the team had long-term clarity, they broke it into incremental steps easier to roll out and measure. Near-term design work was stronger because it accounted for both today's constraints and future plans.

### Maker Week Icon Swap (Mihika Kapoor)
A scrappy tactic to make a hackathon project feel real by changing a highly visible UI element with minimal code.

How it works: Instead of building a whole new entry point, swap an existing icon (like the file browser icon) to the new product's icon to communicate the vision and make it feel believable during a demo.

### WhatsApp 'Face-to-Face' Metaphor (Ami Vora)
An example of using an emotional metaphor to drive decentralized product design decisions.

How it works: WhatsApp used the metaphor of 'face-to-face communication' or 'sitting around a kitchen table'. This drove specific product decisions: typing indicators mimic taking a breath, joinable calls mimic walking in and out of a living room, and disappearing messages mimic casual banter where you don't record everything said.
