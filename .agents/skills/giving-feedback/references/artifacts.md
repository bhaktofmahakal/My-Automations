# Giving Effective Feedback - Frameworks, Templates & Checklists

*65 artifacts extracted from Lenny's Podcast and Newsletter*

## Frameworks

### 3-Step High Expectations Feedback Loop (Elizabeth Stone)
A method for upleveling team members when their work doesn't meet the bar.

How it works: Step 1: Set clear expectations ahead of time that the bar is high. Step 2: Give specific, direct feedback on where the gap is (preferably in private, not in front of a large group). Step 3: Jump into the work (e.g., the document) and actively help them fill the gap so they learn what excellence looks like for next time.

### Acknowledge Your Own Contributions (Introducing the GAIN framework for feedback: an evidence-based approach to giving feedback that people love, appreciate, and act on)
A feedback principle where the giver names their own role in the problem to preempt defensiveness and model vulnerability

How it works: When you as the feedback giver have contributed to a situation but aren't noting that, the recipient's attention latches onto this gap. 'Yeah, but what about YOUR actions?'

Preempt this by directly acknowledging your contribution. This is one of the most disarming feedback approaches and one of the least used.

Example from the handoff scenario: '...which I failed to flag at the time.'

Benefit: Once you acknowledge your contribution and show you're monitoring your own behavior, the other person can release their focus on your actions, freeing attention for their own.

Added benefit: Shifts you make in one relationship often apply to others. Example: Realizing you always said yes to rescheduling requests — addressing it across all clients dropped rescheduling to zero.

Principle: Humans unconsciously mirror behavior. If you want to lead a conversation in a direction, start moving there yourself. The message shifts from 'You need to change' to 'We can both shift some things.'

### Emotionless Decision Test (Molly Graham)
A mental tool for making hard personnel decisions by stripping away the human reaction.

How it works: Ask: 'If there were no emotions involved, and this person had no negative reaction, what would I do?' Once the right business decision is clear, figure out how to communicate it in the kindest way possible.

### Empathetic Accountability Feedback (Varun Parmar)
A feedback approach where observations are posed as questions rather than statements, combining accountability with empathy

How it works: Approach: When providing feedback, pose observations as questions rather than statements. Example framing: 'I believe I am seeing certain things happening. It could be just me that I'm not seeing the other things. Can you help me understand why certain things are happening? Because I might just be missing the perspective.' Practice: Dedicate 1-2 hours at every leadership offsite for open feedback to the leader. Leader shows vulnerability openly in front of 8-10 people. This builds trust and normalizes feedback culture. Key principle: If you do things with the lens of being empathetic, accountability becomes constructive rather than threatening.

### Exponential Feedback (Ravi Mehta)
A concept for giving feedback that has compounding returns by addressing root cause behaviors rather than surface-level symptoms

How it works: Surface feedback on symptoms = one-time fix, no compounding. Exponential feedback on underlying behaviors = person improves continuously and learns to self-diagnose. Method: Use PM competency framework to rate each competency (needs focus / on track / outperforming), have both manager and report do it independently in 5-10 min, then discuss areas of agreement and disagreement as conversation starters. Tips for soliciting: explicitly give manager permission to give real-time unpolished feedback; respond to all feedback with enthusiastic gratitude (per Jules Walter).

### Feedback Flash Tags (Lane Shackleton)
A categorization system for product feedback to calibrate how strongly the giver feels about their input.

How it works: Four tags based on a 'dying on a hill' metaphor: 1. FYI (no hill in sight), 2. Suggestion (there's a hill, won't die on it), 3. Recommendation (climbing the hill, don't ignore), 4. Plea (dying on this hill).

### Feedback Trust-Building Framework (How to make an impact in your first 90 days)
A two-way feedback approach for building trust rapidly in the first 30 days at a new role

How it works: Two components:

1. Receiving feedback:
- Actively ask for feedback to get input AND establish that you want to hear from others
- Diagnostic: If you're not getting substantive feedback, it's not because you're perfect—it's that others don't trust you yet to share it

2. Giving feedback:
- Be brave—someone has to start giving honest feedback
- When done thoughtfully, you will elevate your relationship
- Share feedback even in the first week of working together
- Establishes from the outset that you are: transparent, invested in their success, and want to win together

Key insight: In growth, everyone needs to be rowing in the same direction and get into formation ASAP. Some of the strongest rapports at Dropbox and Webflow were built through first-week feedback.

### GAIN Framework for Feedback (Introducing the GAIN framework for feedback: an evidence-based approach to giving feedback that people love, appreciate, and act on)
A four-part evidence-based framework for giving feedback that people are receptive to and act on. GAIN stands for Goal, Actions, Impacts, Next actions. The core philosophy is framing feedback around what someone stands to gain rather than the pain of what's wrong.

How it works: G - Goal: Name the topic and point to the possible benefits of making a change. Must be appealing to the feedback recipient. Not what you want them to DO differently, but the impact created by doing it. What will they gain from making a change?

A - Actions: Share specific, observable behaviors (not judgments). Describe what the person actually did or said. Include your own contributions to the situation.

I - Impacts: Describe the effects/consequences of those actions. Connect actions to outcomes that matter to the recipient. Simple cause-and-effect that the actor often lacks visibility into.

N - Next actions: Define concrete steps. Ask 'Who will do What by When?' (WWW). Can include actions for both parties. Schedule a check-in.

Core principle: Framing feedback based on what we want to move toward (the gain) instead of what we want to move away from (the pain) is profoundly more effective.

The order doesn't have to be G-A-I-N as long as the goal is clarified early and the spirit of GAIN is maintained.

### Golden Rule of Feedback (What 5 years at Reddit taught us about building for a highly opinionated user base)
A principle that asking for feedback and not acting on it burns more trust than never asking at all.

How it works: The golden rule: Ask for feedback only if you'll consider it.

Rationale: If you ask someone to give you their precious time and feedback and then don't act on it, you're actually burning more trust than if you had never asked at all.

The #1 trap companies fall into with advisory programs: treating them as a checkbox to placate the crowd rather than genuinely incorporating feedback into decision-making.

### Kind and Candid (Alexander Embiricos)
A leadership and communication motto for giving feedback effectively.

How it works: Frame candor as an act of kindness. Do not delay difficult conversations just to be 'nice'. Constantly re-evaluate if you were candid enough in the past to ensure you are actually helping your team grow.

### Left Hand Column (Claire Hughes Johnson)
A communication tool to safely share difficult feedback without causing defensiveness.

How it works: Take the harsh running commentary in your head (the left hand column) and detoxify it by: 1. Asking a question (e.g., 'Is there something we are not talking about?'). 2. Owning the observation as your own perception, not an absolute judgment.

### Nonviolent Communication (NVC) Framework (Rachel Lockett)
Marshall Rosenberg's four-step framework for having conflict conversations that create mutual understanding rather than defensiveness: Observations, Feelings, Needs, Requests.

How it works: Step 1 - Observations: State what happened factually (you could take a picture of it, no one would argue). Example: 'In the last three sprint planning meetings, you didn't invite me.' Step 2 - Feelings: Express emotions without blame. Use emotion words after 'I feel' (not 'I feel like...' or 'I feel that...' which lead to judgments). Example: 'I felt anxious not knowing the roadmap.' Step 3 - Needs: Name universal human needs that aren't being met (clarity, collaboration, connection). This isn't requiring anything, just helping them understand. Step 4 - Requests: Make a small, achievable olive branch request. The other person doesn't have to agree but should understand you. Example: 'Could you include me as optional or send the roadmap afterward?' Key principle: Stay on your side of the net—share your emotions and needs without crossing into blame.

### Observations vs. Judgments distinction (Introducing the GAIN framework for feedback: an evidence-based approach to giving feedback that people love, appreciate, and act on)
A core principle in GAIN feedback: share observations of actions and impacts, never judgments (critical or flattering)

How it works: Critical judgments trigger defensiveness. Flattering judgments destroy growth mindset.

Examples of translations:
- Judgment: 'You're being unprofessional' → Observation: 'Response times to client emails averaged 4.2 days last month'
- Judgment: 'You're a rockstar PM' → Observation: Describe specific actions and their impacts
- Judgment: 'They don't care about our time' → Observation: 'The last handoff meeting, you didn't share background customer research'

Why flattering judgments are harmful (Carol Dweck's research): 'If the cause of my success is my brilliance, what does that imply about the cause of my potential failures?' This is especially pernicious in contexts requiring experimentation where failure is inevitable.

Practice tool: Custom GPT prompt: 'Give me examples to practice identifying whether something is an observation or a judgment. Explain why I'm right or wrong after I answer, then give another example, making them increasingly ambiguous each time.'

### POV Thought Exercise for Managers (Autonomy vs. direction - Issue 35)
A thought exercise to clarify your point of view by removing emotional considerations first, then working backward to manage feelings

How it works: Ask yourself: 'If I take people's feelings out of the equation, what would I do?'

This question often reveals the right answer. Then it becomes a question of how to make people feel OK about the decision:
- Personally walk people through your thinking
- Give them a different project to work on
- Move people to new teams
- etc.

Benefits of always having a POV:
- Shifts from 'what should we do?' to 'how do I make it happen?'
- Gives you and your team a clear reference point
- Helps you know what questions to ask
- Helps you know when to challenge your team
- Helps you gauge how far you are from alignment

### Perception-Based Feedback Delivery (Anneka Gupta)
A communication framework for delivering hard feedback without triggering defensiveness.

How it works: Instead of saying 'You are X', say 'This is how you are being perceived.' This gives the person the benefit of the doubt that their intent doesn't match their impact, allowing for a collaborative discussion on how to change the perception.

### Pinches and Crunches (Carole Robin)
A mental model for addressing minor interpersonal irritations before they escalate into major conflicts.

How it works: A 'pinch' is a mild irritation. If unaddressed, it grows into a 'crunch'. To decide if a pinch is worth raising, substitute the word 'it' in 'it's not worth it' with 'I', 'you', or 'we' (e.g., 'We're not worth it'). If the relationship is worth it, raise the pinch.

### Radical Candor (Maya Prohovnik)
A feedback framework focusing on caring personally while challenging directly.

How it works: Requires two dimensions: Care Personally and Challenge Directly. Helps reframe underperformance as a role-fit issue rather than a personal failure, enabling supportive mentorship even during tough transitions.

### Radical Candor 2x2 Matrix (Kim Scott)
A compass to guide conversations based on two dimensions: Care Personally (vertical) and Challenge Directly (horizontal).

How it works: Four quadrants: Radical Candor (Care Personally + Challenge Directly), Obnoxious Aggression (Challenge Directly only), Ruinous Empathy (Care Personally only), Manipulative Insincerity (Neither).

### Radical Candor — 'It's not mean. It's clear.' (Inspiration for the year ahead)
The origin story and core principle of Radical Candor: moving up on the 'care personally' dimension while challenging directly. Clarity is kindness, not cruelty.

How it works: The origin story: Kim Scott was walking her dog in Manhattan and almost got hit by a cab. A stranger said: 'I can tell you love that dog' (moved up on the care-personally dimension). Then: 'You're going to kill that dog if you don't teach her to sit' (challenged directly). He pointed at the ground and said 'Sit.' The dog sat. He then said: 'It's not mean. It's clear.' The principle: Direct feedback that comes from a place of caring is not mean — it's clear. Separating clarity from cruelty is essential for effective communication and leadership.

### Situation-Behavior-Impact (SBI) (Paige Costello)
A structured format for delivering clear, non-confrontational feedback.

How it works: 1. Situation: Specific time and place (e.g., 'On Tuesday in the 3:00 meeting'). 2. Behavior: Observable action ('You interrupted me'). 3. Impact: Subjective feeling or result ('It made me feel like my voice wasn't valued').

### Start/Continue/Stop Feedback Framework (The Power of Performance Reviews: Use This System to Become a Better Manager 🤝)
A three-question peer feedback framework originally from HBR, used to gather actionable input on a person's performance

How it works: Three questions to ask each peer:
1. What are 2-3 things [person] should START doing? Why?
2. What are 2-3 things [person] should CONTINUE doing? Why?
3. What are 2-3 things [person] should STOP doing? Why?

Designed to take 5-10 minutes. Responses kept anonymous unless the peer opts in.

### T3 B3 Feedback Framework (Keith Yandell)
A structured feedback mechanism used during reviews or 1:1s to ensure balanced, mandatory feedback.

How it works: Requires the employee to provide exactly three positive things (Top 3 / T3) and three constructive/bad things (Bottom 3 / B3) about their manager. The mandatory nature removes the fear of giving constructive feedback.

### The 'Fascinating' Curiosity Pause (Ami Vora)
A mental model for handling visceral disagreements by sublimating ego and defaulting to curiosity.

How it works: When someone says something you think is crazy wrong: 1. Notice your visceral, lizard-brain reaction. 2. Take a deliberate pause to calm your body. 3. Respond with, 'Fascinating, you have to tell me more why you think that.' 4. Assume they have information you lack and focus on the shared outcome rather than being right.

### The Three Realities (Carole Robin)
A model for understanding communication breakdowns and giving feedback by separating what you know from what you are assuming.

How it works: Reality 1: My intent and background (Known only to me). Reality 2: My behavior, verbal and nonverbal (Shared). Reality 3: The impact on you and your background (Known only to you). Rule: 'Stay on your side of the net' by only speaking about Realities 1 and 2.

### Three Questions for Joint Problem-Solving (Healing your co-founder relationship)
A framework for generating solutions to co-founder or interpersonal conflicts by simultaneously answering three questions.

How it works: When generating a solution to an interpersonal conflict, answer these three questions simultaneously:

1. 'What do I need?'
2. 'What do you/others need?'
3. 'What is best for the organization?'

Additional guidance:
- The resolution should include an explicit understanding that agreed-upon changes are a TRIAL PERIOD
- Build in a mechanism to assess whether the impact of changes is working for both parties
- Discuss upfront how to handle regressions should old patterns recur
- Use a superordinate goal (e.g., 'what's best for the organization') to move from 'you are the problem' to 'how can we resolve this together'

### Three Realities Model (Stay on Your Side of the Net) (Healing your co-founder relationship)
A framework for having difficult conversations without triggering defensiveness. In any interaction between two people, there are three realities: Person A's intent/motives (known only to A), the observable behavior (known to both), and the impact on Person B (known only to B). The 'net' sits between intent and behavior — you should only speak to what's on your side of the net.

How it works: The Three Realities:
- Reality #1: Person A's intent and motives (known ONLY to Person A)
- Reality #2: Person A's observable behavior (known to BOTH people — the shared reality)
- Reality #3: The impact on Person B (known ONLY to Person B)

The 'Net' sits between Reality #1 and Reality #2.

Rules:
1. Stay on YOUR side of the net — speak only to what you know (their observable behavior + its impact on you)
2. Never cross the net — don't attribute motives, impute intent, or make character judgments
3. Be curious about their side — ask questions about what's driving their behavior

Good example (staying on your side):
'This is the third time this month you have decided you want to do something different than what we agreed to after discussing all the pros and cons multiple times [observable behavior] and I am feeling more and more frustrated [impact on me]. And if this is becoming a problem for me, I'm also concerned this might be discouraging for others, which is another reason I think it is important that we talk about it.'

Bad example (crossing the net):
'You always have to have the last word' [attributing motive]
'I feel that you don't know what you want' [judgment disguised as a feeling — no actual feeling word]

Follow-up curiosity prompt:
'What is happening here that leads you to want to come back to revisit decisions we've already made? I'm wondering what we're seeing differently that leads me to think we're done with an issue and you don't think so?'

## Templates

### AAA (Matt Abrahams)
A three-step framework for delivering a proper apology.

How it works: 1. Acknowledge: State the specific behavior or transgression you committed. 2. Appreciate: Express understanding of the difficulty or negative impact you caused the other person. 3. Amends: Offer a specific, actionable step you will take to fix it or prevent it from happening again.

### AI Voice Mode role-play prompt for practicing feedback (Introducing the GAIN framework for feedback: an evidence-based approach to giving feedback that people love, appreciate, and act on)
A detailed prompt template for using AI voice mode to practice feedback conversations before having them

How it works: Prompt template:

'You are going to play the role of [person's name or role], and I will practice giving them feedback.

Here is the context: [Describe the situation, your relationship, and what you want to address]

Here is what I plan to say: [Paste your GAIN-framed feedback]

Please respond as [person] would — realistically, not ideally. Push back where they likely would. Be somewhat defensive at first but open to hearing me out if I handle it well.

After each exchange, briefly coach me: What did I do well? What could I improve? Then continue the role-play.

Let's begin. I'll start.'

Alternative: Flip roles — ask AI to take your role while you play the feedback recipient. This models possibilities and helps you access the recipient's perspective.

Recommendation: 3-4 practice reps in 15 minutes builds significant confidence.

### Constructive Feedback Script (Alisa Cohn)
A script for delivering performance feedback neutrally based on observable facts.

How it works: Structure: 1. 'I want to have a conversation with you about some things I've been hearing/observing...' 2. State the observable facts. 3. 'We both know that the most important way you can be successful is...' 4. 'I want to hear what you have to say, but the most important thing is we leave knowing how you're going to make sure [behavior changes].'

### Defensiveness Pause Script (Alisa Cohn)
A script to pause a conversation when an employee gets defensive or emotional.

How it works: Structure: 1. 'Let's pause for a second.' 2. 'I want you to know I'm telling you this to make you better/help your career.' 3. 'My observation is that you're getting a little bit emotional/heated.' 4. 'I want to know if we can continue or if we need to pause it and come back to it.'

### Difficult Conversation Script (EOY Review)
A step-by-step conversational structure for delivering bad news without triggering a defensive amygdala response.

How it works: Step 1 (Warn): 'This is going to be a difficult conversation. I want you to take a few seconds and prepare yourself.' Step 2 (Deliver): Share the news clearly and directly. Step 3 (Process): 'My guess is you're feeling a lot of anger/fear/sadness right now. Is that true? Would you be willing to share what you're feeling?' Step 4 (Listen): Actively listen to make them feel heard.

### Good Inside Feedback Script (Dr. Becky Kennedy)
A framework for addressing chronic bad behavior by separating identity from actions.

How it works: Structure: 1. Establish alignment ('I want to say we're on the same team'). 2. Affirm their identity/competence ('I know you're a good person. You probably don't need me to tell you that we need to start meetings on time'). 3. State the behavioral observation ('It's also been happening consistently'). 4. Get curious ('Tell me what's been going on... let's get to the bottom of it together').

### Interpersonal Feedback Formula (Carole Robin)
A fill-in-the-blank sentence structure for delivering feedback while staying on your side of the net.

How it works: Structure: 'When you do [insert specific behavior], I feel [insert feeling word from vocabulary], and I'm telling you this because / I'm hoping the outcome is [insert desired outcome/problem-solving goal].' Rule: Never follow 'I feel' with 'that' or 'like'.

### One Thing Feedback Question (Dr. Becky Kennedy)
A specific question to solicit upward feedback and build immediate goodwill.

How it works: Ask: 'If I could do one thing different this week to be a better [parent/manager] to you, what would it be?' Then, receive the feedback without defensiveness, even if you cannot accommodate the specific request.

### Peer Feedback Request Email (The Power of Performance Reviews: Use This System to Become a Better Manager 🤝)
A copy-paste email template for requesting peer feedback using the Start/Continue/Stop framework from 5-8 peers

How it works: Subject: Peer feedback request

Hi Joe,

In an effort to help Jane level up in her career, I'm gathering peer feedback from people she works most closely with. I would really love your input. If you can find 5-10 minutes in the next few days to answer these questions, I would truly appreciate it (and so will Jane):

1. What are 2-3 things Jane should start doing? Why?
2. What are 2-3 things Jane should continue doing? Why?
3. What are 2-3 things Jane should stop doing? Why?

I will keep your answers anonymous, unless you tell me otherwise. Please be honest and candid, as that's what'll help me give Jane the best support in her career. And, if there's anything else you'd like to share, good or bad, I'd love to hear it.

Thank you!

Note: BCC all 5-8 recipients on the same email. Send at least a month before the scheduled review.

### Promotion Denial Script (Alisa Cohn)
A script for telling someone they aren't getting promoted while maintaining their morale.

How it works: Structure: 1. Don't bury the lead: 'I know this is going to be challenging to hear... we are going to look for an external candidate.' 2. Explain the 'why' based on business needs. 3. Provide hope: 'I want you to know it's important to me that you succeed here, and I want to continue to help you find opportunities to build your skills.'

### Script for Overruling Your Team Gracefully (Autonomy vs. direction - Issue 35)
A specific verbal script managers can use when they need to override their team's preferred direction

How it works: Script: 'Normally, I want you all to feel great about the direction, but I feel strongly about this path, so can we just try it and see how it goes? If I'm wrong, I'm wrong, and I'll take full responsibility.'

Preconditions for this to work:
- Team members had a chance to share their perspective
- Team members genuinely feel heard
- They agree with your decisions most of the time

Key insight: Do not assume that team members will be upset if their suggestion is not followed. They'll often prefer someone just making a call and moving on.

### The 'Go-To' Feedback Question (Kim Scott)
A prompt to ask at the end of 1:1s to solicit constructive criticism.

How it works: Base template: 'What could I do or stop doing that would make it easier to work with me?' Alternatives: 'Tell me why I'm wrong', 'What's one way I could help you be more successful?', or 'What could I have done this week to better support you in your work?'

### The 4 Is (Matt Abrahams)
A comprehensive structure for delivering constructive feedback.

How it works: 1. Information: Set the playing field (what happened). 2. Impact: Explain how the behavior affected you or the team. 3. Invitation: Ask them to collaborate on a solution. 4. Implications: State the positive or negative consequences of changing the behavior.

### The Repair Script (Dr. Becky Kennedy)
A script for apologizing and reestablishing trust after a mistake or outburst.

How it works: Structure: 1. Acknowledge the specific behavior ('Hey, earlier in the meeting, I totally cut you off'). 2. Validate their experience ('I used a really harsh tone'). 3. Take ownership without excuses ('Honestly, I did disagree, but it's no excuse for me to talk to you that way'). 4. State your commitment to change ('I'm sorry. I'm going to work on that').

## Checklists

### 4-Step Feedback Solicitation Process (Kim Scott)
A step-by-step method for managers to pull critical feedback from their team.

How it works: 1. Ask an authentic 'go-to' question. 2. Embrace discomfort (close mouth and count to 6). 3. Listen to understand (ask follow-up questions, don't defend). 4. Reward the candor (fix the problem visibly or offer a respectful explanation of disagreement).

### Goal-setting phrases for GAIN feedback (Introducing the GAIN framework for feedback: an evidence-based approach to giving feedback that people love, appreciate, and act on)
A collection of opening phrases and approaches for framing the Goal component of GAIN feedback

How it works: Approach 1 - Reference a positive instance: If you can find even one instance of someone already using the desired behavior, reference it. They'll recognize what it looks and feels like and feel more confident they can do it again. The impact of that behavior serves as the Goal.

Approach 2 - 'Even more' framing: Use 'even more' to convey they have foundations to build on AND they're not there yet. Example: 'I'd love to see you do even more of X because when you do, Y happens.'

Approach 3 - High expectations + support (from Stanford study): 'I'm giving you these comments because I have very high expectations [challenge] and I know that you can reach them [support].' This sentence quadrupled revisions compared to a placebo and revisions were 26% better.

Approach 4 - Dialogue, not monologue: Pause routinely and ask questions that elicit reflections. 'What's your take?' 'How does that land?' 'What am I missing?'

Approach 5 - Listen before you talk: If you're not clear on why they would care or what their goal is, listen first.

### HIP CORE Feedback Checklist (Kim Scott)
Criteria and structure for delivering effective praise and criticism.

How it works: HIP: Humble, Helpful, Immediate, In-person (synchronous), Praise in public, Criticize in private, not about Personality. CORE: Context (where it happened), Observation (what you saw/heard), Result (the impact), nExt step (what to do about it).

### Next Actions techniques for closing feedback conversations (Introducing the GAIN framework for feedback: an evidence-based approach to giving feedback that people love, appreciate, and act on)
Five specific techniques for turning feedback into concrete commitments

How it works: 1. Ask for their ideas first: Before offering your own suggestions, ask what they think should change. People resist change that costs them autonomy. They often come up with useful options you hadn't considered and are more motivated to pursue their own ideas.

2. Co-create through brainstorming together: 'I have some ideas, and I'd love to hear yours too. Want to brainstorm together?' Lets you build on each other's ideas.

3. Make specific requests — and make it easy to say no: Share your idea as a suggestion, not a demand. 'Feel free to say no' doubled yes rates across 52 studies (meta-analysis). Ensure requests state what you want MORE of, not just less of. As Marshall Rosenberg said, 'You can't do a don't.'

4. Frame it as an experiment: Instead of committing to something new 'forever,' acknowledge it as an experiment for a set time period. 'Would you be open to trying X for two weeks and then we can check in on how it's going?'

5. WWW — Who will do What by When?: The question that moves growth, progress, and whole companies forward faster than any other. Watch out for 'Let's' (who?) and 'Should' (commitment or idea?). Use these words as automatic signals to pause and clarify.

6. Schedule a check-in: Set a specific date/time. Without it, you risk frustration festering into resentment. With it, you get: increased accountability, a built-in moment to appreciate progress, and faster course-correction if needed.

### Tactics for Extracting Honest Feedback (Jules Walter)
Specific techniques to make it safe for others to give you constructive criticism.

How it works: 1. Ask about a specific behavior (e.g., 'Did I show executive presence?'). 2. Provide self-critical feedback first to give them an opening to agree. 3. Respond with enthusiastic gratitude, regardless of how it feels internally.

## Examples

### 'But you are free' meta-analysis (Introducing the GAIN framework for feedback: an evidence-based approach to giving feedback that people love, appreciate, and act on)
Research showing that explicitly supporting someone's autonomy to decline doubles compliance

How it works: A meta-analysis of 52 studies showed that adding 'Feel free to say no' DOUBLED the percentage of time that people said YES to a request.

### Script for Raising an Issue with a Co-Founder (Healing your co-founder relationship)
A concrete example of how to raise a recurring behavioral issue with a co-founder using the Three Realities / Stay on Your Side of the Net framework

How it works: Scenario: Your co-founder keeps revisiting and reversing decisions you thought were finalized.

Good approach (stays on your side of the net):
'This is the third time this month you have decided you want to do something different than what we agreed to after discussing all the pros and cons multiple times [observable behavior — known to both]. I am feeling more and more frustrated [impact on you — your reality]. If this is becoming a problem for me, I'm also concerned this might be discouraging for others, which is another reason I think it is important that we talk about it [shared concern / superordinate goal].'

Follow up with curiosity:
'What is happening here that leads you to want to come back to revisit decisions we've already made? I'm wondering what we're seeing differently that leads me to think we're done with an issue and you don't think so?'

Bad approach (crosses the net):
- 'You always have to have the last word' [attributing motive — you don't know their intent]
- 'I feel that you don't know what you want' [judgment disguised as feeling — contains no actual feeling word]

Why the good approach works: It describes only observable behavior and personal impact, doesn't attribute intent, and uses genuine curiosity to invite the other person's perspective — lowering defensiveness and building toward joint problem-solving.

### Stanford 'high expectations' feedback study (Introducing the GAIN framework for feedback: an evidence-based approach to giving feedback that people love, appreciate, and act on)
A randomized controlled study showing that combining challenge with support in feedback quadruples effort

How it works: Randomized, double-blind controlled study tested the impact of one sentence added to feedback:

Treatment: 'I'm giving you these comments because I have very high expectations [challenge] and I know that you can reach them [support].'

Placebo: 'I'm giving you these comments so that you'll have feedback on your paper.'

Results: The treatment sentence more than QUADRUPLED the number of revisions students made, and the revisions themselves were on average 26% better.

Key insight: Challenge and support are usually seen as opposites ('I'm either extremely honest or extremely caring'), but this is a misconception. Integrating both is dramatically more effective.

### Tversky framing study: Approach vs. Avoid (Introducing the GAIN framework for feedback: an evidence-based approach to giving feedback that people love, appreciate, and act on)
Research showing that gain-framed information leads to dramatically better decision-making than loss-framed information

How it works: Study: Doctors given identical data with different framing.

- Approach framing: 'The survival rate is 90%' → Made the most effective decision 84% of the time

The meaning is identical, but the avoid framing and its threat mindset distort our ability to think and process information clearly — precisely the ability we want people engaging when receiving feedback.
