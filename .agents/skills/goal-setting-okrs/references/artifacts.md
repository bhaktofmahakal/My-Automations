# Goal Setting and OKRs - Frameworks, Templates & Checklists

*43 artifacts extracted from Lenny's Podcast and Newsletter*

## Frameworks

### Anti-OKR Philosophy for Product Quality (How Shopify builds product)
Shopify's case against formal OKRs and when metrics-driven goals work vs. don't

How it works: Arguments against formal OKRs: 1. Teams optimizing for individual metrics create micro-optimizations of local maxima. 2. The product doesn't feel good anymore—parts push in different directions. 3. You can't explain why the product feels weird until you discover two teams had conflicting metrics. 4. Systems and structures that prevent truth-seeking are bad in themselves. When metrics DO work: - When there's a one-to-one relationship between the metric and merchant value (e.g., checkout conversion rate = merchant revenue). - Performance metrics (speed and quality of the product). When to skip metrics: - Product redesigns done because 'it'll look better and we think it's the right thing to do.' - Success criteria: 'We come out the other side, look at it, and think: That's rad. I'm psyched. Merchants are psyched.' Key insight: You can afford to not be metrics-obsessed if you have a successful business and strong founder-led culture of quality.

### Crazy Big Goals (Melanie Perkins)
A goal-setting philosophy that requires setting targets so large they make the team feel inadequate, forcing them to work incredibly hard to will the goal into existence.

How it works: Structure: 1. Set the Crazy Big Goal (e.g., Empower the world to design). 2. Break into Mission Pillars (e.g., every ingredient, every language, every device). 3. Set successive annual goals for each pillar. 4. Plan a massive, unique celebration for when the goal is hit (e.g., smashing plates).

### Duolingo Quarterly OKR Process (3-Step, 3-Week) (How Duolingo builds product)
A bottom-up OKR planning process that takes three weeks and involves teams, areas, and senior leadership

How it works: Step 1 — 2 weeks before end of quarter: Teams grade current quarter OKRs and draft next quarter OKRs. Team leads lead the process, collaborating with area leads and relevant leaders. Work with team members to write draft OKRs. Collect feedback and iterate over one week to finalize team OKRs. Step 2 — 1 week before end of quarter: Areas grade current quarter OKRs and draft next quarter OKRs. Area leads use team OKRs as main input to draft area OKRs. Collect and incorporate feedback from senior leadership over one week, iterating from first draft to final draft. Step 3 — First week of new quarter: Companywide OKR reviews with senior leadership. Area leads present final version of OKRs to senior leadership. All OKR reviews completed in the first week. Feedback and changes incorporated ASAP. Final OKRs published company-wide by end of first week. Note: In practice, team and area OKRs happen in parallel — areas start their first draft when reviewing first version of team OKRs. The only strict rule is that companywide reviews always happen in the first week of the quarter.

### Five Ingredients of Wildly Ambitious Goals (What Seven Years at Airbnb Taught Me About Building a Business)
A framework for setting and executing on stretch goals that push teams to think bigger without demoralizing them.

How it works: Five ingredients: 1) Set uncomfortable goals — pick a goal that makes you uncomfortable while understanding why hitting it would be incredible. Ask: 'What would need to be true for us to hit this goal?' and 'What could we accomplish without barriers (budget, people, dependencies)?' 2) Make sure someone is directly accountable — if a number doesn't have a person's name next to it, it's not going to happen. 3) Think long-term — look ahead 5-10 years to determine this year's goal, in terms of both growth and mission. 4) Give a cross-functional team ownership of how — your #1 job as a leader is to assemble the right team, point them in the right direction, and stay vigilant unblocking them. 5) Celebrate success, don't punish failure — if you don't hit the goal but get close, congratulate the team and move on.

### Four Working Backwards Paths (Jonathan Lowenhar)
The four ultimate goals a startup is working towards at any given time, used to anchor planning.

How it works: 1. An exit, 2. A next fundraise, 3. Profitability, 4. Winding down. Founders must choose one and quantify what has to be true to unlock it.

### Frontier of Understanding / NCT Goal-Setting (Ravi Mehta)
A framework for setting the right type of goal based on four types of risk, rather than always defaulting to outcome-based goals

How it works: Four risk types that determine what goal to set: 1) Understanding Risk - don't know what levers move the metric → set learning/research goals, 2) Dependency Risk - know the levers but lack tools/resources → set dependency-resolution goals, 3) Execution Risk - have resources and hypotheses but may not execute well → set execution goals (e.g., 'run 20 experiments'), 4) Strategic Risk - have hypothesis and execution but hypothesis may be wrong → set outcome goals. Also includes a 2x2: Did we hit goals? × Do we know why? Best quadrant = hit goals AND know why.

### Goal-Setting Altitude Shift Framework (The definitive guide to mastering analytical thinking interviews)
Method for translating product-level metrics into specific team-level goals for 3-6 months

How it works: Steps:

1. **Pick one ecosystem player** who can drive the most growth given your established framework
   - Connect choice back to NSM and product mission
   - Explain why this player represents the highest-leverage opportunity RIGHT NOW

2. **Map the user journey** for that player, working backward from the key actions that contribute to NSM

3. **Identify friction points or opportunities** along that journey (aim for 3)

4. **Score each potential goal** on two dimensions:
   - Impact on North Star metric (low/medium/high)
   - Ability to influence within 3-6 months (low/medium/high)
   - Consider: cross-team dependencies, political capital required

5. **Make a clear decision** — pick highest-impact, highest-influence opportunity
   - No hedging between multiple options
   - Articulate why this is the strongest candidate for your team's goal
   - Show clear throughline: company vision → product mission → NSM → team goal

Tip: Spend 2 minutes organizing thoughts before walking interviewer through goals.

### Headlines (rebranded as Commitments) (How Figma builds product)
An alternative to traditional OKRs where teams define 'headlines' — claims they want to make by the end of a time period — evaluated through a combination of quantitative and qualitative signals.

How it works: Instead of traditional OKRs (objectives + key results with a single metric), teams write a headline claim, e.g., 'Figma is the most efficient way to design.' Teams then offer both quantitative and qualitative ways to evaluate that claim. Benefits: (1) Surfaces philosophical alignment, (2) Produces the right debates, (3) Allows creative measurement approaches, (4) Acknowledges that some experiences are hard to reduce to a single metric, (5) Gives room to explore different KPIs. Later evolved into 'commitments' to emphasize team ownership and responsibility for achieving goals (not just technical measurement). Implemented as FigJam widgets.

### Imperatives (Jeremy Henrickson)
A prioritization tool to align independent teams around company-wide goals.

How it works: A forced-ranked list of roughly 10 top-level priorities (e.g., globalization, platform improvements) that every product and engineering team must integrate into their individual quarterly roadmaps.

### Linear's Approach to Goals (Anti-OKR Framework) (How Linear builds product)
An alternative to OKRs using strategic theme goals and a single North Star metric.

How it works: What they do:
- Set strategic theme goals: e.g., 'Be the default tool for startups' or 'Get xxx number of companies'
- Use these themes to drive roadmap planning
- Maintain one North Star company-level metric goal
- Pull up specific data/metrics on certain projects for additional insights (but not for decision-making)

What they don't do:
- No OKRs
- No per-project metrics goals
- No A/B tests
- No specific engagement numbers to track

Rationale: For B2B tools with long adoption cycles, specific metrics goals can lead you astray. Product goals should be about how to add value for existing customers or expand to a new customer base — solving customer problems better.

Validation approach: Beta test → see feedback → iterate → gain conviction the feature is as good as it can be → release to see how it works at scale.

### Metric Breakdown Frameworks for Goal Setting (This Week #10: Keeping designers and engineers excited about metrics + Transitioning from DS to PM 🕺)
Three referenced frameworks for breaking down high-level metrics into contributing factors during quarterly planning

How it works: 1. Growth Loops (Reforge) — Model growth as self-reinforcing loops rather than funnels to identify levers
2. HEART Framework (Google) — Measures Happiness, Engagement, Adoption, Retention, Task success for UX metrics
3. North Star Framework (Amplitude) — Identifies a single North Star metric and its contributing input metrics

Use these to help teams break down their mission metric into actionable contributing factors during quarterly planning.

### Mission → Vision → Strategy → Goals → Roadmap Hierarchy (Setting goals, Setting goals - Issue 23)
A hierarchical framework showing how goals fit into the bigger picture of company direction, from abstract purpose down to concrete execution plans

How it works: The hierarchy flows as follows:
1. Mission: What are you trying to achieve (e.g., Tesla: 'Accelerate the world's transition to sustainable energy')
2. Vision: What does the world look like when you achieve it (e.g., Tesla: 'Create the most compelling car company of the 21st century by driving the world's transition to electric vehicles')
3. Strategy: How you plan to get there (e.g., Tesla: Build a sports car → Use that money to build an affordable car → Use that money to build an even more affordable car → Also provide zero-emission electric power generation options)
4. Goals: How you tell whether you're successfully executing your strategy — measurable targets tied to key metrics
5. Roadmap: The specific initiatives and timeline to achieve those goals

### Mountain Peak / Basecamp Goal Setting (Tomer Cohen)
A visualization framework for setting ambitious product goals: see the peak clearly, know basecamp to start from, accept the middle is blurry

How it works: Start from what the product COULD be (potential), not from current numbers. Visualize: Peak (the ambitious end state, described specifically), Basecamp (where you start today), Middle (blurry, you'll figure it out). Share the peak to inspire. Start from potential market size and member needs, not existing metrics.

### OKR Ladder Framework (How Miro builds product)
A three-level OKR system designed to avoid duplication, reduce process overhead, and drive focus

How it works: Structure: Company OKRs (most encompassing) → AMPED OKRs → Stream OKRs. Teams decide which OKRs they support rather than creating their own. Cadence: Semi-annual OKR setting (not quarterly). Quarterly check-ins for course correction (e.g., if a KR metric is no longer relevant or targets need adjustment based on rolling forecasts). Monthly AMPED-wide traction meeting to discuss most important topics and address challenges. Limits: 3-4 objectives per level, 2-3 KRs per objective. Stream leaders present to CPO and CTO for review and sign-off. Three problems this solved: (1) Overlapping initiatives from OKRs at every level - removed extra layers, (2) Too much time on process from quarterly planning - moved to semi-annual, (3) Too many priorities with 3-5 objectives and 3-4 KRs each - limited the numbers.

### Perplexity's OKR Approach (How Perplexity builds product)
A quarterly planning framework with aggressive measurable objectives where 70% completion is expected and the remaining 30% gap is used diagnostically

How it works: Structure: 1) Plan quarterly — don't commit beyond that in a fast-moving AI environment. 2) All objectives must be measurable: either quantifiable thresholds OR Boolean (completed/not completed). 3) Set very aggressive objectives. 4) Expect ~70% completion rate at end of quarter. 5) Use the remaining ~30% gap diagnostically to identify underinvestments in prioritization and staffing. Example: If infrastructural goals aren't met, it reveals underinvestment in hiring infra engineers. 6) Within quarters, keep the product roadmap stable but allow small tasks to shift as priorities change. 7) Run product roadmap in parallel with a technical/model development roadmap.

### Six-Month OKRs with Monthly Traction (Varun Parmar)
An evolved OKR cadence that sets targets every six months but reviews traction monthly, reducing planning overhead while maintaining accountability

How it works: Structure: Company-level OKRs → AMPED org OKRs → Stream OKRs. Cadence: KR targets set every 6 months (evolved from quarterly). Traction reviewed every 4 weeks at AMPED level. Rationale: Quarterly replanning was too much overhead and didn't give teams enough execution time. Six months provides better balance. Tool: KRs tracked in Coda.

### The 'How Do We Know?' Metric Generator (Christina Wodtke)
A brainstorming method for translating qualitative objectives into measurable key results.

How it works: 1) State the qualitative objective. 2) Ask 'How do we know we succeeded?'. 3) Spend 10 full minutes brainstorming every possible way to measure it to get past the obvious answers and find the most insightful metrics.

### The OKR Spectrum Problem (Yuhki Yamashata)
Diagnostic framework for why OKRs fail: teams either anchor on metrics that matter but can't be moved, or metrics that can be moved but don't matter

How it works: Two failure modes: 1) Secondary metrics you can measure and move, but haven't proven correlate with anything that matters (e.g., satisfaction scores not tied to retention). 2) Business metrics that matter but you can't prove you moved because there are too many hops between your work and the outcome (e.g., rider experience PM trying to claim incremental trips). Figma's evolution: OKRs → deprecated to 'headlines' → OKRs with better data → 'commitments' → still iterating.

### Theoretical Limits Framework (Nilan Peiris)
A mental model for setting product goals based on theoretical maximums or minimums rather than incremental improvements.

How it works: Ask: What is the theoretical minimum cost? What is the theoretical maximum speed? Set the goal there and take years to build it, rather than making 10% incremental improvements.

### Three Benefits of Goals (Setting goals)
The three primary reasons goals matter for teams

How it works: 1. Clarity: You know what success looks like
2. Alignment: All team members know what success looks like
3. Motivation: A push to achieve more than you would have otherwise

### Three Criteria for Good OKRs (Yuhki Yamashata)
A framework for evaluating whether goals/OKRs are actually useful, based on three tests: legibility, actionability, and authenticity

How it works: 1) Legibility: People look at it and immediately understand what it means—not an obfuscated metric that means nothing to anyone. 2) Actionability: The OKR inspires action—you look at it and feel compelled to do something differently. 3) Authenticity: It honestly depicts what the team is actually trying to do day-to-day—if an engineer stopped in the hallway can't recite it, it fails this test.

### Three Steps to Impact-First Product Teams (Matt LeMay)
A three-step approach to ensure product work drives actual business value.

How it works: 1. Set team goals no more than one step away from company goals. 2. Keep impact first at every step of the product process (strategy, epics, OKRs). 3. Connect every bit of work back to impact (measure prioritization impact in the same unit as the goal).

### Toddler Soccer Goal Alignment (Ami Vora)
A metaphor and framework for preventing teams from cannibalizing each other's efforts by detangling top-line metrics.

How it works: Problem: Giving every team the same top-line goal (e.g., GMV) results in 'toddler soccer' where everyone chases the same ball. Solution: Detangle the customer journey. Assign different, specific input metrics (visits, conversion, reorders) to different teams so they have distinct swim lanes that all ladder up to the top-line goal.

### Two-Step Goal Definition Process (Pick Metric + Pick Threshold) (Setting goals)
A structured method for defining a goal by first selecting the right metric and then calibrating the right target

How it works: Step 1 — Pick the Metric:
  a. Start with your business's north star metric (e.g., revenue, subscriptions, media consumed)
  b. Map out all the levers that move that metric (your growth model — all levers should add up to 100% of what drives the business)
  c. Identify which lever is the largest constraint or bottleneck — ask: 'What's the one thing that, if moved, would unlock the most growth?'
  d. Determine the best concrete metric to track that lever
  Example: Airbnb's north star was 'nights booked.' Levers: demand, supply, dynamics. Sub-levers for demand: site visits, conversion, cancellation rate. Identified supply as the constraint → metric: 'New listings that received at least one booking.'

Step 2 — Pick the Threshold:
  a. Tops-down: In order for the business to achieve ideal long-term growth, how much does this metric need to move?
  b. Bottoms-up: What impact could the team realistically deliver with current (or slightly increased) resources?
  c. The number usually ends up somewhere in the middle, adjusted for actual resources
  d. Calibration principle (Brian Chesky): Goals should feel 'uncomfortable, but not impossible' — like a video game level that's just hard enough to motivate but not so hard you give up

Fallback: If you can't find a perfect metric, describe as concretely as possible what success looks like (e.g., 'launch product by X date, with Y resources, with no more than Z bugs')

### V2MOM (Vision, Values, Methods, Obstacles, Measures) (My favorite product management templates)
Salesforce's strategic alignment framework created by Marc Benioff for creating company-wide alignment

How it works: V2MOM stands for Vision, Values, Methods, Obstacles, and Measures. Used to create alignment within a company by having every employee write their own V2MOM that cascades from the CEO's. Linked at: https://www.salesforce.com/blog/2013/04/how-to-create-alignment-within-your-company.html

### Video Game Difficulty Calibration for Goal Ambition (Setting goals)
A mental model for calibrating how ambitious a goal should be, using video game design as an analogy

How it works: Video games are designed so that the next level is just hard enough to motivate you to continue, but not so hard that you give up. Victory always seems just within reach. When it feels impossible, you quickly give up. Apply the same principle to goal-setting: your goal should motivate your team to push beyond what's comfortable, but never make it feel impossible. Brian Chesky's formulation: goals should feel 'uncomfortable, but not impossible.'

### Vision, Mission, Strategy, Objectives Pyramid (Nickey Skarstad)
A top-down framework to connect long-term existential goals to short-term execution.

How it works: Top: Vision (Where are we going in 10 years?). Level 2: Mission (How do we make the vision come to life?). Level 3: Strategy (What needs to happen to execute the mission?). Bottom: Objectives/OKRs (What are the specific notes we need to hit in the next 3-6 months?).

## Checklists

### Duolingo OKR Best Practices (How Duolingo builds product)
A set of best practices for writing effective OKRs as used at Duolingo

How it works: Best practices for writing OKRs at Duolingo (from their internal guide): 1. OKRs should be simple, easy to understand, and clear. 2. Objectives should be qualitative and inspirational. 3. Key Results should be quantitative and measurable. 4. Each Objective should have 2-4 Key Results. 5. OKRs should be ambitious but achievable — stretch goals, not sandbagging. 6. OKRs should align with team's area OKRs and company OKRs. 7. OKRs should reflect what the team plans to actively work on that quarter. 8. Keep the time invested in OKR planning to a reasonable minimum — time planning is time taken away from executing. ### Five Attributes of a Good Goal (Setting goals)
Criteria to evaluate whether a goal is well-constructed before committing to it

How it works: A good goal has the following attributes:
1. Simple: Everyone understands what it is and how it's measured
2. Concrete: It's obvious if you've achieved it
3. Worthwhile: Making an impact actually matters to the business
4. Stable: You're confident your work won't be drowned out by noise
5. Quick feedback loop: Changes in the product can quickly be seen in the metric

### Five Criteria for Well-Set Goals (What Seven Years at Airbnb Taught Me About Building a Business)
A checklist for evaluating whether team goals are properly set.

How it works: Goals should: 1) Be limited in number — ideally just one or two. 2) Have quick feedback loops that allow you to see impact immediately. 3) Be directly connected to top-line business growth. 4) Be easily understood. 5) Be uncomfortable (stretch goals).

### Five Steps to Operationalize Goals (Setting goals)
A step-by-step process for making goals actionable after they've been defined

How it works: 1. Get buy-in: Make sure everyone on your team (and folks above) buy into the goal
2. Instrument: Implement a tool that allows your team to easily watch this metric. Build a dashboard that's accessible and easy to find. Triple-check the data is valid.
3. Prioritize: Decide what to prioritize based on what will have the most impact on your goal. With alignment on what success looks like, previously hard decisions become much easier.
4. Operationalize: Build this metric into your team workflows. Find a cadence of checking in on this metric individually with your team and with management. Share progress on the goal frequently.
5. Revisit: As you continue to monitor your metric (and how your work impacts it), give yourself an opportunity to revisit the goal. Consider whether it still meets the broader attributes and if your team actually has the ability to move it.

### Goal Subtraction Exercise (Matt LeMay)
A workshop exercise to simplify team goals and eliminate bloated OKR boards.

How it works: 1. Everyone writes down the most important goal on a post-it note. 2. Pair up and combine notes, making the goal smaller and more concise. 3. Repeat the pairing and shrinking process until the team arrives at 1-2 highly specific, concise goals (e.g., reducing a massive Miro board to a single target like '100 million pounds in profit').

### Peer Goal Approval Process (Christina Wodtke)
A fast alternative to top-down management approval for quarterly goals.

How it works: 1) Write goals. 2) Send to 3 peer teams that work closely with you. 3) Require a 24-hour turnaround for feedback on feasibility. 4) Finalize (avoids heavy executive review bottlenecks).

### Six Rules for Goal Setting (Molly Graham)
A checklist for creating effective company goals that actually drive clarity and prioritization.

How it works: 1. No more than 3 company goals. 2. One goal must win in a fight (strict prioritization). 3. Explain it like I'm 5 (an intern should understand it). 4. Strategy should hurt (make painful trade-offs/non-goals). 5. One goal has one owner (a specific name attached). 6. Build a process to follow up and learn from the goals.

### Three benefits of setting goals (Setting goals - Issue 23)
A quick checklist of the three primary benefits that well-set goals provide to a team.

How it works: 1. **Clarity** — You know what success looks like
2. **Alignment** — All team members know what success looks like
3. **Motivation** — A push to achieve more than you would have otherwise

Use this as a litmus test: if your goals aren't providing all three of these benefits, they may need to be reworked.

## Examples

### Async OKR Review Process at Slack (Tamar Yehoshua)
How Slack moved from synchronous OKR reviews consuming 300+ person-hours to an async process using Slack Clips (video) and channels.

How it works: Problem: OKR reviews with all teams consumed 300+ person-hours per quarter. Solution: Each team creates a Slack channel for their OKRs, posts a planning doc and a time-limited Slack Clip (video) covering major points. CPO and CTO watch all videos together, then post follow-up questions async in the channel. Only escalate to live meetings for high-priority projects or many open questions (typically 5-10 teams). Launched shortly after Slack Clips became available.

### Duolingo Area-Level OKR Example (Revenue) (How Duolingo builds product)
A real example structure of what a revenue-based product area OKR looks like at Duolingo

How it works: Example structure (numbers are made up): Objective: [Qualitative, inspirational objective related to revenue growth]. Key Result 1: Increase IAP revenue from $X to $Y. Key Result 2: Increase subscription revenue from $X to $Y. Key Result 3: Increase ads revenue from $X to $Y. Area OKR rolls up from team-level OKRs (e.g., In-App Purchases team, Subscriptions team, Ads team each have their own OKRs that feed into the Monetization area OKR).

### Duolingo Yearly Company OKR Example (DAU Growth) (How Duolingo builds product)
Screenshot-based example of what Duolingo's annual company OKRs look like for DAU growth

How it works: Structure of yearly company OKR for 2022 DAU growth: Led by Strategy and Biz Ops team. Process takes 1-2 months starting in October for next calendar year. Each key result has a designated 'key result facilitator' — a senior leader responsible for: (1) Finalizing the OKR, (2) Tracking progress throughout the year, (3) Helping solve roadblocks. Example: Manu (Head of Marketing) and Cem (VP Product) are key result facilitators for User Growth OKRs. Yearly OKRs define biggest strategic bets and highest-priority investments and guide quarterly team OKRs.

### MailChimp First Email Goal (Matt LeMay)
A case study of a team taking on a scary, high-impact commercial goal instead of staying in their safe feature domain.

How it works: Instead of just building new platform features, the VP of Product set a goal to improve the rate of users successfully sending their first email. A PM took ownership, mapped where users got stuck, and achieved the goal by subtracting steps and streamlining the experience rather than adding new features.

### Online Interior Design Magazine Goals (Christina Wodtke)
A concrete example of moving from strategy to objective to key results for a digital publication.

How it works: Strategy: Connect humans with brands. Objective: Collect profiles of people's passions. Key Results: 1) 30% of audience becomes members, 2) Users 'like' 3 items per week, 3) Positive qualitative feedback from bi-weekly customer panels.
